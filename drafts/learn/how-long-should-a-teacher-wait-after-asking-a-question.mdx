If you ask a class to explain its reasoning, try counting silently to three before you rephrase the question or call on someone. Once a student answers, give them a moment to add to it. Mary Budd Rowe studied both pauses in elementary science classes. When teachers extended the pauses to an average of about three to five seconds, students gave longer answers. Treat three seconds as something to try. A student who needs to sketch a diagram may need longer <Cite k="rowe1972classroompauses" />.

## Which pause are you timing?

The first starts when you finish the question. If you spend the next few seconds offering hints, those seconds can feel like a long wait from the front of the room. A student has heard you speaking for much of it.

Researchers analyzed the timing of 945 questions in 22 German eighth-grade mathematics lessons. From the teacher's question to the first student's response, the average was 4.2 seconds. From the teacher's *last words* to that same response, it was 2.5 seconds. The **1.7-second** difference comes from where the researchers started the clock. Teachers sometimes added information after asking the question. The researchers excluded intervals longer than 20 seconds. They also left out individual and group work, so these averages tell us little about how long to give students to sketch <Cite k="heinze2006questiontiming" />.

The second pause comes when the student has already said something. Maybe you hear “Three quarters,” draw breath to say “Correct,” and realize you asked how they knew. Leave the student the floor for another moment. In Kenneth Tobin's mathematics study, teachers in ten classes received coaching and feedback aimed at an average wait of three to five seconds. Compared with ten classes whose teachers received neutral feedback, teachers in those classes interrupted students less often <Cite k="tobin1986classroompauses" />.

## Try it with a question about fractions

Put 3/4 and 5/8 on the board. Tell the class, “Everyone gets a moment to sketch first. Then I'll ask someone to show their thinking.” Ask, “Which fraction is larger, and how could your drawing show it?” Give them time to draw before you invite an answer.

If the first student says “Three quarters,” hold off on confirming it. They might go on to say, “I drew two bars the same length. Three quarters fills six eighths of the first bar. That is one more eighth than the five I shaded on the second.” If the explanation doesn't come, ask the student to point to the part of their sketch that helped them decide.

If a colleague can watch the exchange, ask them to jot down your last words before the answer, the student's first words, and who spoke next. Teaching alone? After class, make a note of who explained the drawing. Did the student show why the fractions differed, or did you supply the explanation?

## Frequently asked questions

### What if everyone is waiting for me to call on someone?

Say who gets the next turn. In a study of 17 mathematics lessons with pupils aged 12 to 14, Ingram and Elliott showed how the meaning of a silence depended on who could speak next. If students usually wait to hear a name, “Take a moment, then raise your hand if you'd like to explain” tells them what to do when they're ready <Cite k="ingram2014classroomturns" />.

### What if I wait and nobody answers?

Give students a specific place to start. For the fractions question, try “Show me where five eighths ends on your bar.” In 17 hours of recordings from three university English classes, researchers watched one teacher work through silences after a question. Sometimes she offered a hint or repeated a narrower part of what she'd asked. Those recordings give examples of how a teacher can reopen a question when waiting alone hasn't drawn a response <Cite k="gunes2025classroompauses" />.
