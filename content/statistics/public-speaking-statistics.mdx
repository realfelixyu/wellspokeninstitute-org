Public speaking is a routine part of most jobs, and fear of it is common but far from universal. National survey data put a lifetime fear of public speaking at about 1 in 5 U.S. adults, not the 75% figure that circulates online <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears" />. The research also shows that speaking skill and speaking confidence respond to training. Below are 20 statistics we could trace to a primary source, grouped by the question they answer. Each one links to the study or official dataset it came from.

## How many jobs depend on speaking?

- Speaking is rated "important" or higher in 89.1% of the 910 occupations in the O*NET® 31.0 Database, according to the Wellspoken Institute's analysis of the U.S. Department of Labor ratings <Cite k="onet31" />.
- Weighted by May 2025 employment, about 122.3 million U.S. workers, or 85.5% of the employment we could match, are in occupations where speaking is rated important or higher <Cite k="onet31,blsoews2025" />.
- About 37.8 million workers (26.4%) hold jobs where speaking is rated "very important" or higher <Cite k="onet31,blsoews2025" />.
- Speaking is rated more important than writing in 77.8% of occupations and more important than mathematics in 93.8% <Cite k="onet31" />.

## How common is the fear of public speaking?

- In the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, a nationally representative U.S. survey fielded in 2001 to 2003, 21.2% of adults reported a lifetime fear of public speaking, the most common of the 14 social fears measured <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears" />.
- In a random telephone survey of 499 adults in Winnipeg, Canada, one third said they felt excessive anxiety when speaking to a large audience <Cite k="stein1996publicspeaking" />.
- In that same survey, 10% said public-speaking anxiety had markedly interfered with their work, social life, or education, or caused them marked distress <Cite k="stein1996publicspeaking" />.
- In the 2026 Chapman University Survey of American Fears, 28.9% of Americans said they were afraid or very afraid of public speaking, which ranked 51st among the fears tested <Cite k="chapmanfears2026" />.
- Among 1,135 undergraduates in Brazil, 63.9% reported fear of public speaking and 89.3% said they wanted their degree program to include classes on it <Cite k="ferreiramarinho2017fear" />.

## Does public speaking training work?

- A meta-analysis of 30 randomized controlled trials with 1,355 participants found an average effect of g = 0.74 for psychological interventions aimed at fear of public speaking <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />.
- The same meta-analysis found that Internet-delivered and other technology-assisted treatments worked as well as face-to-face treatment <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />.
- In a study of 908 college students, both public speaking courses and broader introductory communication courses reduced public speaking anxiety and raised self-rated communication competence by similar amounts <Cite k="broeckelmanpost2017hybrid" />.
- In a study of 144 first-year undergraduates, feedback from a teacher did more to improve presentation behavior than peer feedback or self-assessment <Cite k="vanginkel2015feedback" />.

## How fast do people speak?

- In a large corpus of recorded U.S. telephone conversations, speakers averaged 196 words per minute, and individual conversations ranged from 111 to 291 words per minute <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />.
- Across 77 studies with 5,965 participants, adults reading English aloud averaged 183 words per minute <Cite k="brysbaert2019reading" />.
- Most writers on radio recommend a news-reading pace of 160 to 180 words per minute, and in a comparison of 40 European news bulletins only the BBC stayed inside that range <Cite k="rodero2012speechrate" />.

## How do listeners react to how you sound?

- When researchers edited a recorded talk so its ums were either deleted or replaced with silence, listeners rated the version with no pauses best and rated filled pauses above silent ones <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />.
- In everyday conversations recorded across 5 studies, um and uh appeared at similar rates for men and women and for younger and older speakers <Cite k="laserna2014filler" />.
- Silent video clips of teachers shorter than 30 seconds were enough for strangers to make ratings that predicted end-of-semester student evaluations <Cite k="ambady1993thinslices" />.
- In videotaped job interviews with 110 managers, a combined measure of vocal cues such as pitch variability, speech rate, and pauses correlated with interviewers' judgments at r = .20 <Cite k="degroot1999vocal" />.

## How we chose these statistics

We included a number only if we could read it in the original study, a government dataset, or the survey publisher's own report. We left out figures that appear on statistics roundup sites without a traceable source, including the claim that 75% of people fear public speaking. The occupation figures are the Wellspoken Institute's own analysis of O*NET 31.0 ratings and Bureau of Labor Statistics employment estimates; the full method and data are on our [Speaking by Occupation](/data/speaking-by-occupation) page. Several studies here used student or single-city samples, so treat them as evidence about those groups rather than about everyone.

## Frequently asked questions

### What percentage of people are afraid of public speaking?

The best national estimate for U.S. adults is 21.2% with a lifetime fear of public speaking, from the National Comorbidity Survey Replication <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears" />. Surveys that ask about milder nervousness, or that sample students, report higher numbers.

### Is it true that 75% of people fear public speaking?

We could not find any survey or study that reports that figure. The national data we found range from about 21% for a lifetime fear to about 29% for being afraid or very afraid <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears,chapmanfears2026" />.

### Can public speaking skills be improved?

Yes. Randomized trials show that fear of public speaking drops with treatment, and course studies show gains in both confidence and self-rated competence <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops,broeckelmanpost2017hybrid" />.
