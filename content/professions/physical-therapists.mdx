Physical therapy is hands-on, but much of what makes it work is said out loud. O*NET® rates speaking importance for physical therapists at 4 out of 5, and the occupation ranks 195th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Therapists explain a diagnosis, coach movement in real time, persuade patients to keep doing exercises at home, and talk people through pain that may not match what an X-ray shows. The relationship built through that talk appears to affect outcomes directly.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for physical therapists include several conversations a patient would recognize. Therapists "instruct patient and family in treatment procedures to be continued at home," "confer with the patient, medical practitioners, or appropriate others to plan, implement, or assess the intervention program," and "provide information to the patient about the proposed intervention, its material risks and expected benefits, and any reasonable alternatives" before obtaining informed consent <Cite k="onet31" />. They also direct and communicate with support staff, give educational information on injury prevention and ergonomics, and may direct group rehabilitation sessions.

The importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.63 out of 5, and face-to-face discussion is rated 4.78 for frequency. Telephone use is rated 4.48. Formal public speaking is rated 2.16, with 2.72% of incumbents reporting it daily, likely those who lead classes or teach students.

What sets physical therapy apart is how long the relationship lasts. A patient may see the same therapist twice a week for two months, which gives the therapist many conversations to build trust or lose it. Each session also includes live verbal coaching: counting reps, cueing posture, and adjusting effort as the patient moves.

## What the research says

**The alliance is associated with better outcomes, though the evidence is limited.** A 2010 systematic review in Physical Therapy looked at 13 prospective studies of patients in physical rehabilitation, including people with brain injury, musculoskeletal conditions, and cardiac conditions. The working alliance, usually measured in the third or fourth session, was positively associated with treatment adherence, depressive symptoms, treatment satisfaction, and physical function in some patient groups. The studies were too varied to pool, and the authors called for more research on the strength of the effect <Cite k="hall2010alliance" />.

**An enhanced alliance reduced pain in an experiment.** Researchers randomized 117 people with chronic low back pain to receive active or sham interferential current therapy combined with either an enhanced or a limited therapeutic alliance. Mean pain reduction was largest with active treatment plus an enhanced alliance (3.13) and smaller with active treatment plus a limited alliance (1.83). Even the sham treatment delivered with an enhanced alliance showed a larger mean reduction (2.22) than the real treatment delivered with a limited one. The study measured a single session in a laboratory setting <Cite k="fuentes2014alliance" />.

**Certain communication styles build the alliance.** A systematic review of 12 studies examined which communication factors were linked to therapeutic alliance across health settings. Interaction styles that helped clinicians engage more with patients, by "listening to what they have to say, asking questions and showing sensitivity to their emotional concerns," showed large positive correlations with alliance. Evidence on specific verbal and nonverbal behaviors was scarce and inconclusive <Cite k="pinto2012communication" />.

**Explaining pain science can reduce pain and disability.** A systematic review of 8 studies with 401 participants found compelling evidence that neuroscience education, which teaches patients how the nervous system produces pain, may reduce pain ratings, improve function, and reduce catastrophizing in chronic musculoskeletal pain <Cite k="louw2011neuroscience" />. That education is delivered mostly through spoken explanation, so how a therapist explains pain becomes part of the treatment.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Asking and listening early.** The communication review points to open questions and attention to emotional concerns as the behaviors most tied to alliance. In the first session, ask what the patient most wants to get back to doing, and refer to it later.

**2. Explaining pain in plain language.** If a patient assumes that more pain means more damage, offer a short, concrete explanation of pain as the nervous system's protective response, using an everyday metaphor. Keep it to a few sentences and invite questions.

**3. Home program instructions that survive the drive home.** The instruct-at-home task depends on clear spoken instruction. Demonstrate, have the patient perform it, then ask them to describe the exercise back to you in their own words.

**4. Real-time cueing.** During exercise, keep cues to a few words ("push the floor away") and save explanation for rest breaks, when the patient can listen.

**5. Informed consent as a conversation.** The O*NET task requires explaining risks, benefits, and alternatives. Present them briefly, then ask what concerns the patient has before asking for agreement.

## How to practice

- **Script your pain explanation.** Write a 60-second explanation of why persistent pain does not always mean damage. Run it through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and break up any long or technical sentences.
- **Time it.** Use the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to keep your core explanation short enough to deliver without losing the patient.
- **Collect your best cues.** Make a list of one-line cues for your most common exercises and practice saying them in rhythm with a movement.
- **Review a session recording.** With consent from a colleague in a role-play, record a mock initial evaluation and count your open questions and responses to emotion. Run the transcript through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter) to see where you hesitate.
- **Practice the pause after a question.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you wait long enough for a patient to answer an open question fully.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the therapist-patient relationship affect physical therapy outcomes?

Studies link a stronger alliance to better adherence, satisfaction, and function in some patient groups, and one experiment found an enhanced alliance reduced pain more than the same treatment delivered with a limited alliance. The evidence base is still small.

### What is pain neuroscience education?

It is teaching patients how the nervous system produces pain. A systematic review found it may reduce pain, disability, and catastrophizing in chronic musculoskeletal pain.

### How much do physical therapists speak in public?

Not often. O*NET rates public speaking at 2.16 out of 5 for the occupation, while face-to-face discussion is rated 4.78.
