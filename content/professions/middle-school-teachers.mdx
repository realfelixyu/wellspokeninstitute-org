Middle school teachers spend most of the day speaking, and the research says their biggest speaking challenge is getting students to talk back. In O\*NET® data, speaking is rated 4.12 out of 5 for importance and oral expression 4.25, placing the job 105th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking frequency is rated 4.13 out of 5, and 70.45% of surveyed teachers report public speaking every day. Discussion-based teaching helps students in these grades, yet it is uncommon, so the skill gap is in running discussions as much as in presenting.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Middle school teachers usually teach one or two subjects to several groups of students a day. The O\*NET task list asks them to "instruct through lectures, discussions, and demonstrations in one or more subjects, such as English, mathematics, or social studies." They communicate the objectives of each lesson, enforce rules for behavior, tutor students who need extra help, and guide and counsel students with adjustment or academic problems. They also meet or correspond with parents, coordinate clubs and academic contests, and supervise games and recreational activities <Cite k="onet31" />.

Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.80 out of 5 for frequency, about every day, and telephone use 3.39. One contrast with elementary teaching stands out: dealing with the public is rated 2.83 out of 5 for importance, well below the elementary figure. Middle school speaking is concentrated inside the classroom, aimed at a roomful of students rather than at families.

## What the research says

**Discussion is rare, and a structured program can change that.** Lawrence, Crosson, Paré-Blagoev, and Snow tested Word Generation, a program that gives teachers across content areas texts and activities built around discussable dilemmas. The study involved 1,554 middle grade students in 28 schools randomly assigned to the program or to control conditions. The authors note that classroom discussion is "exceedingly rare in U.S. schools." The program had large effects on discussion quality in every subject, especially math and science, and a small but significant effect on the vocabulary it taught. It did not change scores on a standardized test of general vocabulary. Discussion quality accounted for 14% of the program's effect on vocabulary <Cite k="lawrence2015wordgen" />.

**Discussion-based English classes were linked to stronger literacy.** Applebee, Langer, Nystrand, and Gamoran studied 64 middle and high school English classrooms. After controlling for fall performance and background, students in classrooms that emphasized discussion-based approaches, combined with high academic demands, performed better in the spring. The pattern held for low-achieving and high-achieving students, though the authors caution that discussion was unevenly distributed across tracks <Cite k="applebee2003discussion" />.

**Argument can be taught as a way of talking.** Osborne, Erduran, and Simon worked with 12 science teachers in London junior high schools. Teachers taught at least nine lessons built around scientific or socioscientific argument, and the researchers analyzed recorded student group talk using Toulmin's model of claims, data, and warrants. The quality of students' argumentation improved, although each teacher's use of argument followed a personal pattern <Cite k="osborne2004argumentation" />.

**Teachers rarely leave time to think.** Mary Budd Rowe's review summarized research showing that teachers typically wait less than one second after asking a question before speaking again, and react within one second after a student stops talking. She distinguished the pause after a question from the pause after an answer, and reviewed training procedures that help teachers lengthen both <Cite k="rowe1986waittime" />.

The studies differ in design. Word Generation is a randomized trial, while the English classroom study is correlational. They agree that well-structured talk among students supports learning in these grades, and that it does not happen without deliberate planning.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Posing a question worth arguing about.** Word Generation worked by giving students dilemmas with more than one defensible answer. A good discussion prompt is the teacher's most important spoken line of the lesson.
- **Teaching the shape of an argument.** Students who learn to state a claim, back it with evidence, and explain the link can disagree without the conversation falling apart.
- **Waiting.** Longer pauses after a question and after an answer give more students time to form a reply.
- **Managing turns across a whole class.** Calling on a range of students, and asking one student to respond to another, spreads the talk beyond the few who volunteer.
- **Short, clear explanations.** Mini-lectures still matter. Stating the lesson objective and the key idea in a few plain sentences frees time for discussion.

## How to practice

1. **Write one dilemma per unit.** For your next unit, draft a question that reasonable students could answer two ways, and test it on a colleague before using it in class.
2. **Post sentence starters.** Display frames such as "I claim that...", "My evidence is...", and "I disagree with ___ because..." and model each one aloud before students use them.
3. **Time your pauses.** Count silently to at least three after each question. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you rehearse holding a silence without filling it.
4. **Tally who talks.** Keep a seating chart on a clipboard and mark each student contribution for one class period. Look for students who never appear.
5. **Size your mini-lecture.** Write the explanation you plan to give and check how long it will take with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). If it runs past 10 minutes, cut it and move the rest into discussion.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for middle school teachers?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.12 out of 5 for this job, and it ranks 105th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score.

### Why is classroom discussion so uncommon?

The studies here document how rare it is rather than explain why. Discussion takes planning, time, and classroom routines, and the Word Generation trial suggests that ready-made discussable materials help teachers make room for it.

### Does discussion help students who are behind?

In the study of 64 English classrooms, discussion-based approaches were linked to better performance for low-achieving as well as high-achieving students, although low-track classes received less of this kind of instruction.
