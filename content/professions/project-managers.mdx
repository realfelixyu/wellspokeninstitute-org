Project management looks like a planning job on paper, but most of a project manager's day is spent talking. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 3.5 out of 5 for project management specialists, and the occupation ranks 449 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score (62.7 of 100) <Cite k="onet31" />. That middle ranking understates the conversational load. The same data rates communicating with supervisors, peers, or subordinates at 4.73 out of 5, and oral comprehension (understanding what others say) at 4.12. Project managers are paid to keep many people pointed at the same deadline, and they do it mostly with their voice.

There were 1,066,670 project management specialists employed in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $102,320 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list reads like a schedule of conversations <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Communicate with key stakeholders to determine project requirements and objectives."
- "Confer with project personnel to identify and resolve problems."
- "Create project status presentations for delivery to customers or project personnel."
- "Negotiate with project stakeholders or suppliers to obtain resources or materials."
- "Monitor the performance of project team members to provide performance feedback."
- "Schedule or facilitate project meetings."

The work context ratings add detail. Telephone conversations average 4.36 and face-to-face discussions 4.24 on a 1 to 5 frequency scale, so both happen most days. Public speaking, in the sense of one person presenting to an audience, averages 2.64. That average hides a sizable group of presenters: 21.21% of respondents said they speak in front of an audience weekly (but not daily), and another 6.06% said every day. Those are the status presentations and kickoff meetings that come with the role. Dealing with external customers or the public rates 3.25 out of 5 for importance, reflecting the many project managers who serve as the client's main contact.

## What the research says

**Project managers' listening and speaking skills predict team productivity.** Henderson surveyed 564 respondents recruited through the Chief Project Officer website. She measured project managers' competence at "decoding" (receiving and understanding messages) and "encoding" (sending clear messages). Both competencies significantly contributed to team members' satisfaction and productivity. Geographic dispersion of the team also played a significant and unexpected role in how these competencies affected outcomes <Cite k="henderson2008pm" />. The study relied on survey reports, so it shows association rather than proof of cause, but it is one of the few to measure project managers' communication directly.

**Communication is part of teamwork quality, and teamwork quality tracks performance.** Hoegl and Gemuenden defined teamwork quality with six facets, with communication listed first, alongside coordination, balance of member contributions, mutual support, effort, and cohesion. Using data from 575 team members, team leaders, and managers of 145 German software teams, they found teamwork quality was significantly associated with team performance as judged by members, leaders, and outside managers, although the strength of the link depended on who was rating <Cite k="hoegl2001teamwork" />.

**Rich communication shows up among the strongest success factors.** Andersen and colleagues collected data on actual project work in the United Kingdom, France, Norway, and China. The factors most important for delivering on time and on budget were strong project commitment, early stakeholder influence, stakeholder endorsement of project plans, and rich project communications. For achieving the project's intended impact, commitment and rich communications were the main contributors. The authors concluded that project managers should put more energy into communication within the project and toward its wider environment <Cite k="andersen2006projectsuccess" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Listening to stakeholders, then playing it back.** Henderson's "decoding" finding matters most at the start, when stakeholders explain what they want. Summarizing their goal in your own words, and asking them to correct you, catches misunderstandings before they become change requests.

**2. Status presentations that lead with the decision.** With 21.21% of project managers presenting weekly and 6.06% daily, status updates are a core performance. The strongest ones open with the one thing the audience must decide or know, then show the evidence.

**3. Facilitating meetings.** Running a meeting is a speaking skill: stating the purpose in one sentence, inviting quieter members in by name, and closing with owners and dates said out loud.

**4. Negotiating for resources.** Asking a functional manager for people or budget requires a clear case, a specific number, and a willingness to propose tradeoffs rather than repeating the request.

**5. Delivering feedback.** Performance feedback to team members who do not report to you depends on specific, behavior-based language and a calm tone.

## How to practice

- **Three-minute status drill.** Write a weekly update that opens with the decision or risk, then paste it into the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). If it runs over three minutes, cut the history and keep the ask.
- **Paraphrase practice.** In your next requirements meeting, after each stakeholder speaks, summarize what you heard in one sentence and ask, "Did I get that right?" Track how often they correct you.
- **Kickoff rehearsal.** Rehearse a project kickoff using the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) at a steady pace, then deliver it again from bullet points only.
- **Meeting openings.** Practice a 30-second opening that states the meeting's purpose, the decision needed, and the time limit. Record it and check for filler words with the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter).
- **Bad-news rehearsal.** Script how you would tell a sponsor the project will slip two weeks. Use the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) to slow down at the key sentence instead of rushing past it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often do project managers give presentations?

In O*NET survey data, 21.21% of project management specialists report speaking to an audience weekly but not daily, and another 6.06% report doing so every day. The face-to-face discussion item asks a separate question. Just over half of respondents (51.52%) selected “every day.”

### Do communication skills matter more on remote or distributed projects?

The evidence suggests they work differently. Henderson found that geographic dispersion significantly changed how project managers' communication competencies related to team satisfaction and productivity, which makes clear, deliberate communication more important when the team is spread out.

### How does project management compare with other business roles on speaking demand?

It ranks 449 of 910 occupations. [Management analysts](/professions/management-analysts) rank 169 and general and operations managers rank 188, partly because their roles involve more presenting to and persuading people outside the immediate team.
