Marketing managers spend much of their working day persuading colleagues, not customers. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4 out of 5 in importance for marketing managers, and the occupation ranks 143 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score with a score of 70.6 <Cite k="onet31" />. A marketing plan depends on sales teams, product developers, buyers, agencies, and distributors acting on it. Getting those groups to understand and trust what marketing has learned about customers is a spoken job, done in meetings, calls, and presentations.

There were 395,240 marketing managers in the United States in May 2025, and their median annual wage was $166,790 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Many O*NET task statements for marketing managers involve coordinating with other people <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Formulate, direct, or coordinate marketing activities or policies to promote products or services, working with advertising or promotion managers."
- "Consult with product development personnel on product specifications, such as design, color, or packaging."
- "Consult with buying personnel to gain advice regarding the types of products or services expected to be in demand."
- "Negotiate contracts with vendors or distributors to manage product distribution, establishing distribution networks or developing distribution strategies."
- "Coordinate or participate in promotional activities or trade shows, working with developers, advertisers, or production managers, to market products or services."
- "Direct the hiring, training, or performance evaluations of marketing or sales staff and oversee their daily activities."

Telephone conversations are close to daily, averaging 4.9 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale, and face-to-face discussions average 4.38. Public speaking averages 2.69, with 16.07% of respondents presenting weekly and 0.49% daily. One detail stands out: communicating with people outside the organization rates 4.4 out of 5 for importance, slightly higher than communicating with people inside it (4.12). Agencies, distributors, retail buyers, and partners take up a large share of a marketing manager's conversations.

## What the research says

Marketing research has studied the "interfaces" between marketing and other departments for decades. These studies show that sharing customer knowledge across departments is a communication problem with some counterintuitive answers.

**More meetings are not always better, and formal channels get used more.** Maltz and Kohli surveyed 788 managers outside marketing at high-tech equipment manufacturers about the market intelligence they received from marketing. Both the frequency and the formality of that communication had nonlinear effects on how good the information seemed, meaning the payoff from communicating more often or more formally was not constant. They also found a "mere formality effect": intelligence delivered through formal channels was used more than intelligence passed along informally. How often marketing shared information was related to factors such as joint customer visits, the sender's positional power, and the receiver's trust in the sender <Cite k="maltz1996dissemination" />.

**Marketing and sales see the world differently, and some differences help.** Homburg and Jensen studied the "thought worlds" of marketing and sales. In general, differences between the two groups hampered cooperation, which led to weaker market performance. Some differences, however, improved performance directly. Business units did better when one side argued for the customer while the other argued for the product, or when one side pushed short-term considerations and the other long-term ones. Differences in product knowledge and interpersonal skills, by contrast, hurt performance <Cite k="homburg2007thoughtworlds" />.

**Working with R&D depends on structured contact.** Griffin and Hauser reviewed research on integrating R&D and marketing in new product development. They organized the methods firms use into categories that include relocating people and designing facilities, moving personnel between functions, informal social systems, organizational structures, incentives, and formal integrative management processes <Cite k="griffin1996rdmarketing" />. Most of these methods work by putting marketers and engineers in more frequent, better-structured conversation.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Briefing other departments formally and at a steady rhythm.** The Maltz and Kohli findings suggest that a regular, structured briefing (a monthly market readout, for example) is more likely to be acted on than a stream of hallway updates, and that simply sending more updates is not a reliable way to raise their value.

**2. Arguing a position productively with sales.** Homburg and Jensen's results imply that marketing managers should not try to erase every disagreement with sales. The skill is making the customer or long-term case clearly while respecting the sales team's short-term or product view.

**3. Translating customer insight for product teams.** Consulting with product development on design and packaging requires turning research into concrete requests that engineers can act on.

**4. Negotiating with vendors and distributors.** Contract negotiations with distributors reward clear opening positions, careful listening for the other side's constraints, and a calm pace.

**5. Presenting to executives and at trade shows.** Pitching a campaign or launch plan to leadership, or presenting a product at a trade show, calls for a short, structured story with one main claim and evidence behind it.

## How to practice

- **Build a five-minute market readout.** Draft a recurring briefing for sales or product teams with three customer insights and one recommended action. Use the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to keep it under five minutes.
- **Make research sound concrete.** Read your briefing aloud, then check it with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker). Replace abstract phrases ("drive engagement") with specific customer behaviors.
- **Rehearse a campaign pitch.** Practice your next campaign pitch with the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter), then deliver it a second time from a single index card.
- **Trade show introductions.** Use the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) to prepare a 30-second spoken introduction to your product for buyers and partners.
- **Listen for your fillers in debates.** Record a mock disagreement with a sales colleague and run the transcript through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter). Fillers often climb when you defend a position you have not fully prepared.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much do marketing managers present?

In O*NET data, 16.07% of marketing managers report speaking to an audience weekly and 0.49% daily. Phone calls and meetings with partners and colleagues make up far more of their speaking time.

### Should marketing share customer insights with other teams as often as possible?

Not necessarily. Maltz and Kohli found that the frequency of sharing had a nonlinear effect on how useful the information seemed, and that formally delivered intelligence was used more. A predictable, structured cadence tends to work better than constant informal updates.

### How is this different from a sales manager's speaking work?

Marketing managers mostly persuade colleagues, partners, and executives, while sales managers spend more of their talk coaching salespeople and speaking with customers directly.
