Customer service representatives are measured, often minute by minute, on conversations. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 3.88 out of 5 in importance for this occupation, oral expression at 4.12, and oral comprehension at 4.12. Customer service representatives rank 318 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, with a score of 67.9 <Cite k="onet31" />. The phone dominates: telephone conversations average 5.0 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale, the maximum. Representatives answer questions, take orders, explain bills, and absorb complaints, frequently from customers who are already frustrated before the call begins.

This is one of the largest occupations in the country. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported 2,595,750 customer service representatives in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $44,770 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Nearly every O*NET task statement for this job is a spoken exchange or depends on one <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Confer with customers by telephone or in person to provide information about products or services, take or enter orders, cancel accounts, or obtain details of complaints."
- "Contact customers to respond to inquiries or to notify them of claim investigation results or any planned adjustments."
- "Resolve customers' service or billing complaints by performing activities such as exchanging merchandise, refunding money, or adjusting bills."
- "Obtain and examine all relevant information to assess validity of complaints and to determine possible causes, such as extreme weather conditions that could increase utility bills."
- "Refer unresolved customer grievances to designated departments for further investigation."
- "Solicit sales of new or additional services or products."

Beyond the phone, face-to-face discussions average 3.61, and dealing with external customers or the public rates 4.79 out of 5 for importance. Public speaking is rare at 1.65. Training and teaching others rates 4.02 out of 5 for importance, which reflects how much time representatives spend walking customers through processes step by step.

## What the research says

Customer service is one of the few jobs where researchers can study thousands of real spoken and written exchanges, and the findings are specific enough to change how representatives phrase things.

**Saying "I" works better than the company "we."** Packard, Moore, and McFerran found that firm agents at over 40 companies tended to emphasize "we" (the firm) and "you" (the customer). Across five studies using lab experiments and field data, agents who referred to themselves as "I" increased customers' perceptions that the agent felt for them and was acting on their behalf. Those perceptions of empathy and agency raised customer satisfaction, purchase intentions, and actual purchases. Customer-focused "you" pronouns had little effect and sometimes backfired <Cite k="packard2018pronouns" />.

**Concrete language signals listening.** Packard and Berger studied linguistic concreteness, meaning how tangible and specific an employee's words are. In five studies, including text analysis of more than 1,000 real customer-employee interactions in two field settings, customers were more satisfied, more willing to buy, and bought more when employees spoke concretely. The mechanism was perception: customers inferred that employees using concrete language were listening to them <Cite k="packard2021concrete" />. In practice, that is the difference between "I'll send the replacement charger today" and "I'll take care of that for you."

**Listening shows up as three behaviors on the phone.** De Ruyter and Wetzels studied voice-to-voice service encounters in call centers and separated listening into attentiveness, perceptiveness, and responsiveness. Attentiveness directly drove satisfaction with the encounter, perceptiveness was mainly related to trust, and responsiveness was related to both. Satisfaction and trust, in turn, affected whether customers intended to contact the call center again <Cite k="deruyter2000listening" />.

**Too much warmth can get in the way of the fix.** Marinova, Singh, and Singh analyzed video recordings of real problem-solving interactions with airline customers, plus a controlled experiment. Representatives' problem-solving work had a positive effect on satisfaction that grew as the interaction went on. That effect weakened when representatives did relatively more relational work or displayed more positive emotion, leading the authors to conclude that overdoing relational talk and positive affect diminishes the effectiveness of problem-solving interactions <Cite k="marinova2018frontline" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Taking personal ownership.** Saying "I can fix this" instead of "We'll look into it" is a small change with experimental support. It matters most when resolving complaints and notifying customers of adjustments.

**2. Being concrete.** Naming the exact action, item, and time ("I've refunded 42 dollars to your card; it will show in three to five business days") tells customers they were heard. Vague reassurance does not.

**3. Showing you are listening.** Attentiveness (not interrupting, acknowledging), perceptiveness (catching what the customer means), and responsiveness (answering what was asked) each relate to satisfaction or trust.

**4. Balancing empathy with problem-solving.** Acknowledge feelings briefly, then move to the fix. The airline research suggests that extended rapport talk or forced cheerfulness can dilute the effect of actually solving the problem.

**5. Explaining processes step by step.** With teaching rated 4.02 out of 5 in importance, walking a customer through a return, a billing change, or a setup is a core skill. One step per sentence, with a check-in, works best on the phone.

## How to practice

- **Rewrite your stock phrases.** List the ten phrases you say most often. Rewrite each to use "I" for your own actions and a concrete detail. Read the new versions aloud until they feel natural.
- **Concreteness check.** Paste a typical explanation you give customers into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and replace abstract words with specific ones.
- **Pace your explanations.** Customers on the phone cannot see what you see. Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) to keep instructions at a comfortable pace.
- **Pause after the acknowledgment.** After "I understand why that's frustrating," a short pause lets the customer feel heard before you move to the fix. Practice the timing with the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice).
- **Fillers under pressure.** Record a mock complaint call and run the transcript through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter). Fillers tend to rise while you search for an answer in your systems.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should customer service representatives say "I" or "we"?

Experiments and field data from Packard and colleagues found that representatives who said "I" when describing their own actions were seen as more empathetic and more likely to act on the customer's behalf, which raised satisfaction and purchases.

### Can being too friendly hurt a service call?

In a study of airline service interactions, heavier relational talk and positive emotional displays weakened the positive effect of problem-solving on customer satisfaction. Brief empathy followed by a concrete fix appears to work better.

### How is this job different from IT support?

Both are phone-heavy, but customer service representatives handle billing, orders, and complaints for external customers, while [IT support specialists](/professions/computer-support-specialists) diagnose technical problems, often for coworkers.
