Pharmacists are often pictured counting pills, but the job depends heavily on talk. O*NET® rates speaking importance for pharmacists at 4 out of 5, and the occupation ranks 242nd of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Pharmacists explain how to take a drug, warn about interactions, answer questions about over-the-counter products, and call prescribers to sort out problems with an order. Much of the safety value of a pharmacist is delivered in short conversations.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements spell out the spoken side of pharmacy. Pharmacists "provide information and advice regarding drug interactions, side effects, dosage, and proper medication storage" and "advise customers on the selection of medication brands, medical equipment, or healthcare supplies." They also "collaborate with other health care professionals to plan, monitor, review, or evaluate the quality or effectiveness of drugs or drug regimens, providing advice on drug applications or characteristics," teach pharmacy interns, and refer patients to other health professionals <Cite k="onet31" />.

The work context ratings show how often these conversations happen. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.96 out of 5 and telephone use 4.86, so both are daily. The importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.14. Public speaking is rare at 2.25, with no incumbents reporting it daily.

Pharmacist talk falls into three main settings:

- **At the counter.** A short counseling exchange with a patient picking up a new prescription, often with a line behind them.
- **On the phone with prescribers.** Calls to clarify a dose, flag an interaction, or suggest an alternative, usually to a busy office.
- **On the phone with patients.** Follow-up calls about adherence, side effects, or refills, a setting where the pharmacist has no visual cues.

## What the research says

**Counseling varies widely, and busyness cuts it.** In a field study across 306 community pharmacies in eight states, trained shoppers posing as patients presented three new prescriptions at each pharmacy. About 63% of shoppers received oral drug information, with a mean of 2.3 informational items. Busyness reduced the odds that the pharmacist talked with the shopper at all, gave oral information, or checked understanding. The share of shoppers who received any information rose from 40% to 94% as state counseling regulations became more intensive <Cite k="svarstad2004counseling" />. The study is from 2004, so current counseling rates may differ.

**Phone counseling reduced deaths in a randomized trial.** A 2-year trial enrolled patients taking five or more drugs for chronic disease who had been found non-adherent. The intervention group received periodic telephone counseling from a pharmacist. After two years, 11% of the intervention group had died compared with 17% of the control group. After adjustment, telephone counseling was associated with a 41% reduction in the risk of death, and about 16 patients needed counseling to prevent one death <Cite k="wu2006telephone" />. This is a single trial in one hospital clinic, so the size of the effect should be read with care.

**Counseling at discharge prevented drug harm.** A randomized trial of 178 patients discharged from a general medicine service tested pharmacist counseling at discharge plus a follow-up phone call 3 to 5 days later. The pharmacists reviewed indications, directions, and side effects, screened for adherence barriers, and fed problems back to physicians. Thirty days after discharge, preventable adverse drug events were found in 11% of control patients and 1% of intervention patients. Total adverse drug events and health care use did not differ. Pharmacists also found unexplained differences between pre-admission medications and discharge orders in 49% of intervention patients <Cite k="schnipper2006counseling" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Opening the counseling conversation.** The field study shows that counseling often does not happen at all. A short opening question at the counter ("Has your doctor told you what this is for?") starts the exchange and shows what the patient already knows.

**2. Prioritizing the warning.** A full list of side effects read at the register buries the point that matters. Lead with the one or two points that prevent harm: the interaction to avoid, the side effect that needs a call, or the timing that matters.

**3. Checking understanding.** The Svarstad study counted "questions asked to assess shopper understanding" as part of good counseling. Asking the patient to say how they will take the drug catches errors that a nod does not.

**4. Talking to prescribers efficiently.** A call to a prescriber's office should state the problem, the patient, and a specific suggestion. "Ms. Diaz is on warfarin and this new antibiotic can raise her INR. Would you consider an alternative?" is easier to act on than a general concern.

**5. Phone voice without visual feedback.** On follow-up calls, the pharmacist cannot see confusion. Pause more often, ask direct questions about how doses are going, and summarize at the end.

## How to practice

- **Build three counseling scripts.** Write a 30-second counter script for each of your three most-dispensed new-start drugs. Use the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to keep each one short enough for a busy line.
- **Simplify the language.** Paste each script into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and replace terms a patient would not use, such as "hepatic" or "concomitant."
- **Rehearse prescriber calls.** Write the first two sentences of a typical clarification call and practice them aloud until the request comes early.
- **Pace your phone counseling.** Record a mock follow-up call and check your rate with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Without visual cues, a measured pace gives the listener room to ask questions.
- **Practice the pause after the warning.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) can help you stop after the key safety point so the patient has a moment to ask a question.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does pharmacist counseling actually improve outcomes?

In randomized trials, pharmacist counseling with phone follow-up reduced preventable adverse drug events after discharge, and periodic telephone counseling for patients on many drugs was associated with lower mortality. Both were single trials, so the exact size of the benefit is uncertain.

### How often do pharmacists counsel patients?

In a study using trained shoppers in 306 community pharmacies, about 63% received oral drug information. Counseling was less likely when the pharmacy was busy and more likely in states with stronger counseling rules.

### Do pharmacists need public speaking skills?

Formal public speaking is rare, rated 2.25 out of 5 in O*NET data. The skills that matter most are short, clear one-on-one explanations in person and on the phone.
