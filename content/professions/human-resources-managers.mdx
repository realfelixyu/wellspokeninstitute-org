HR managers need to ask a question that several candidates can answer on equal terms. They also need to explain a decision clearly enough that an employee can question it. Those conversations call for different habits. In an interview, give each candidate the same starting point and leave room for a concrete example. In a decision meeting, tell the employee what has been decided early enough that they have time to respond.

Human resources managers rank 45th among 910 occupations in the Institute’s comparison of speaking demands, with a score of 75.9 out of 100. O*NET® rates the importance of speaking at 4.25 out of 5 for the role. Its task descriptions include explaining workplace policies to employees, handling staffing disputes and advising managers <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

An employee says the weekend rota changed without warning. The department head remembers giving a week's notice. An HR manager can open the conversation by asking each person when they first saw the revised rota, what channel it came through and what they understood the change to mean. That sequence gives the group a timeline before anyone tries to explain someone else's intentions. Handling employee questions and helping resolve disputes both appear in the O*NET task statements for this role <Cite k="onet31" />.

The audience changes when the HR manager briefs a department head. The employee may want to know whether tomorrow's shift has changed. The department head may need to know which version of the rota reached the team. Keep the distinction audible: “Here is what we know about tomorrow. Here is the part of the rollout I still need to check.”

## What the research says

Campion, Pursell and Brown studied a process for hiring entry-level production employees. The interviewers used job-based questions, a panel and examples of how to score answers. Panelists' scores agreed closely, and the interview scores predicted later performance in that setting. Because the researchers tested the whole interview process, several features could account for its performance <Cite k="campion1988structured" />.

An interview also helps the candidate decide whether to take the job. In a qualitative study, Wingate and Bourdage spoke with 29 professional interviewers. They described wanting to assess applicants, make a good impression and give applicants information. These were interviewers’ accounts of how they designed their conversations. The researchers did not compare hiring outcomes across formats <Cite k="wingate2024interviews" />.

Richter and colleagues tested training for people delivering dismissal news. Across two studies, trained participants did better at delivering the news in simulated meetings and showed behaviors associated with procedural fairness. The transfer exercise later on this page applies the rehearsal method in a different setting <Cite k="richter2016badnews" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

### Ask about the work a candidate would actually do

Suppose two supervisors are hiring someone to coordinate shift changes. One wants a “good communicator.” Ask what that person actually has to say on a busy day. The answer might be, “They have to tell the incoming supervisor which orders are late and who has already called the customer.” That gives the panel something observable to ask about.

Try this opening with each candidate: “Tell us about a handover where something important was still unresolved. What did you tell the next person?” Agree beforehand on a neutral follow-up, such as “What did you personally do?” Afterward, have panelists write down the action they heard before discussing their scores. If someone says “very polished,” ask which part of the handover the candidate actually explained.

### Give the candidate a turn

A simple transition helps both sides. After the scored portion, say, “Those are our questions about the shift. What would you like to know about working it?” If the candidate asks how often the schedule changes, answer using the actual policy or a recent example that can be shared. If you need the staffing manager to check, tell the candidate who will follow up.

### Close an unresolved question with an owner

When an employee asks why a request stalled, a vague “we'll check” can send them back to the same queue. Name the part you can check and the person who will contact them. Ask which question they want that person to answer. You may find that you were preparing to explain the policy while the employee simply needed the date their request was received.

## How to practice a difficult employee conversation

Take a fictional example: an employee requested a transfer and the role went to another applicant. Ask a colleague to play the employee. Open with the decision, give the reason you can explain, then stop so the person can ask a question. If they ask whether another position will open next month and the staffing plan is unsettled, practice saying exactly that. Finish by naming the next conversation you can commit to.

Ask the colleague to recall the decision, the reason they heard and the question still on their mind. A listener who remembers your sympathetic opening but cannot tell whether the transfer was approved has shown you where to revise.

For a solo rehearsal, put the opening and the first follow-up into the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice). Read the decision aloud. Pause long enough for someone to ask a question. On a second run, ask a colleague to interrupt before the explanation. Can you answer what they asked without starting the entire script again? Leave employee names and details out of the practice copy.

## Frequently asked questions

### What can I do when two interviewers disagree about the same answer?

Ask each interviewer to read back the candidate's words that affected their score. One might have heard the candidate name who owned the handover, while the other remembers no deadline. Those observations can both be accurate. Compare them with the scoring example the panel agreed to before interviewing anyone. If the example itself is ambiguous, flag it for revision before the next hiring round. Changing the rule mid-round makes it harder to compare candidates who have already answered.

### Is this mostly public speaking?

Much of the work takes place in smaller conversations. O*NET gives face-to-face discussions a frequency rating of 4.81 and telephone conversations 4.85 on a scale where 5 means daily. Speaking to an audience rates 2.77 on the same scale. These averages describe the occupation across workplaces <Cite k="onet31" />.

### What do the employment figures cover?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 220,660 U.S. human resources manager jobs in its May 2025 wage survey, with a median annual wage of $149,280. Its separate employment projections put growth in this occupation at 5.5% from 2025 to 2035. These figures refer to human resources managers, occupation code 11-3121. HR specialists have a separate code <Cite k="blsoews2025,blsoutlook2035" />.
