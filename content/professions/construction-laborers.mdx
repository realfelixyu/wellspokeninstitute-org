Construction laborers use speech to check the day’s instructions, report a hazard and coordinate with operators. An exchange may take only a few seconds, as when a laborer asks the foreman which entrance the next delivery will use. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 3.25 out of 5 for importance. Face-to-face discussions score 4.80 out of 5 for frequency and telephone conversations 4.31, while public speaking scores 1.38. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 51.2 out of 100, a score shared by the occupations listed 653rd through 655th of 910 <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

One core O*NET task for this occupation is about communicating with machines and the people running them: laborers "signal equipment operators to facilitate alignment, movement, or adjustment of machinery, equipment, or materials." Other tasks put them between the work zone and the public, such as "control traffic passing near, in, or around work zones," and alongside other trades, such as providing assistance to carpenters, plasterers or masons <Cite k="onet31" />. Teaching others rates 3.73 out of 5 for importance <Cite k="onet31" />.

The two O*NET work context questions on noise and hazardous equipment each had 21 respondents. On average they rated exposure to distracting or uncomfortable noise at 4.12 out of 5 and exposure to hazardous equipment at 4.34 <Cite k="onet31" />.

Picture a fictional crew setting a pipe section with a crane. Teodor, the designated and qualified signal person, can see the landing point. The operator cannot. If another worker starts giving ordinary directions, the operator may hear two instructions at once. There is a separate rule for anyone who sees a safety problem.

## What the research says

**When a crane operator needs signals.** OSHA’s construction crane and derrick standard, 29 CFR 1926.1419, requires a signal person when the operator lacks a full view of the point of operation. One person at a time gives directions from the operator’s perspective. Anyone who becomes aware of a safety problem must give a stop or emergency stop signal to alert the operator or signal person. If signal transmission fails, the operator must safely stop the operations that depend on signals. They can resume once transmission returns and a proper signal has been given and understood. For hand signals the Standard Method applies unless it is infeasible or does not cover the operation or attachment. Where nonstandard signals are used, the signal person, operator and lift director, if there is one, must agree on them before the operation. These provisions concern equipment within the construction crane and derrick standard <Cite k="osha1926cranesignals" />.

**Flagging traffic.** Traffic flagging has separate requirements. Under OSHA’s construction signaling rule, flaggers’ signals, how flaggers are used, and the warning garments they wear must conform to Part 6 of the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices <Cite k="osha1926flaggers" />. A worker who has learned the signals for a crane still needs the instructions for their assigned traffic-control role.

**Fear of speaking up, in workers' own words.** Cora Roelofs and colleagues held Spanish-language focus groups in Lawrence, Massachusetts, with 18 Hispanic construction workers, 8 union and 10 non-union, all men born in Puerto Rico or the Dominican Republic. Participants described supervisor pressure and "intimidation with regard to raising safety concerns." They feared being fired or not offered future work if they complained about a ladder or unsafe conditions. Not understanding spoken English was not strongly raised as a cause. In one exchange, workers said there was always a foreman who spoke Spanish to explain tasks and translate. The authors wrote that Spanish-language training for workers is necessary but "may not translate into improved conditions" <Cite k="roelofs2011hispanicfocus" />.

**Few trainees reported speaking up afterward.** In Las Vegas, an assertiveness simulation was part of fall prevention classes for construction workers. Eight weeks later, more than half of the 760 trainees completed telephone interviews. Among respondents, 11% of Hispanic trainees and 28% of non-Hispanic trainees named the assertiveness component among the topics they found most useful. Only 2% of each group reported practicing assertiveness at work. The authors suggested Hispanic workers may weigh job security above speaking up, and recommended improving safety climate and engineering controls instead <Cite k="shrestha2014assertiveness" />.

**Training time and English ability.** Tom O'Connor and colleagues interviewed 50 young Latino immigrants, aged 21 or under, who had worked at least 10 days in construction in the previous year. Of those who reported hazardous tasks, 68% to 72% said they had received some training, but the median training time was 1 hour, and only 24% received written material. Those with less English ability received less training <Cite k="oconnor2005latinotraining" />.

**Safety meetings and safety climate.** Thomas Arcury and colleagues surveyed 119 Latino residential framers, roofers and general construction workers in western North Carolina, and 90 of them phoned in daily diary reports for up to 21 days. Workers who reported attending meetings on more days tended to give a higher overall safety-climate rating. That association fell short of the study’s threshold for statistical significance <Cite k="arcury2012latinoclimate" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Clarify the assignment.** For a lift under the construction crane standard, find out who has the signaling assignment. A laborer who works near a crane may need to raise a concern without being the person assigned to direct the lift. The emergency stop provision applies to anyone who sees a safety problem <Cite k="osha1926cranesignals" />.

**Describe the place unambiguously.** In a planning conversation, two colleagues standing on opposite sides of a sketch may each call a different part of the site “the left side.” In the example above, directions to the crane operator take the operator's perspective. For an ordinary hazard report, use a fixed reference such as “the east entrance” so both people can locate the problem <Cite k="osha1926cranesignals" />.

**Asking about the plan.** O*NET lists reading plans and instructions to decide the day's work. When an instruction is unclear, ask a specific question ("Do the forms come off tomorrow or after the inspection?") rather than guess.

**Raising a hazard.** Name the hazard, where it is and what you need: "The ladder on the east side has a cracked rail. I need a different one before I go up."

## How to practice

These drills have not been tested in a study. They draw on the tasks, rule and studies above.

**Draw the site.** Give two people blank copies of the same simple plan. On yours, mark a fictitious damaged ladder at the east entrance and a delivery expected at the west gate. Without showing the marks, explain both details. Ask your partner to mark their copy and tell you which job they think needs attention. Compare the two maps. If the partner marked the wrong entrance, try again with a fixed location rather than a direction relative to where you are sitting.

**Check a change of assignment.** A fictional foreman explains that the delivery will arrive after the break, then mentions another job near the old drop-off point. Ask a question that establishes where the delivery will go. If the foreman does not know yet, have the listener identify who will check and how the crew will hear the answer. Leave machinery and vehicles out of the exercise.

**Hazard report in three parts.** Write a 15-second report: what, where, what you need. Say it to a partner playing a foreman who is in a hurry and answers, "Just finish it." Practice your second sentence. The Institute's guide to [asking someone to repeat what you missed](/learn/how-to-ask-someone-to-repeat-what-you-missed) helps when a phone call breaks up.

## Frequently asked questions

### What did workers say made it hard to raise a hazard?

In the Massachusetts focus groups, workers described fear of losing work if they raised a concern. Participants also described a Spanish-speaking foreman who could explain tasks. A supervisor who hears no objections still needs to make room for workers to raise a problem <Cite k="roelofs2011hispanicfocus" />.

### Who is allowed to give signals to a crane?

Under OSHA's crane rule for construction, only one person gives signals at a time, but anyone who sees a safety problem must give the stop or emergency stop signal <Cite k="osha1926cranesignals" />.

### How much do construction laborers earn?

BLS estimated 1,096,780 construction laborer jobs in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $47,120 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.
