Nurse practitioners spend most of the workday in conversation, and a large share of that conversation is persuasion: helping someone take a medication, cut back on drinking, or come back for a test. O*NET® rates speaking importance for the occupation at 4 out of 5 and oral expression at 4.12, and nurse practitioners rank 171st of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Face-to-face discussion and telephone use each receive the maximum frequency rating of 5, meaning they happen every working day.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for nurse practitioners put spoken counseling at the center of the role. Nurse practitioners "counsel patients about drug regimens and possible side effects or interactions with other substances, such as food supplements, over-the-counter (OTC) medications, or herbal remedies," "educate patients about self-management of acute or chronic illnesses, tailoring instructions to patients' individual circumstances," and "recommend interventions to modify behavior associated with health risks" <Cite k="onet31" />. They also recommend tests and treatments "with attention to safety, cost, invasiveness, simplicity, acceptability, adherence, and efficacy," which is a long list of things to explain to a patient in plain words.

Other spoken tasks face colleagues: consulting with or referring to specialists, supervising support staff, and talking with peers to keep current. The importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.26 out of 5. Formal public speaking is uncommon at 2.22, and none of the incumbents surveyed reported doing it daily, though 17.39% report it weekly, which likely reflects group education and staff teaching.

What makes the nurse practitioner's speaking distinct is the combination. In a single 20-minute visit, the same clinician may take a history, deliver a diagnosis, explain a prescription and its interactions, and then try to change a habit the patient has held for decades.

## What the research says

**Patients tend to be more satisfied with nurse practitioner visits, and those visits run longer.** A 2002 systematic review in the BMJ pooled 11 trials and 23 observational studies comparing nurse practitioners and doctors at the first point of contact in primary care. Patients were more satisfied with nurse practitioner care (standardized mean difference 0.27), health status did not differ, and nurse practitioner consultations were longer by an average of 3.67 minutes <Cite k="horrocks2002np" />. The review did not measure communication directly, but longer visits and higher satisfaction suggest more time spent talking and explaining.

**A larger Cochrane review reached similar conclusions.** The 2018 update included 18 randomized trials of nurses working as substitutes for primary care doctors, with nurse practitioners among the nurse types studied. Patient satisfaction was probably slightly higher with nurse-led care, and consultations were probably longer, both rated moderate-certainty evidence. Health outcomes were probably similar or better <Cite k="laurant2018nurses" />. Again, the review counts minutes and satisfaction scores, not the content of the talk, so the link to specific speaking behaviors is an inference.

**Motivational interviewing outperforms advice-giving.** Motivational interviewing is a counseling style built on open questions, reflective listening, and drawing out the patient's own reasons for change. A meta-analysis of 72 randomized trials found that it had a significant and clinically relevant effect in about three out of four studies, and it beat traditional advice-giving across a broad range of problems. Brief encounters of 15 minutes still showed an effect in 64% of studies, and more than one encounter improved results. One caution for this profession: studies led by psychologists and physicians found effects about 80% of the time, while studies by other health care providers found them 46% of the time <Cite k="rubak2005mi" />. Training and practice appear to matter.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Explaining medication interactions without a lecture.** The O*NET counseling task includes supplements, over-the-counter drugs, and herbal products. Start by asking what the patient already takes, including the things they do not consider medicine, then explain only the interactions that apply to them.

**2. Asking before telling.** Behavior-change counseling works better when the patient talks first. "What do you think would be hardest about cutting back?" gets information that a list of recommendations cannot.

**3. Reflecting back what you heard.** A short reflection ("So mornings are the problem, because you skip breakfast") shows the patient you heard them and lets them correct you. Reflective listening is one of the main techniques of motivational interviewing.

**4. Translating risk and cost.** When recommending a test or treatment, nurse practitioners are expected to weigh cost, invasiveness, and acceptability. Saying those trade-offs out loud, in numbers the patient can picture, turns a recommendation into a shared decision.

**5. Referring with a reason.** When a condition exceeds scope of practice, the patient should leave knowing why they are seeing a specialist and what question the specialist will answer.

## How to practice

- **Rewrite one standard explanation.** Pick the counseling script you give most often, such as starting a statin or an inhaler, and paste it into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker). Split any sentence it flags as hard to follow when heard once.
- **Count your questions.** Record a role-played visit with a colleague (not a real patient), then transcribe it. Count open questions, closed questions, and reflections. A visit with no reflections is a sign to slow down.
- **Time your opening.** Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) on a recorded explanation, then record the same explanation on a day the clinic runs late and compare the two rates.
- **Drill the pause after a question.** After asking about change, wait. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) builds tolerance for silence, which gives patients room to answer.
- **Practice the referral sentence.** Write one sentence per common referral that states the reason and the expected next step, and say it aloud until it sounds natural.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do patients prefer nurse practitioners to doctors?

Two systematic reviews found patient satisfaction was somewhat higher with nurse practitioner or nurse-led primary care, with similar health outcomes. Visits were also longer, which may explain part of the difference.

### What is motivational interviewing?

It is a counseling style that uses open questions, reflective listening, and the patient's own reasons for change instead of direct advice. A meta-analysis of 72 trials found it beat traditional advice-giving in about three out of four studies.

### How often do nurse practitioners speak in public?

Rarely. O*NET rates public speaking at 2.22 out of 5 for the occupation, while face-to-face and phone conversations are rated 5, the highest possible frequency.
