Food preparation workers need to tell a cook or supervisor what went into a batch, which supplies are running low and when a piece of equipment needs attention. O*NET® rates speaking importance for Food Preparation Workers at 2.88 out of 5. Face-to-face discussions score 4.10 for frequency and public speaking scores 1.78. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 41.4 out of 100, shared by the occupations listed 874th and 875th of 910. Much of the role's speaking can happen between two people at a prep station <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Suppose a fictional prep worker, Niko, sees that a replacement carton of stock has arrived. The early soup batch was made with the usual stock. Nobody has opened the replacement yet. A server asks, “Did the soup change today?” Niko knows which carton went into his batch. He may have no idea what the next shift will use or whether anyone else used a different stock. He can tell the server which batch he made and show the kitchen lead the packaging so the person responsible can check the request.

O*NET includes weighing ingredients, keeping records of quantities, helping cooks and reporting when supplies are low or equipment is malfunctioning. Telling the lead “We've got one carton left” is more useful if the lead also knows which carton and whether it has already been counted for a planned batch. An equipment report needs a different detail. “The display on the prep refrigerator is blank” tells the lead what a worker observed. It doesn't give anyone a temperature reading <Cite k="onet31" />.

## What the research says

**Equipment and time both came up when food workers discussed prep.** In 11 focus groups across eight U.S. states, restaurant workers and managers discussed how they actually handled tasks such as checking doneness and keeping food at the right temperature. Time pressure, available equipment and the expectations of managers and coworkers were among the factors participants raised. These were accounts of working conditions. They help explain why a report about a missing tool could be relevant, though the researchers did not test a reporting script <Cite k="green2005prepconstraints" />.

**A recipe chart and a conversation can serve different people.** Research commissioned by the UK's Food Standards Agency included interviews with owners of 30 small and medium-sized food businesses in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The team also visited 10 other businesses. In businesses with more staff in separate roles, researchers more often encountered written allergen information alongside communication between kitchen and front-of-house staff. A shop owner who buys ingredients, prepares the food and serves it can have information that a server in a larger restaurant must get from someone else. The study was qualitative and does not say how often U.S. restaurants use either arrangement <Cite k="fsabasis2023foodallergen" />.

At 278 U.S. restaurants, a separate research team interviewed managers, food workers and servers about food allergies. Food workers generally answered knowledge questions correctly, but some answers revealed gaps. For instance, fewer food workers recognized eggs as a major allergen than recognized peanuts. The interview questions measured what people knew. They did not check which ingredients were actually in any dish or establish that a particular worker could vouch for a meal <Cite k="radke2016foodworkerknowledge" />.

**Who answers an ingredient question varies by establishment.** In a Swiss survey, 387 food businesses participated. Among the 170 that reported giving allergen information orally through an informed person, respondents could name more than one role. Service staff were named by 109, while 73 named a chef, butcher or baker. That second group includes people who prepare food, but its members are not all prep workers. For a U.S. kitchen, a practical question is simply, “Who here checks an ingredient question and who gives the customer the answer?” <Cite k="eisenblaetter2022allergeninformation" />.

Reports about illness have a different recipient. U.S. researchers interviewed 491 food workers and 387 managers in nine states. Repeatedly working while experiencing vomiting or diarrhea was associated with several factors, including the absence of a policy requiring workers to report illness to a manager. This was an interview study, so it could not isolate the effect of making a phone call. For a worker, the finding gives a reason to learn the establishment's illness-reporting procedure and whom it designates to receive a report <Cite k="sumner2011illnessreporting" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Name a particular batch.** Niko can say, “I made the first soup batch with the stock from this carton. I didn't make the second batch.” The kitchen lead now knows which claim they can check against a package and which still needs checking with another worker.

**Be explicit about substitutions.** A product with the same everyday name can arrive in different packaging. If the product changes during your shift, say which item arrived, when you saw it and whether you used it. Keep the materials needed for the lead to verify the change according to the kitchen's procedure. An informal description of what you remember adding cannot establish that a meal meets a customer's allergy requirements.

**Give the lead a report someone can check.** If the usual carton is nearly empty, explain which planned work depends on it. A pretend example is “I have enough of the usual stock for this batch, and there's none set aside for the afternoon batch.” The lead can decide what to check or order. “We're out” might send someone to find more stock even though today's first batch is already accounted for.

## How to practice

Sit away from the prep area with three blank cards. Write “morning batch” on one, “afternoon batch” on another and “new carton, sealed” on the third. Give the cards to a partner playing a prep worker. Play a coworker asking whether both batches were made with the same carton. Ask the worker to say what they can establish and what someone still needs to check. Swap roles and change just one fact on a card.

For another round, replace the sealed-carton card with “prep refrigerator display blank.” Have the listener write down exactly what the worker reported. If the listener wrote that the refrigerator was warm, compare that claim with the card and try again. These are untested language exercises using imaginary foods and equipment. Real ingredient and food-safety decisions belong with the establishment's approved process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I answer a guest's allergy question from memory?

Tell the person handling the request what you know about the particular batch and which records or labels can be checked. Use the kitchen's procedure for verifying ingredients and communicating the answer.

### What if the same person preps and serves the food?

That person may have direct knowledge of the batch. The UK study included businesses where one worker handled several functions, as well as businesses with separate prep and service staff <Cite k="fsabasis2023foodallergen" />.

### Do the wage figures cover only restaurant prep cooks?

The BLS category is Food Preparation Workers across covered employers. Its May 2025 estimate was 893,600 U.S. jobs and a median annual wage of $35,320 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.
