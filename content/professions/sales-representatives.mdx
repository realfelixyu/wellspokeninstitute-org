For business-to-business sales representatives, the sales call is the product demonstration, the negotiation, and the relationship all at once. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4 out of 5 in importance for wholesale and manufacturing sales representatives (outside technical and scientific products), and the occupation ranks 255 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, at 69 <Cite k="onet31" />. Dealing with external customers rates 4.91 out of 5 for importance, and only 5 of the 910 occupations in our dataset rate it higher. A representative who knows the catalog but cannot read a buyer, ask the right questions, and adjust on the spot leaves orders on the table.

The occupation employed 1,238,190 people in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $72,080 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Most of the O*NET task statements for this role happen in conversation with a buyer <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Answer customers' questions about products, prices, availability, product uses, and credit terms."
- "Recommend products to customers, based on customers' needs and interests."
- "Contact regular and prospective customers to demonstrate products, explain product features, and solicit orders."
- "Negotiate with retail merchants to improve product exposure, such as shelf positioning and advertising."
- "Negotiate details of contracts and payments."
- "Consult with clients after sales or contract signings to resolve problems and to provide ongoing support."

Telephone conversations average 4.99 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale and face-to-face discussions 4.65, so both are effectively daily. Formal public speaking is rare: it averages 2.08, with 2.48% of respondents doing it daily and 0.25% weekly. The talk in this job is one-to-one or one-to-few, on the phone and across a buyer's desk.

## What the research says

Sales performance is one of the most heavily studied topics in marketing, which means this occupation has unusually strong evidence behind it.

**Adapting to the customer is linked to objective sales performance.** Franke and Park combined results from 155 samples covering more than 31,000 salespeople. Adaptive selling behavior, meaning changing your approach to fit each customer and situation, increased self-rated, manager-rated, and objective measures of performance. Customer orientation increased only self-rated performance. Both increased job satisfaction <Cite k="franke2006adaptive" />.

**Knowledge and adaptiveness are among the strongest predictors.** Verbeke, Dietz, and Verwaal synthesized studies of sales performance published from 1982 to 2008. Five groups of factors had significant relationships with performance: selling-related knowledge (β = .28), degree of adaptiveness (β = .27), role ambiguity (β = -.25), cognitive aptitude (β = .23), and work engagement (β = .23) <Cite k="verbeke2011drivers" />. Knowing the product and adjusting the conversation to the buyer sit at the top of the list.

**Reading the buyer early pays off.** Hall, Ahearne, and Sujan combined survey, observation, and objective sales data from salesperson-customer interactions. Salespeople who made accurate intuitive judgments about a customer at the start chose better initial strategies, sold more, and used less selling time. Performance suffered when an accurate first read was followed by an inaccurate deliberate reassessment. Listening skills and customer orientation shaped the accuracy of those deliberate judgments <Cite k="hall2015intuition" />.

**Active, empathetic listening supports trust and relationship quality.** Drollinger and Comer surveyed salespeople and found that active empathetic listening was positively related to their communication skills, relationship quality, and trust with customers, and that communication and trust played a role in predicting sales performance <Cite k="drollinger2013listening" />. The study relies on salespeople's own reports, so it shows associations.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Adaptive questioning in the first minutes.** Representatives "recommend products to customers, based on customers' needs," which first requires finding those needs. Early, open questions about the buyer's goals, constraints, and buying process give the representative the information needed to adapt.

**2. Listening that the buyer can hear.** Active empathetic listening is visible: paraphrasing the buyer's concern, asking a follow-up about it, and referring back to it later in the call. These moves build the trust that the listening research links to performance.

**3. Explaining products in the buyer's terms.** Product knowledge was the strongest predictor in the meta-analysis, but knowledge only helps when it is translated into the buyer's outcomes, such as shelf turns, margin, or downtime.

**4. Negotiating terms and placement.** Negotiating payments, contract details, and shelf positioning with retailers requires a clear opening position, patience, and the habit of trading concessions rather than giving them away.

**5. After-sale problem conversations.** Ongoing support calls after a contract is signed are where repeat orders are won or lost. Calm acknowledgment and a specific fix matter more than reassurance.

## How to practice

- **Discovery question bank.** Write ten open questions for a typical account and practice asking them in different orders with a colleague who plays a busy buyer. Notice which questions produce useful answers.
- **Paraphrase drill.** In practice calls, summarize the buyer's main concern in one sentence before you present anything. Record the calls and count how often you skipped this step.
- **Two-minute product story.** Script a two-minute explanation of your top product in customer outcomes. Check it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) and the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Phone pace check.** Because phone calls are a daily constant, test your phone delivery with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Fast talk on price and terms sounds like pressure.
- **Cold-call introduction.** Use the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) to prepare a 30-second opening for prospective customers.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is adaptive selling?

Adaptive selling means changing your sales approach during and between interactions to fit the customer and the situation. A meta-analysis of more than 31,000 salespeople found it was linked to better self-rated, manager-rated, and objective performance.

### Do sales representatives need public speaking skills?

Less than one-to-one conversation skills. O*NET respondents rate public speaking at 2.08 out of 5 for frequency in this role, while phone calls and face-to-face meetings happen nearly every day.

### How is this different from retail sales?

B2B representatives manage longer relationships, negotiate contracts and terms, and sell to professional buyers. [Retail salespersons](/professions/retail-salespersons) handle short, in-store conversations with shoppers, where first impressions and rapport carry more weight.
