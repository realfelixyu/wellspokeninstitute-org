General internists treat adults with many conditions at once, and much of that work is done by talking. O*NET® rates speaking importance for general internal medicine physicians at 4 out of 5, with oral expression required at a level of 5 on a 7-point scale, and the occupation ranks 108th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Internists talk with patients, with surgeons who want a risk assessment, with colleagues who need a consult, and with the physician taking over their patients overnight.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for general internists include patient-facing and colleague-facing talk in roughly equal measure. Internists "explain procedures and discuss test results or prescribed treatments with patients" and "advise patients and community members concerning diet, activity, hygiene, and disease prevention." They also "provide consulting services to other doctors caring for patients with special or difficult problems" and "advise surgeon of a patient's risk status and recommend appropriate intervention to minimize risk" <Cite k="onet31" />.

That consultative role is what sets internal medicine apart. A surgeon asking whether a patient can safely go to the operating room wants a clear opinion, the reasoning behind it, and the specific steps to reduce risk, delivered in a minute or two.

The work context ratings show daily conversation in both directions: face-to-face discussion is rated 4.58 out of 5 and telephone use 4.59. Public speaking is rated 2.16, and only 3.01% of incumbents report doing it weekly. For most internists, teaching rounds and case conferences are the closest thing to a presentation.

## What the research says

**Clinicians seldom ask for the full agenda, and interrupt quickly when they do.** Researchers reviewed 112 recorded clinical encounters from trials of shared decision-making tools. Clinicians asked about the patient's agenda in 40 encounters (36%). They asked more often in primary care (49%) than in specialty care (20%), a gap that fell just short of statistical significance. When they did ask, they interrupted in two-thirds of encounters after a median of 11 seconds, although uninterrupted patients needed a median of only 6 seconds to state their concern <Cite k="singhospina2019agenda" />. The time cost of listening is small; the cost of missing the main concern is not.

**Poor sign-out causes real errors.** In a critical incident study, 26 interns who had cared for 82 patients after receiving sign-out from another intern described 25 discrete incidents caused by communication failures during that sign-out. The main problems were omitted content, such as medications, active problems, and pending tests, and failure-prone processes such as skipping a face-to-face conversation. Nearly all failures led to uncertainty in care decisions. Interns wanted "thorough but relevant face-to-face verbal sign-outs that reviewed anticipated issues" <Cite k="arora2005signout" />.

**Serious-news conversations are teachable.** The Oncotalk program ran a 4-day residential workshop for 115 medical oncology fellows, a subspecialty of internal medicine, from 62 institutions. Fellows were recorded with standardized patients before and after. Afterward they used a mean of 5.4 more bad-news skills and 4.4 more skills for discussing a transition to palliative care. Before the workshop, 16% of fellows used the word "cancer" when giving bad news; afterward, 54% did <Cite k="back2007oncotalk" />.

**The relationship has a small but real effect on outcomes.** A meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials that manipulated the patient-clinician relationship found a small but statistically significant effect on objective or validated health outcomes (d = 0.11). The authors noted that few trials were designed to test this question directly <Cite k="kelley2014relationship" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Letting the opening statement finish.** The agenda research shows the habit that most needs changing is the early interruption. Ask an open question, then let the patient finish. Most patients finish quickly.

**2. Giving a consult opinion in one breath.** When advising a surgeon or another physician, lead with the recommendation, then the two or three reasons, then the specific steps. Save the full differential for the note.

**3. Signing out what could go wrong tonight.** Verbal sign-out should cover active problems, pending results, and anticipated issues, with a concrete "if this, then do that" for each. The sign-out study shows omitted content is the main failure.

**4. Saying the hard word.** When delivering serious news, use the plain term ("cancer," "dying") after a short warning, then stop and let the patient respond. The Oncotalk data show how often clinicians avoid the word without training.

**5. Explaining risk in numbers people can picture.** Internists routinely discuss treatments with trade-offs. Try natural frequencies ("about 3 in 100 people") in place of percentages with decimals, and check whether the patient can repeat the number back.

## How to practice

- **Time your sign-out.** Write a verbal sign-out for one complex patient and run it through the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). If it runs long, cut history and keep anticipated problems.
- **Rehearse the consult summary.** Practice a 30-second spoken recommendation to a surgeon. The [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) can be used as a simple stopwatch for short, structured answers.
- **Simplify a risk explanation.** Paste your explanation of a common treatment decision into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and fix any sentence it flags as dense.
- **Practice silence after serious news.** Use the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) to rehearse stopping after the key sentence.
- **Audit your openings.** In role-play, have a colleague time how long you let them speak before your first question. Aim to let the opening statement finish.

## Frequently asked questions

### How quickly do doctors interrupt patients?

In one study of 112 recorded encounters, clinicians who asked about the patient's agenda interrupted after a median of 11 seconds. Patients who were not interrupted needed a median of 6 seconds to finish.

### Can physicians learn to deliver bad news better?

Yes. In the Oncotalk study, oncology fellows who attended a 4-day workshop showed substantially more observed skills for giving bad news and discussing palliative care in standardized patient encounters.

### Why does verbal sign-out matter?

Interns in one study described 25 incidents caused by sign-out failures, mostly omitted information or skipped face-to-face conversations. A verbal handoff that covers anticipated problems lets the next physician act with less uncertainty.
