Retail selling is a job made of short conversations. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 3.75 out of 5 in importance for retail salespersons and oral expression at 4, and the occupation ranks 447 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score with a score of 62.8 <Cite k="onet31" />. The middle ranking reflects the fact that retail talk is less technical than, say, a negotiation or a presentation. The frequency is another matter: face-to-face discussions average 4.88 on a 1 to 5 scale. In the survey, 87.79% of respondents selected “every day.” A good retail salesperson has dozens of brief conversations per shift, and each one either helps the sale or quietly ends it.

Retail sales is one of the largest occupations in the country, with 3,897,860 workers in May 2025 and a median annual wage of $35,410 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for retail salespersons begin with a greeting and run through the whole sale <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Greet customers and ascertain what each customer wants or needs."
- "Recommend, select, and help locate or obtain merchandise based on customer needs and desires."
- "Describe merchandise and explain use, operation, and care of merchandise to customers."
- "Demonstrate use or operation of merchandise."
- "Exchange merchandise for customers and accept returns."
- "Sell or arrange for delivery, insurance, financing, or service contracts for merchandise."

Beyond face-to-face talk, telephone conversations average 4.61, often for calls to other stores or customer inquiries. Dealing with the public rates 4.6 out of 5 for importance. Public speaking averages 1.98, though 8.46% of respondents reported doing it daily and 12.34% weekly, which fits product demonstrations and in-store announcements.

## What the research says

**Echoing customers' words and gestures was linked to higher sales.** Jacob, Guéguen, Martin, and Boulbry ran an experiment in a retail store in which four sales clerks were instructed either to mimic some of customers' verbal expressions and nonverbal behavior or not to. Mimicry was associated with a higher sales rate, more customers following the clerk's suggestions, and more positive evaluations of both the clerk and the store. Those evaluations explained part of the link between mimicry and buying. A second experiment with a baseline condition confirmed the effect on behavior <Cite k="jacob2011mimicry" />.

**Warm delivery works through the customer's mood.** Tsai and Huang collected data from 352 employee-customer pairs in 169 retail shoe stores in Taiwan. Employees' affective delivery, the warmth and friendliness they displayed, increased customers' willingness to return to the store and to say good things about it to friends. The effect worked indirectly, by lifting customers' in-store mood and their perception of the employee's friendliness. Affective delivery also influenced how long customers stayed in the store, which in turn affected their intentions <Cite k="tsai2002affective" />.

**Rapport is related to satisfaction and loyalty.** Gremler and Gwinner studied rapport between customers and employees in two different service settings. They found two empirically distinct dimensions of rapport, and both were positively related to customer satisfaction, intent to stay loyal, and word-of-mouth recommendations <Cite k="gremler2000rapport" />.

**How salespeople explain their own wins matters too.** Silvester, Patterson, and Ferguson collected managers' performance ratings for 452 retail sales assistants. Assistants who explained their successes as the result of things within their control received higher performance ratings (r = .20) and were more satisfied with their work (r = .12) <Cite k="silvester2003retail" />. The correlations are modest, but they suggest that the way a salesperson thinks about each conversation shapes how they approach the next one.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. An open, unpressured greeting.** The first task in the O*NET task list is to greet customers and find out what they need. A brief, friendly greeting followed by one open question ("What brings you in today?") starts the conversation without pushing.

**2. Using the customer's own words.** The mimicry experiment suggests that reflecting a customer's language, such as calling an item "comfy" if they did, builds liking. Done subtly, it also shows you were listening.

**3. Warm, energetic delivery.** The shoe store study links displayed warmth to the customer's mood and their plans to return. In a spoken exchange, that warmth comes through mostly in tone of voice and unhurried attention.

**4. Explaining products simply.** Describing use, operation, and care means turning specifications into one or two practical points the customer will remember at home.

**5. Calm handling of returns and complaints.** Returns are a test of rapport. Acknowledging the problem before explaining policy keeps a disappointed customer from becoming a lost one.

## How to practice

- **Greeting variations.** Write five greetings that do not end in a yes-or-no question and practice them until each sounds natural. Avoid "Can I help you?", which invites "Just looking."
- **Listen and reflect drill.** With a coworker playing a shopper, practice repeating one key word or phrase the shopper used in your next sentence. Then check whether the conversation felt more connected.
- **Product explanations in 30 seconds.** Pick your store's three best sellers and script a 30-second explanation of each. Use the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to keep them short.
- **Smooth out fillers.** Record yourself describing a product, transcribe it, and run it through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter). Fewer fillers make product knowledge sound more confident.
- **Handle the after-work debrief.** After a shift, note one sale you made and what you did that helped. In the attribution study, assistants who credited their successes to things within their control received higher ratings, so it helps to name the controllable actions behind each sale.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do retail salespersons need strong speaking skills?

Yes, though the skills are conversational rather than presentational. Retail salespersons rank 447 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, but they report face-to-face conversations almost every day and rate dealing with the public at 4.6 out of 5.

### Does mirroring a customer really increase sales?

In one retail experiment, salespeople who mimicked some of customers' expressions and behavior had higher sales and better evaluations. It is a single study in one setting, so treat it as a promising technique, and keep it subtle.

### How is retail selling different from business-to-business sales?

Retail conversations are short and focused on one shopper's immediate need. [Wholesale and manufacturing sales representatives](/professions/sales-representatives) manage long relationships with professional buyers and negotiate contracts.
