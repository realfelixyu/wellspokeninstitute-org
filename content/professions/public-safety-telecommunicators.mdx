Public safety telecommunicators, usually called 911 dispatchers or call takers, work entirely through their voice. They cannot see the scene, touch the patient, or hand anyone a form. O*NET® rates speaking importance for the occupation at 4.12 out of 5 and oral expression at 4.38, and it ranks 81st of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. On one measure it stands alone: the importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.99 out of 5, the highest of all 910 occupations in our data.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for public safety telecommunicators read like a script for a single call. Call takers "question callers to determine their locations and the nature of their problems to determine type of response needed," "provide emergency medical instructions to callers," and "relay information and messages to and from emergency sites, to law enforcement agencies, and to all other individuals or groups requiring notification" <Cite k="onet31" />. They also answer routine inquiries, refer calls that do not need a dispatch, monitor radio frequencies, and read maps to give directions.

Telephone use receives the maximum frequency rating of 5. Face-to-face discussion is lower at 4.17, and public speaking is rated 1.18, close to never. The importance of communicating with people outside the organization is rated 4.78, among the five highest of any occupation in the data.

Telecommunicators talk to two very different audiences, often at the same time:

- **Callers** who may be panicking, injured, intoxicated, or watching someone they love collapse, and who need calm, directive instructions.
- **Responders** on the radio, who need short, coded, accurate information about location, hazards, and patient condition.

Switching between those registers, sometimes mid-sentence, is a core skill of the job.

## What the research says

**Recognizing cardiac arrest depends on the conversation.** A systematic review of 32 studies examined what helps or hinders dispatchers in recognizing that a caller's patient is in cardiac arrest. The dominant finding was the difficulty of recognizing abnormal or agonal breathing over the phone. The authors concluded that "the interaction between the caller and the Emergency Medical Dispatcher is critical," identified the caller's emotional distress as a major theme, and recommended regular dispatcher training <Cite k="kirby2021recognition" />.

**Wording changes whether callers start CPR.** A linguistic study analyzed 424 emergency calls for confirmed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest where the dispatcher recognized the arrest and the caller was with the patient. When dispatchers framed CPR as a choice ("do you want to do CPR?"), only 43% of callers agreed. When they framed it as a plan ("we are going to do CPR"), 97% agreed, and when they framed it as an obligation ("we need to do CPR"), 84% agreed. In 38% of calls where the caller first declined, the dispatcher eventually got agreement by trying again <Cite k="riou2018cpr" />. The design was observational, so other differences between calls may contribute, but the gap is large.

**Training and feedback improved survival.** In metropolitan Phoenix, a telephone CPR program for two regional dispatch centers combined guideline-based protocols, telecommunicator training, call audits, and feedback. Across 2,334 cardiac arrests, the share of calls with telephone CPR instructions rose from 43.5% to 52.8%, and the median time to the first chest compression fell from 256 to 212 seconds. Survival to hospital discharge rose from 9.1% to 12.0%, and survival with good functional outcome rose from 5.6% to 8.3% <Cite k="bobrow2016tcpr" />. This was a before-after study, not a randomized trial, but the improvements held after adjustment for other factors.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Directive language in a crisis.** The CPR wording study points to a simple rule: tell, don't ask. "We're going to do CPR together. Put the phone on speaker" gives a panicking caller a plan to follow.

**2. Precise questions about breathing.** Because agonal breathing is hard to recognize by phone, use your protocol's exact questions and ask the caller to describe the breathing rather than accepting "he's breathing" as the answer.

**3. Controlling the call.** When a caller starts telling the whole story, acknowledge it briefly, then redirect with the next question. A steady tone and short sentences help a distressed caller follow along.

**4. Counting and coaching aloud.** Telephone CPR requires counting compressions out loud and giving continuous encouragement. Keep your counting rhythm steady so the caller can match it.

**5. Radio brevity.** When relaying to responders, give location, nature, and hazards first, in your agency's standard order.

## How to practice

- **Script your CPR initiation.** Write the exact sentences you use to start telephone CPR, using future or obligation framing, and read them aloud until they come out firmly.
- **Practice compression counting.** Count aloud to a metronome at your protocol's compression rate, keeping your voice even. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps with controlled phrasing between instructions.
- **Check your pace on instructions.** Record yourself giving pre-arrival instructions and check the rate with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Instructions have to be fast enough to save time and slow enough to follow.
- **Simplify your instructions.** Paste a set of pre-arrival instructions into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and simplify any phrase a frightened caller might struggle to follow.
- **Review recorded calls.** If your center audits calls, as the Phoenix program did, listen to your own recordings for hesitations, questions phrased as options, and places where the caller lost track.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does what a 911 dispatcher says affect whether CPR happens?

Yes. In a study of 424 cardiac arrest calls, 43% of callers agreed to CPR when asked if they wanted to do it, compared with 97% when the dispatcher said "we are going to do CPR."

### Why is cardiac arrest hard to recognize over the phone?

A review of 32 studies found that abnormal or agonal breathing is difficult to identify from a caller's description, and the caller's distress complicates the conversation. The review recommends regular training for dispatchers.

### Do dispatchers need public speaking skills?

Not in the usual sense. O*NET rates public speaking at 1.18 out of 5, but telephone use is rated 5 and dealing with the public is rated more important for this job than for any other occupation in our data.
