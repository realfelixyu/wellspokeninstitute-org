At pickup, a dad asks how the afternoon went. “Mina had a good day” gives him little to go on. If you were with her, tell him something you saw. “She got the ends of her train track to meet today. She called me over to see it before we put the trains away.” Now he has something he can ask Mina about on the way home.

O*NET lists talking with parents or guardians about the day and keeping individual records among a childcare worker's duties. The occupation scores 54.9 out of 100 on the Institute’s Speaking Demand Score, higher than 34.6% of the 910 occupations in the dataset. Face-to-face discussion has a frequency rating of 4.55 out of 5 <Cite k="onet31" />.

## What can you find out at drop-off?

Suppose Mina’s dad tells you that she kept talking about the train table over breakfast. Then the next family arrives. Before he leaves, ask what she said about it. He may just want to share a story. Or he may tell you that she wants to build the track again and keeps asking whether the pieces will still be there. Those are different messages to pass to the person opening the play area.

How much time is there to talk? In one study, observers tracked 1,004 guardians dropping children off. The guardians spent an average of 63 seconds in the classroom, including the time it took to say goodbye to their children. In an earlier study at 16 privately operated centers, about two-thirds of arrivals and departures included a parent–staff exchange. The median *conversation* lasted 12 seconds. The two studies timed different things. Neither establishes how long a family should stay <Cite k="perlman2012dropoff,endsley1991childcaretransitions" />.

## What if someone else saw the part the family asks about?

At an afternoon pickup you may be the person at the door even though another worker spent the morning with Mina. If her dad asks about the train table and you weren't there, give him the part of the day you know. “I joined after lunch, so I missed the trains. She showed me the station she drew this afternoon. Would you like me to ask Jo about the track?” If he says yes, agree on when he'll hear back and pass on the actual question.

In an Australian study, researchers examined field notes from 27 people who assess early childhood education and care services. Assessors had recorded families and staff exchanging news about a child's day as well as talking about things such as the family's weekend. Those notes describe what assessors chose to record on their visits. They give examples of what a doorway conversation can hold, without telling us how often each kind happens <Cite k="casey2025childcarehandover" />.

## Practice a pickup across two shifts

Give a partner playing the morning worker a slip that says, “Mina made the track into a loop with two short curved pieces.” The afternoon worker’s slip says, “Mina drew a station after lunch.” Only the morning worker saw the track. Have a third person play the parent and ask the afternoon worker, “Did she get the pieces to fit?”

Try the exchange twice. The first time, the morning worker is still available to ask. The second time, they've already left. After each round, ask the parent what they learned about the track, what the afternoon worker saw for themselves, and whether anyone owes them an answer. If they understood “I'll ask tomorrow” as “Jo will call tonight,” rehearse just that line again.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every family need a long conversation at pickup?

Ask whether the parent has time. A parent with another pickup to make might welcome one detail now and time to talk later. The observational study at 16 centers found large differences between centers in how often parents and staff spoke and what they discussed <Cite k="endsley1991childcaretransitions" />.

### What is the U.S. job outlook for childcare workers?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects a 2.0% decline in employment from 2025 to 2035, while expecting an average of 150,300 openings per year as workers move to other occupations or leave the labor force <Cite k="blsoutlook2035" />.
