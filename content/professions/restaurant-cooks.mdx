“Hold the fish.” Two tables have ordered fish. Before changing the order you’re working on, ask, “Which table?” If the server answers “Eight, they're still on starters,” you can give a short reply they can correct on the spot. “Holding the two fish for eight. Eighteen stays as ordered.”

## What does a cook need to say during service?

In a small beach restaurant in Italy, a server called each order to the kitchen, then hung up a written copy. Researchers filmed the two cooks working. When dishes were ready, one of the cooks, usually the one stationed nearest the window, called the server to collect them <Cite k="galatolo2008twocooks" />. The order coming in and the dish going out each had its own call. The ticket stayed on the wall where either cook could check it.

An observation and interview study in 13 small Swedish restaurants gives another example. During service, the observer heard chefs thank servers to acknowledge incoming orders. Staff also used short confirmations with each other in the kitchen. Much of the work took place quietly <Cite k="wellton2018restaurantservice" />. In those kitchens, a server hearing “thank you” knew the order had reached the cook. They would still need to know when to collect it.

## What changes when one table needs more time?

Gary Fine spent a month in each of four restaurants in Minnesota’s Twin Cities and interviewed 32 cooks. Servers often told cooks that dishes were needed sooner or later than expected. Each restaurant had its own way of using tickets. In one, moving a ticket from a wheel to a counter told the cooks that diners were getting close to their main course <Cite k="fine1990kitchentime" />.

Suppose a server asks, “Can eight wait? They’re still eating.” You have both starters and mains for eight on your station. “Do you want me to hold just the mains for eight?” settles which part of the ticket should wait. Use the table and course names people use at your restaurant.

## Practice with two tickets before service

Write two practice tickets. Table eight ordered two fish with potatoes. Table eighteen ordered one fish with potatoes. Give a coworker the role of server and let them privately choose which table needs to wait. Have them tell you, “They’re still on starters. Can you hold the fish?”

Ask for the missing detail. Once you have it, tell the server which dish you’ll hold and what will happen to the other ticket. Ask them to point to the ticket they expect to collect first. If you had the other ticket in mind, try the exchange again, naming both tables. This rehearsal borrows the two features the field studies described, a written ticket and a spoken timing change <Cite k="galatolo2008twocooks,fine1990kitchentime" />.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if I missed the table number?

Say which part you heard. “I heard two fish. Which table?” Check the answer against the ticket. If the ticket says one fish, point to the difference and ask the caller to confirm the count.

### How do I answer when a server asks if a table is ready?

Say what you can see from your station. “Eighteen’s fish is ready here. I’m waiting for the potatoes.” If the server needs a time for the whole table, check with the person handling the potatoes before giving an estimate.
