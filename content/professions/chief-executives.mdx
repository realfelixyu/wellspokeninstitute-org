Few jobs depend on speaking as much as running an organization. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, chief executives rank 25 of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, with a score of 78.9 out of 100. Speaking rates 4.25 out of 5 for importance, and the level of speaking skill required is 4.75 on a 7-point scale <Cite k="onet31" />. A chief executive's decisions mostly reach the organization as spoken words: in board meetings, town halls, investor calls, negotiations, and one-on-one conversations with the executives who carry out the plan.

The occupation is small and highly paid. In May 2025 there were 204,350 chief executives in the United States, with a median annual wage of $213,990 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for chief executives include an unusual number of formal speaking duties <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Confer with board members, organization officials, or staff members to discuss issues, coordinate activities, or resolve problems."
- "Deliver speeches, write articles, or present information at meetings or conventions to promote services, exchange ideas, or accomplish objectives."
- "Serve as liaisons between organizations, shareholders, and outside organizations."
- "Make presentations to legislative or other government committees regarding policies, programs, or budgets."
- "Negotiate or approve contracts or agreements with suppliers, distributors, federal or state agencies, or other organizational entities."
- "Preside over, or serve on, boards of directors, management committees, or other governing boards."

Public speaking is a routine part of the role. It averages 3.07 on a 1 to 5 frequency scale, and 19.51% of respondents said they speak to an audience every day, with another 7.79% weekly. Telephone conversations (4.92) and face-to-face discussions (4.9) are close to daily for nearly everyone. Communicating with people outside the organization rates 4.55 out of 5 for importance, almost as high as communicating inside it (4.75).

## What the research says

**Who a CEO talks with is linked to firm performance.** Bandiera, Prat, Hansen, and Sadun collected detailed diary data on how 1,114 CEOs in six countries spent their working time, then used machine learning to sort them into behavioral types. "Leaders" spent their time in high-level meetings involving multiple functions, while "managers" held more individual meetings with core functions. Firms that hired leaders performed better, and it took about three years for a new CEO to make a difference. The authors attribute the gap to mismatches between CEOs and firms, rather than to one type being better for every company <Cite k="bandiera2020ceo" />. Since meetings are spoken by definition, this is evidence that the structure of a CEO's conversations matters.

**Executives' voices carry information about the company.** Mayew and Venkatachalam analyzed audio from earnings conference calls using vocal emotion analysis software. When managers were questioned by analysts, the positive and negative emotion in their voices was informative about the firm's financial future, beyond the numbers and the words used. Analysts did not use this information when forecasting near-term earnings, though their stock recommendation changes did reflect positive (but not negative) vocal affect <Cite k="mayew2012voice" />. The measurements came from the moments when managers were being scrutinized by analysts, the part of a call where scripted language gives way to unscripted speech.

**Deeper voices are associated with running larger firms.** Mayew, Parsons, and Venkatachalam measured voice pitch in speech from 792 male CEOs of public companies. CEOs with deeper voices managed larger companies and, as a result, earned more. For the median CEO, an interquartile drop in pitch of 22.1 Hz was associated with managing a firm about $440 million larger and with $187 thousand more in annual pay. Deep-voiced CEOs also had longer tenures. The authors stress that this is a study of association <Cite k="mayew2013pitch" />. It covers men only and does not show that changing one's voice changes outcomes.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Composure under live questioning.** The earnings call research shows that listeners read emotion from an executive's voice when they are being questioned. Preparing for hard questions, and practicing answers aloud until they come out calm and specific, reduces the gap between what the CEO says and what the voice reveals.

**2. Convening across functions.** The CEO diary study links firm performance to time spent in multifunction, high-level meetings. Running those meetings well means framing the decision, drawing out disagreement between functions, and closing with a clear call.

**3. Speeches with one message.** Speaking at conventions, town halls, and official functions is a listed task. Strong executive speeches are built around one memorable idea that the audience can repeat afterward.

**4. Testimony and formal presentations.** Presenting to legislative committees and boards requires precise language, comfort with interruption, and a habit of answering the question asked before adding context.

**5. Negotiation.** Approving contracts with suppliers, distributors, and agencies often comes down to a few spoken exchanges where a calm pace and well-placed silence matter.

## How to practice

- **Mock Q&A sessions.** Ask a colleague to play a skeptical analyst or board member and record a 20-minute session. Listen back for where your pace speeds up or your voice tightens. The [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) helps you spot rushed answers.
- **One-message speech drafts.** Write a five-minute talk around a single sentence you want quoted. Run the draft through the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) and the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Teleprompter rehearsal.** For prepared remarks, rehearse with the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) at your natural pace, then deliver the same remarks from three bullet points.
- **Silence in negotiation.** Practice stating a position and then holding silence. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) builds tolerance for the pause that follows a firm number.
- **Handle the nerves directly.** Many executives still feel anxious before high-stakes talks. The [public speaking anxiety self-check](/tools/public-speaking-anxiety-self-check) gives a private read on where that anxiety comes from.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much public speaking do chief executives do?

A lot compared with other occupations. In O*NET data, 19.51% of chief executives report speaking to an audience every day and 7.79% weekly, and the occupation ranks 25 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score.

### Should executives try to lower their voice pitch?

The evidence does not support that. The voice pitch study found an association in male CEOs only and cannot show that changing pitch would change outcomes. Clear content and composure under questioning are more practical targets.

### Do investors really pay attention to how a CEO sounds?

Research on earnings calls suggests vocal emotion carries information about future performance, and analysts' stock recommendations reflected positive vocal cues. For the related finance side of the job, see the page on [financial managers](/professions/financial-managers).
