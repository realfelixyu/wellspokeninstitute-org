Paramedics talk in short, high-stakes bursts. O*NET® rates speaking importance for the occupation at 3.5 out of 5, and paramedics rank 521st of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. That middle ranking understates how much rides on a few conversations: questioning a confused patient, coordinating with police and fire crews on scene, and handing a patient over to an emergency department team that has a minute to listen.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Several O*NET task statements for paramedics are spoken. Paramedics "comfort and reassure patients," "coordinate work with other emergency medical team members or police or fire department personnel," and "observe, record, and report to physician the patient's condition or injury, the treatment provided, and reactions to drugs or treatment." They also "instruct emergency medical response team about emergency interventions to ensure correct application of procedures" and coordinate with treatment center staff to obtain vital statistics and medical history <Cite k="onet31" />.

The work context ratings show constant contact. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.9 out of 5, the importance of dealing with the public 4.4, and telephone use 4.17. Public speaking is rated 2.71, higher than for 74 of the 84 health care practitioner occupations in the data, and 12.9% of paramedics report doing it daily.

When a patient cannot give a history, paramedics piece one together from relatives, bystanders, medication bottles, and the scene itself, then compress it into a few sentences for the hospital. That compression is a speaking skill in its own right.

Paramedic speech also has to work in poor conditions:

- **On scene,** where there is noise, bystanders, and a patient who may be frightened, intoxicated, or barely conscious.
- **In the ambulance,** where radio or phone reports to the receiving hospital compete with sirens and treatment.
- **At handover,** where the receiving team is already moving the patient, attaching monitors, and starting its own assessment.

## What the research says

**Handover is a known weak point.** A literature review of ambulance-to-emergency department handover found eight studies that met its criteria. Three themes emerged: important information may be missed during handover; structured handovers with both written and verbal parts may improve information exchange; and shared education for paramedics and emergency staff may support teamwork and a common language. The authors noted large gaps in knowledge about the consequences of poor handover <Cite k="bost2010handover" />.

**Listeners retain only about half of a verbal handover.** In a study at two large emergency departments, researchers counted the information packets in each verbal handover and tested how much emergency staff could recall accurately. Before a structured format was introduced, staff retained 56.6% of the information. After the DeMIST format was introduced in one department, retention was 49.2%. The authors pointed out that receiving staff are naturally focused on their own initial assessment, which distracts them from listening, and called for communication training, clinical team leadership, and team discipline <Cite k="talbot2007handover" />. A structured script alone did not fix the problem.

**A co-designed format improved handovers.** Researchers filmed 137 handovers involving 291 staff, had paramedics and emergency clinicians review the footage together, and designed the IMIST-AMBO protocol: identification, mechanism or medical complaint, injuries or information relative to the complaint, signs and vitals, treatment and trends, then allergies, medications, background history, and other information. After training, handovers carried more information in a more consistent order, took less time, and drew fewer questions and repetitions from emergency staff <Cite k="iedema2012imist" />.

Together, the studies suggest that structure helps, but only when the receiving team is ready to listen and both sides have practiced the format.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. A fixed handover order.** Whatever format your service uses, deliver it in the same order every time so the listener knows what comes next. The IMIST-AMBO results show the payoff: fewer interruptions and repeated questions.

**2. Getting the room's attention first.** The Talbot study found that receiving staff split their attention. A brief "Are you ready for handover?" and waiting for eye contact from the team leader can make the next 60 seconds count.

**3. Reassuring while assessing.** The comfort-and-reassure task runs alongside assessment. Tell the patient your name, what you are doing, and what happens next in short sentences, especially when they are frightened.

**4. Clear instructions to crew and first responders.** When directing other responders, use names and specific actions: "Mike, take over compressions at the next cycle." Ask for a read-back of critical tasks.

**5. Radio economy.** Pre-arrival reports to the hospital need to be short and ordered, with the most important information first.

## How to practice

- **Drill your handover script.** Write a handover for a recent call using your service's format and time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Aim for a version you can deliver without notes.
- **Record and count fillers.** Record a mock handover and run the transcript through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter). Fillers in a handover eat time the receiving team does not have.
- **Practice under distraction.** Rehearse your handover while a colleague interrupts or starts a task, so you get used to pausing and resuming in order.
- **Pace your radio reports.** Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) on a recorded mock radio report. Slow down a little for noisy channels and say numbers distinctly.
- **Practice the pause between sections.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you leave a short gap between handover sections so the listener can catch up.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do ambulance handovers go wrong?

Reviews and observational studies point to missed information and to receiving staff being distracted by their own assessment. In one study, emergency staff accurately retained only about half of what paramedics said.

### What is IMIST-AMBO?

It is a structured handover format developed with paramedics and emergency clinicians. After it was introduced, handovers contained more information, were shorter, and drew fewer questions from emergency staff.

### Do paramedics speak in public?

More than most clinicians. O*NET rates public speaking at 2.71 out of 5 for paramedics, and 12.9% report doing it daily.
