Selling services depends heavily on speaking, mostly by phone and with people outside the company. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, sales representatives of services (outside advertising, insurance, financial services, and travel) rate speaking 3.88 out of 5 for importance. Phone calls score 4.88 out of 5 for frequency, and communicating with people outside the organization rates 4.79 for importance. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score for the occupation is 64.2 out of 100, list position 411 of 910 <Cite k="onet31" />. The people in this group sell things you can't hold, such as cleaning contracts, payroll processing, equipment servicing, or security monitoring, so the conversation is most of what the customer has to go on.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

O*NET's task list describes a sales cycle told in conversations. Representatives contact prospective or existing customers to discuss how services can meet their needs. They answer questions about services, prices, availability, and credit terms; quote prices, contract terms, and fulfillment dates; and negotiate prices or terms. They develop presentations to explain service specifications, and after a contract is signed they consult with clients to resolve problems <Cite k="onet31" />.

Face-to-face discussions score 4.43 out of 5, a little below the phone, and public speaking scores 1.83 <Cite k="onet31" />. Most of the selling happens one call or one meeting at a time.

Take Rosa, who sells commercial cleaning contracts. A dental practice's office manager picks up and says, "We already have someone." Rosa's next sentence decides whether the call continues. "That makes sense. Most practices I call do. When does your current agreement come up for renewal?" acknowledges the answer and asks for something the manager can easily provide.

## What the research says

**The first seconds of a call do a lot of work.** Humă and Stokoe analyzed 150 recorded business-to-business cold calls from three British companies that sell, lease, and service office printers and phone systems. Receptionists answered 117 of the 150 calls, so salespeople often had to ask to be put through before reaching anyone who could decide. The researchers found that how callers handled identification and the reason for calling shaped the result of the call. Salespeople also treated a first call differently from a later call to someone they had already spoken to <Cite k="huma2020anatomy" />.

**Salespeople make meeting requests easy to accept.** In a related analysis of 153 cold calls, Humă, Stokoe, and Sikveland identified two practices. Salespeople led up to the meeting request with accounts that brought the prospect along without revealing where the conversation was heading. They also minimized the imposition of the meeting to give the prospect fewer grounds to refuse <Cite k="huma2019coldcalls" />. In the transcripts, this shows up in phrases like "just wondering" and offers of a "quick chat." The study describes what salespeople did; it doesn't test whether these moves raise booking rates.

**Building on the customer's words is one way to build rapport.** Clark, Drew, and Pinch examined audio recordings of business-to-business field sales visits. Salespeople drew expressions of agreement from prospects by answering the prospect's own assessments with matching ones that built on them, and could extend these into longer runs of mutual agreement <Cite k="clark2003prospectrapport" />. If a prospect says the current vendor "never answers the phone," a response that picks up that point does more than a change of subject.

**The first number on the table pulls the deal toward it.** In three negotiation experiments, Galinsky and Mussweiler found that whichever side made the first offer got a better outcome, and first offers strongly predicted final prices. The advantage disappeared when the other side focused on information that ran against the offer, such as the opponent's alternatives or their own target <Cite k="galinsky2001firstoffers" />.

**A price range can work without souring the relationship.** Ames and Mason ran 5 studies, including live two-person negotiations, on range offers such as "$7,200 to $7,600." Some kinds of range offers got offer-makers better terms with no relationship cost, while others improved the relationship with no cost to the deal. The effects depended on how wide and how extreme the range was <Cite k="ames2015rangeoffers" />. Both negotiation studies used experiments with participants rather than recorded service sales, so they show what tends to happen, not a script that always works.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Stating the reason for the call early.** Say who you are and why you're calling in the first two sentences, whether you're talking to a receptionist or the decision-maker.
- **Asking needs questions.** Ask about the customer's current service, renewal date, and problems before describing your own. This matches the O*NET task of discussing how services can meet customers' needs.
- **Building on the customer's words.** When a prospect gives an opinion, respond to that point before moving to yours.
- **Quoting a price clearly.** Name the price or range, what it covers, and the contract length in one breath, then stop and let the customer respond.
- **Handling the post-sale problem call.** Repeat the problem back, say what you'll do and when, and call back when you said you would.

## How to practice

These drills are untested, and no study has checked whether they improve sales. They turn the patterns above into rehearsal.

**Two openings.** Write a 20-second opening for a receptionist ("Hi, this is Dana Ruiz from Northside Cleaning Services. I'm calling about your office's cleaning contract. Who looks after that?") and another for the decision-maker. Say each aloud to a partner who answers with a common brush-off. Note which sentence got you the next turn. The [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) can help you cut the decision-maker version down.

**Quote, then stop.** Invent a service and a price: weekly cleaning for a 3,000-square-foot office at $1,450 to $1,600 a month on a 12-month agreement. Say the quote to a partner and then stay silent until they speak. Try it as a single number, then as a range, and ask your partner which felt more open to discussion. Record yourself and use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) to check whether you sped up at the price.

**Echo the assessment.** Your partner plays a prospect and makes three complaints about their current provider. After each one, respond to their point first ("Missed visits are a real problem when patients are walking in at 8") before saying anything about your service.

**The follow-up call.** Your partner plays a client whose crew skipped the conference room two weeks in a row. Repeat the problem back, give a specific fix and date, and agree on when you'll check in. The [sales representatives](/professions/sales-representatives) page covers selling physical products.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cold calling still part of service sales?

For many service businesses it is. The O*NET task list includes contacting prospective customers to discuss how services can meet their needs <Cite k="onet31" />. BLS estimated 1,256,010 jobs in this occupation in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $69,990 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

### Should I give a single price or a range?

Either can work. Experiments on range offers found that some ranges improved terms for the person making the offer without hurting the relationship, depending on their width and extremity <Cite k="ames2015rangeoffers" />.

### What should I say first when a receptionist answers?

Say who you are and what the call is about in a sentence, then ask who handles it. In a study of 150 recorded business-to-business calls, receptionists answered 117 of them <Cite k="huma2020anatomy" />.
