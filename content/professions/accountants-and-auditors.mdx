Accounting has a reputation as a quiet profession, and the data partly agrees. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 3.38 out of 5 in importance for accountants and auditors, and the occupation ranks 489 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, at 60.6 <Cite k="onet31" />. Yet the same data shows phone calls averaging 4.72 and face-to-face discussions 4.48 on a 1 to 5 frequency scale, both near daily. Accountants talk constantly; they just do it in meetings, interviews, and calls rather than on stage. And some of those conversations, such as telling a client their revenue recognition will not pass audit, are among the hardest in business.

With 1,449,500 workers in May 2025, this is one of the largest professional occupations, and its median annual wage was $83,680 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Many O*NET task statements for accountants and auditors require explaining or questioning <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Report to management regarding the finances of establishment."
- "Confer with company officials about financial and regulatory matters."
- "Examine records and interview workers to ensure recording of transactions and compliance with laws and regulations."
- "Report to management about asset utilization and audit results, and recommend changes in operations and financial activities."
- "Advise clients in areas such as compensation, employee health care benefits, the design of accounting or data processing systems, or long-range tax or estate plans."
- "Represent clients before taxing authorities and provide support during litigation involving financial issues."

Public speaking averages 2.1 on the frequency scale. No respondents reported presenting every day, and 6.9% reported doing so weekly. Communicating with people inside the organization rates 4.07 out of 5 for importance and with people outside it 3.93, reflecting the mix of internal accountants and external auditors and tax advisers in this category.

## What the research says

**Audits involve real negotiations with client management.** Gibbins, Salterio, and Webb developed a model of auditor-client negotiation over accounting issues, drawing on the negotiation literature and interviews with senior practitioners. They then used a questionnaire to describe real negotiations chosen by 93 experienced audit partners, documenting the elements of those negotiations and the contextual features associated with them <Cite k="gibbins2001negotiation" />. For auditors, the practical implication is that resolving accounting disagreements is a negotiation skill as much as a technical one.

**Employers want communication skills that programs do not fully build.** Kavanagh and Drennan studied the views of accounting students and employers. Students were becoming aware that employers expect communication, analytical, professional, and teamwork skills. Employers still wanted strong basic accounting and analytical ability, but they also wanted business awareness. Both groups reported that many essential non-technical skills were not being developed sufficiently in university accounting programs <Cite k="kavanagh2008skills" />.

**Anxiety about speaking in interviews is persistent.** Gardner, Milne, Stringer, and Whiting studied communication apprehension among accounting students in New Zealand after a curriculum redesign that added more communication demands. Among final-year students, apprehension declined most for those who started with high levels. The authors found no strong link between apprehension and academic progress or performance. One concern stood out: students' anxiety about communicating in interviews was not reduced <Cite k="gardner2005apprehension" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Negotiating accounting issues.** Disagreements over estimates, reserves, or revenue timing are worked out in conversation with client management. Auditors need to state their position clearly, understand the client's reasoning, and know which points are open and which are not.

**2. Interviewing staff about transactions.** Interviewing workers to confirm how transactions are recorded is a fact-finding skill. Neutral, specific questions ("Who approves this before it is paid?") produce better evidence than leading ones.

**3. Explaining numbers to non-accountants.** Reporting to management means translating financial results into business consequences. The best explanations pick one or two numbers that matter and say what they imply for decisions.

**4. Delivering findings and recommendations.** Presenting audit results and recommending changes can land as criticism. Leading with the risk to the business, rather than the error, keeps managers focused on the fix.

**5. Composure in high-stakes settings.** Representing clients before taxing authorities, or answering questions in a dispute, calls for a calm voice, short answers, and the discipline to say "I will confirm that" instead of guessing.

## How to practice

- **Two-minute results briefing.** Take a recent set of results and explain them aloud in two minutes to someone outside finance. Time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) and check the script with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Negotiation role-play.** Ask a colleague to play a CFO who disagrees with a proposed adjustment. Practice stating your position, asking for their reasoning, and summarizing where you agree.
- **Interview practice.** Since apprehension about interviews appears especially persistent, rehearse both sides. Use the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) to practice job interview answers, and write a short question list for your next staff interview at a client.
- **Check your anxiety pattern.** If presenting findings makes you tense, the [public speaking anxiety self-check](/tools/public-speaking-anxiety-self-check) offers a private read on where that tension comes from.
- **Cut filler in client calls.** Record a practice explanation of a technical issue and run the transcript through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do accountants need good speaking skills?

Yes, though mostly for meetings and calls rather than presentations. Accountants and auditors rate phone calls at 4.72 and face-to-face discussions at 4.48 out of 5 for frequency, and research shows employers expect strong communication skills from graduates.

### Do auditors really negotiate with clients?

They do. Research by Gibbins, Salterio, and Webb documented real auditor-client negotiations over financial reporting, described by 93 experienced audit partners.

### Is communication anxiety common among accounting students?

Research on accounting students found that apprehension declined in the final year of a communication-heavy program, especially for the most anxious students, but anxiety about interviews did not. For a finance role with heavier speaking demands, see [financial managers](/professions/financial-managers).
