Bookkeeping, accounting, and auditing clerks need moderate speaking skills, and most of what they say involves figures that are easy to mishear. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 3.12 out of 5 for importance in this occupation, while oral comprehension, understanding what others say, rates 4.00. Phone calls score 4.79 out of 5 for frequency and face-to-face discussions 4.55. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 53.4 out of 100, list position 621 of 910 <Cite k="onet31" />. The talk is rarely long, but a wrong digit said or heard can cost real money.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Most O*NET tasks for this occupation happen at a screen: coding documents, posting entries, matching order forms to invoices. Several involve talking. Clerks look up financial records to answer questions about specific accounts, note and report discrepancies they find, answer phones as part of general office duties, and monitor loans and accounts to make sure payments are up to date. Some prepare bank deposits by gathering figures from cashiers <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking scores just 1.89 out of 5 <Cite k="onet31" />, so presentations are rare.

A typical call: a supplier's office manager says, "We still haven't been paid on invoice 40718." The clerk finds it and answers with the details in a fixed order: "Invoice 40718, dated March 3, for $1,264.50. It's approved and scheduled in Friday's payment run." The caller can check each item against their own records.

A typical discrepancy report is internal. The bank statement shows $2,418.60 and the ledger shows $2,481.60. Telling a supervisor "the bank rec is off" starts a search. Saying "The cash account is $63 higher in the ledger than at the bank. The 18 and the 81 look swapped on the March 14 deposit. Can I correct the entry?" gives them the amount, the likely cause, and a decision to make.

## What the research says

There is little research on clerks' talk specifically, so the studies below come from accountancy, laboratory medicine, and debt collection, where the same kinds of spoken exchanges occur.

**Accounting employers value oral skills and find them lacking in new graduates.** Gray asked chartered accountancy professionals in New Zealand how important 27 oral communication skills were for new graduates, and how often employers found those skills <Cite k="gray2010accountancyoral" />. A follow-up study by Gray and Murray, which added interviews, found that employers rated all the oral skills somewhat important and some extremely important. They also reported seldom finding the level they needed in new university graduates <Cite k="gray2011distinguishing" />. Both studies concern accountants rather than clerks, and both rely on employers' opinions.

**Reading back a phoned result catches errors.** At three health care organizations, Barenfanger and colleagues required people receiving critical laboratory results by phone to repeat the message. Across 822 outgoing calls, read-back detected 29 errors. Asking for and hearing the repetition took an average of 12.8 seconds per call <Cite k="barenfanger2004readback" />. Those calls carried test results, not account balances, but phoned numbers can be misheard in either setting.

**Payment follow-up calls pull in two directions.** Harrington studied the training materials for collections agents at a UK utilities company's call center. Agents were told to perform potentially face-threatening acts to collect the debt while also building rapport with the debtor, a style summed up as "helping you to pay us." Harrington suggests this may create tension between collectors and debtors <Cite k="harrington2018debtcollection" />. A clerk chasing a late customer payment faces a milder version of the same balance.

**Requests to change payment details need checking through another channel.** The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center counted 305,033 business email compromise incidents and $55,499,915,582 in exposed losses, domestic and international, from October 2013 to December 2023. Its first prevention tip is to use secondary channels or two-factor authentication to verify requests to change account information <Cite k="ic32024bec" />. For a clerk, that can mean calling the vendor at a phone number already on file before updating bank details.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Saying figures in a fixed order.** Give the reference number, date, and amount every time, and say the cents. This supports the O*NET task of answering questions about specific accounts.
- **Reading back and asking for read-backs.** Repeat any number you are given, and ask the other person to repeat any number you give them.
- **Reporting a discrepancy briefly.** State the amount, where it appears, what you checked, and what you need from the listener.
- **Following up on overdue accounts.** Be clear about the amount and the date without blaming the customer.
- **Verifying by voice.** Confirm changes to payment details by calling a known number, and ask a question only the real contact could answer.

## How to practice

None of these drills has been tested. They borrow the read-back and verification steps from the sources above.

**Number relay.** A partner writes ten invented invoice numbers and amounts, such as "40718, $1,264.50," and reads them to you at normal speed. Write each one down and read it back. Count your errors, then swap roles. Next, try reading long numbers in groups of three digits and see whether the error count changes. If you lose a number, [ask for it again](/learn/how-to-ask-someone-to-repeat-what-you-missed) by naming the part you missed.

**The 30-second discrepancy.** Invent a small reconciliation gap and explain it aloud to a colleague in 30 seconds or less: the amount, where it shows up, the likely cause, and the decision you need. Ask them what they would do next. If they can't answer, the report was missing something.

**The overdue reminder call.** One person plays a customer whose $890 payment is 45 days late and who says, "I thought we paid that." Practice opening with the invoice details, then asking a question: "Could you check whether it went out under a different reference?" Try it once firmly and once warmly, and ask the customer which version made them more likely to pay.

**The callback check.** A partner sends an invented email from a "vendor" asking you to update their bank account. Practice the call to the number on file: "Hi, this is Sam in accounts payable. We received a request to change your remittance details and want to confirm it came from you."

## Frequently asked questions

### Do bookkeeping clerks need strong speaking skills?

They need clear, accurate speech more than polished presentation. O*NET data rates oral comprehension at 4.00 out of 5 and speaking at 3.12 for this occupation <Cite k="onet31" />. BLS estimated 1,373,680 jobs in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $50,670 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

### How should I read a long number over the phone?

Say it in short groups at an even pace, and ask the listener to read it back. In a hospital laboratory study, read-back caught 29 errors across 822 calls <Cite k="barenfanger2004readback" />.

### What should I do if a vendor emails new bank details?

Confirm the request through a separate channel, such as a call to a number you already have. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center recommends verifying changes to account information this way <Cite k="ic32024bec" />.
