Stockers and order fillers do not need to give speeches, but they do need to talk clearly in short bursts. A shopper stops you mid-aisle to ask about a size. A delivery comes in short and the lead needs to know before the truck leaves. A coworker calls out a bin number you did not catch. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 2.75 out of 5 for importance in this job, and face-to-face discussions score 4.25 out of 5 for frequency. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 43.7 out of 100, which ranks the occupation 847th of 910 <Cite k="onet31" />. Low overall demand still leaves several spoken moments in a shift where a missed detail costs time or stock.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Several O*NET task statements for the occupation involve talk. Workers "answer customers' questions about merchandise and advise customers on merchandise selection." They "provide assistance or direction to other stockroom, warehouse, or storage yard workers." They examine stock for wear or defects, "reporting any damage to supervisors," and they read orders for catalog numbers, sizes, colors and quantities <Cite k="onet31" />.

The setting changes the conversation. In a store, the person filling a shelf may be the nearest employee when a shopper has a question. In a warehouse, the listeners are coworkers, leads and drivers, often over the noise of equipment and sometimes across languages.

BLS estimated 2,833,810 stocker and order filler jobs in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $37,330 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

**Speaking first in the aisle.** Tobias Otterbring and colleagues combined exit interviews with shoppers, a field study in stores, online experiments and mystery-shopper data. In retail research, they note, proactivity has been defined as employees starting face-to-face contact with customers on the store floor. Employee proactivity predicted how much customers bought and how satisfied they were, and the effect ran through how warm and competent customers judged the employee to be. A highly proactive approach could make up for a delay before contact <Cite k="otterbring2023proactivity" />. The research did not single out stockers, but a stocker filling a shelf meets the same moment when a shopper hesitates nearby.

**Feedback on picks.** At a foodservice distributor in the southeastern United States, Samuel Berger and Timothy Ludwig tracked 132 selectors before and after the company introduced a voice-assisted selecting tool that gave immediate feedback when an error occurred. Errors fell from 2.44 to 0.94 per 1,000 cases picked, and the largest gains came among selectors who had made the most errors at baseline. The study compared groups that were not randomly assigned <Cite k="berger2007voicepicking" />. The system was a machine talking to workers, not workers talking to each other, but it suggests that catching a mismatch at the moment it happens can improve picking accuracy.

**Many languages on one floor.** Dorte Lønsmann and Kamilla Kraft compared a logistics workplace and a construction workplace. Multilingual practices were essential to the flow of everyday work on the floor. The official language requirements, however, matched the repertoires of higher-status employees, so the multilingual skills of the people doing the floor work counted for less <Cite k="lonsmann2018logisticslanguage" />.

**Safety views may differ between warehouses.** In a case study at a large Dutch logistics provider, Nienke Hofstra and colleagues used interviews and questionnaires to see which parts of safety culture employees thought mattered. Employees assigned safety different importance, which the authors read as a possible sign of sub-cultures across warehouses <Cite k="hofstra2018warehousesafety" />.

**Workers' responses to the rate.** Two years of ethnographic research by EunJeong Cheon and Ingrid Erickson in Amazon fulfillment centers described how workers responded to managers' interventions and imposed productivity rates under labor-tracking systems. In the centers they studied, workers' responses included resisting or working around the rates <Cite k="cheon2025fulfillment" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Answering a shopper in one breath.** Say whether you have it, where it is, or how long a check will take: "We're out of the 32-ounce on the shelf. I can check the back. It'll take about five minutes." Then do what you said.

**Reporting damage or a short shipment.** Name what, how many and where it is now. "Tuesday's dairy delivery came in four cases short on 2% milk. The invoice says 20, we got 16. The driver's still at door 3."

**Confirming a pick or an instruction.** Repeat numbers back. "Aisle 14, bin C, six units, got it." If a coworker or lead speaks another language, slow down, point to the label and use the item number rather than a description.

**Telling a lead you need help.** A blocked aisle or an overloaded pallet is easier to fix early. Say what the problem is and what you need: "The top pallet in 9 is leaning. I need a forklift driver before I pull from it."

## How to practice

These drills have not been tested in a study. They are built from the tasks above.

**Aisle questions.** Write ten shopper questions on cards, such as "Do you have this in a larger size?" or "Where's the gluten-free pasta?" A partner reads one while you are holding a box. Answer in one or two sentences that say what you will do. If you need to check, give a time. Switch after five cards.

**Damage report.** Set out three items and an invoice that lists four. Report the difference to a partner playing the lead in three parts: what was expected, what arrived and where the shipment is now. The lead repeats it back. If the lead's version is missing a part, try again.

**Read-back pick.** One person reads a pick list of five lines with aisle, bin and quantity. The other repeats each line before "picking" it. Swap roles, then add background noise from a phone speaker and try again. Notice which details get lost first. The Institute's guide on [asking a follow-up question](/learn/how-to-ask-a-follow-up-question) has wording for checking the one you missed.

Retail salespersons handle many of the same shopper questions. See the Institute's page on [retail salespersons](/professions/retail-salespersons).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do stockers need customer service skills?

Often, yes. O*NET lists answering customers' questions about merchandise as a core task for the occupation <Cite k="onet31" />. In stores, a short, direct answer that says what you will do next covers most of it.

### What should I say when I don't know where an item is?

Say so, then offer the next step. "I'm not sure. Let me ask the department lead" is better than a guess that sends a shopper to the wrong aisle.

### How do I report a short or damaged shipment?

Give the expected count, the actual count, and where the shipment is now. If the driver is still there, say so first, since that is the detail the lead can act on immediately.
