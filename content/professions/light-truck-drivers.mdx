Light truck drivers need to report delays to dispatch, ask recipients about an authorized handoff and give directions that another driver can actually use. O*NET® rates speaking importance at 3.12 out of 5 and telephone conversations at 4.24 for frequency. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 49 out of 100, shared by the occupations in positions 691 through 695 of 910. A message to dispatch belongs before driving or after parking safely. NIOSH advises employers to require workers to pull over somewhere safe to make or receive a call <Cite k="onet31,niosh2026workdriving" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Suppose Remy, a fictional delivery driver, arrives at a clinic with an order that requires a signature from an authorized recipient. The address matches. A sign at the public entrance directs deliveries to a rear entrance, where nobody answers. That gives Remy several things to report, including something he does not know. He can say where he tried, how he followed the posted instructions and that the shipment is still with him. The fact that no one answered the receiving door tells him nothing about whether the entire clinic is closed.

O*NET lists checking the load against shipping papers, following verbal or written directions and reporting delays, accidents or vehicle problems. A report from the receiving door can affect the rest of a round. Dispatch may need to contact the customer, approve a further attempt or update the next stop. Being specific about what remains in the driver's possession gives the next person a starting point <Cite k="onet31" />.

## What the research says

**Two unsuccessful stops can look alike in a route log.** Researchers rode along on parcel routes in central London and captured 25 rounds. In one of their field observations, a recipient at a pub declined to sign because they did not want responsibility for the delivery. On another occasion, the driver and observer waited about 20 minutes for a shop manager to open up. In the first case, someone was present. In the second, the person the driver needed had yet to arrive. Neither observation tells us how often those situations occur. Both show why “customer unavailable” can leave important information out <Cite k="bates2018parcelrounds" />.

**The street address is only the start of an office delivery.** In a 62-story Seattle office building, Kim and colleagues documented 31 delivery-worker observations. Many drivers had to check in with a security guard to obtain access to the freight elevator. Some had advance clearance. The researchers found that office layouts and delivery policies varied across the building. A driver unfamiliar with the site could reach the correct floor and still have to establish where a particular office accepted deliveries. This was one building with its own arrangements, not a rule for every Seattle office <Cite k="kim2018seattledeliveries" />.

**Knowing the regular recipient can change the question.** In 34 online interviews with parcel couriers in Poland, some drivers described contacting customers in advance to arrange deliveries, particularly for bulky parcels. They also described balancing those arrangements against carrier rules and performance expectations. An arrangement one courier makes on a familiar route doesn't authorize a colleague to leave freight in the same place. A replacement driver needs the current delivery instructions and the receiving party's authorization as required by the carrier <Cite k="miszczynski2024couriers" />.

**A customer can identify their entrance without knowing where a van can safely stop.** Belgian research compared parcel drivers with platform food couriers. The study included interviews with 10 parcel drivers and 12 stakeholders or experts, and a focus group with 16 food couriers. The parcel drivers drew attention to the difficulties of stopping near customers and reentering traffic where loading space was scarce. The authors treated van drivers and food couriers as distinct groups. For a van driver, a call to clarify an address still has to wait until it can be made safely <Cite k="deroeck2026vandelivery,niosh2026workdriving" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Use the same stop identifier as dispatch.** Remy's message might begin, “I'm parked at stop 14, Elm Clinic, rear receiving door.” The stop number distinguishes the order from another clinic with a similar name. The door matters because someone answering the main phone might otherwise send him back to the place he's already tried. Use the carrier's approved channel for information about the customer or shipment.

**Report only what you could establish.** “No one answered at the receiving door” gives dispatch room to ask whether there's another authorized contact. “They refused the delivery” describes a different event. If a person actually declined to sign, tell dispatch that and explain what they said without adding a motive. The London field study illustrates how much the difference between those accounts can matter <Cite k="bates2018parcelrounds" />.

**Request the next decision.** After explaining the problem, Remy can add, “I still have the parcel. Who should confirm whether I make another attempt?” That makes clear that a question is outstanding. He can record the authorized response using the carrier's procedure. If nobody has yet approved a change, the next driver should be able to tell that from the handoff.

## How to practice

Use a chair and a fictional delivery card, away from any vehicle. Write a stop number, an entrance and a signature requirement. A partner plays a receptionist who tells the driver that this entrance does not receive parcels. Give dispatch a short account of the attempt. Have the listener write down where the driver went, whether the parcel changed hands and who is checking the next step. Compare the note with the card. If it says “delivered to receptionist,” replay the one sentence that gave that impression.

For a second round, change the receptionist's reply to “We do take deliveries, but I can't sign for that shipment.” Keep the address and order the same. Notice how few words are needed to make the two attempts distinguishable to dispatch. These untested exercises practice handoffs only. Use your employer's delivery, parking and driver-safety procedures on the road.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if someone at the address takes parcels for another tenant?

Check the carrier's instructions and the shipment's acceptance requirements. A person able to point out the correct office may have no authority to accept that office's delivery.

### Should I answer dispatch while moving just to say I'll call back?

NIOSH recommends employers require workers to pull over in a safe location to make or receive calls. Plan the return call for when you are safely parked <Cite k="niosh2026workdriving" />.

### Who is included in the U.S. employment estimate?

O*NET describes light vehicles such as trucks or vans below 26,001 pounds gross vehicle weight used primarily to pick up and deliver merchandise or packages. BLS estimated 983,300 U.S. jobs and a median annual wage of $44,860 for Light Truck Drivers in May 2025 <Cite k="onet31,blsoews2025" />.
