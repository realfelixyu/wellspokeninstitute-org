Insurance is sold almost entirely through conversation, because the product is a promise that customers cannot see or test before buying. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 3.88 out of 5 in importance for insurance sales agents, and the occupation ranks 306 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, with a score of 68 <Cite k="onet31" />. One rating sets this job apart. Dealing with external customers or the public rates 4.97 out of 5 for importance, and only 2 of the 910 occupations in our dataset rate it higher. Agents spend their days explaining coverage, asking personal questions about health and finances, and hearing "no" more often than "yes."

In May 2025 there were 479,100 insurance sales agents, and the median annual wage was $62,280 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for insurance agents describe a sequence of explaining, interviewing, and following up <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Explain features, advantages, and disadvantages of various policies to promote sale of insurance plans."
- "Interview prospective clients to obtain data about their financial resources and needs, the physical condition of the person or property to be insured, and to discuss any existing coverage."
- "Call on policyholders to deliver and explain policy, to analyze insurance program and suggest additions or changes, or to change beneficiaries."
- "Confer with clients to obtain and provide information when claims are made on a policy."
- "Seek out new clients and develop clientele by networking to find new customers and generate lists of prospective clients."

Telephone conversations average 5.0 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale, the maximum, and face-to-face discussions average 4.93. Formal public speaking is rare, averaging 2.22, with 1.52% of agents presenting weekly and none daily. The work happens one client at a time.

## What the research says

**Relationship quality drives future sales, and specific behaviors build it.** Crosby, Evans, and Cowles studied salespeople who act as "relationship managers" for complex services, a category that includes many insurance products. They found that future sales opportunities depended mostly on relationship quality, meaning the customer's trust and satisfaction, while the ability to convert those opportunities into sales depended more on the salesperson's similarity to the customer and perceived expertise. Relational selling behaviors, including cooperative intentions, mutual disclosure, and intensive follow-up contact, generally produced a strong buyer-seller bond <Cite k="crosby1990relationship" />.

**What agents say can serve their commission more than the client.** Anagol, Cole, and Sarkar ran a series of field experiments to evaluate the advice that life insurance agents in India gave to prospective customers. Agents overwhelmingly recommended unsuitable, strictly dominated products that paid them high commissions, catered to customers' mistaken beliefs, and focused on the premium amount rather than how much coverage the customer needed. When disclosure of commissions was required for one product, agents shifted to recommending other high-commission products without that requirement. A follow-up survey suggested that both incentives and limited product knowledge explained the poor advice <Cite k="anagol2017insurance" />. The study covers one market, but it is a clear warning about how product explanations can mislead.

**How agents explain setbacks predicts sales and staying power.** Seligman and Schulman measured life insurance agents' explanatory style, the way people explain bad events to themselves. Among 94 experienced agents, those who scored in the more optimistic half sold 37% more insurance in their first 2 years than those in the less optimistic half. In a prospective study of 103 newly hired agents, those in the more optimistic half when hired stayed in the job at twice the rate and sold more <Cite k="seligman1986explanatory" />. Rejection is built into prospecting calls, and how an agent interprets each "no" shapes whether they make the next call.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Plain, balanced policy explanations.** The task list asks agents to explain "advantages and disadvantages" of policies. Naming the limits of a product out loud, and matching coverage to the client's actual need, is the honest response to the problems the India study documented, and it builds the trust that the relationship research links to future sales.

**2. Sensitive interviewing.** Asking about income, health, and existing coverage requires a calm explanation of why each question matters, followed by attentive listening without judgment.

**3. Follow-up conversations.** Calling on policyholders to review their coverage is the "intensive follow-up contact" that research associates with strong client relationships. These calls work best when they start with the client's life changes, not a new product.

**4. Supportive claims conversations.** When a claim is made, the client is often dealing with a loss. Clear information about the process, delivered with warmth, is where long-term loyalty is decided.

**5. Recovering from rejection.** Prospecting means hearing "no" constantly. Treating each rejection as specific and temporary, rather than personal, is the mindset the explanatory style research links to staying in the job.

## How to practice

- **Explain one policy in two minutes.** Script an explanation of your most common policy that covers what it does, what it does not do, and who it fits. Check the length with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) and the clarity with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Practice the sensitive questions.** Role-play a needs interview with a colleague. Before each personal question, add one sentence explaining why you need the answer.
- **Slow down on the phone.** Phone calls are constant in this job. Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) to check that you are not rushing through premiums and exclusions.
- **Prospecting opener.** Build a short, honest introduction with the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) for networking and referral calls.
- **Rejection debrief.** After a day of calls, write down each "no" and one specific, changeable reason for it. Over time this builds the habit of seeing setbacks as situational.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do insurance agents need to be good public speakers?

Not in the usual sense. Public speaking averages 2.22 out of 5 for frequency in O*NET data, while phone calls rate 5.0 and face-to-face discussions 4.93. Conversation skills matter far more than presentation skills.

### Does optimism really affect insurance sales?

In a classic study of life insurance agents, those with a more optimistic explanatory style sold 37% more in their first 2 years and new hires in that group stayed at twice the rate. The studies are correlational and decades old, but the pattern fits a job built on frequent rejection.

### What do clients want most from an insurance agent conversation?

Research on relationship selling points to trust and satisfaction as the basis for future business, supported by honest disclosure and regular follow-up. For a related advisory role, see [personal financial advisors](/professions/financial-advisors).
