Police work runs on conversation. Officers take reports, question witnesses, explain a traffic stop, calm an argument, and testify in court. O*NET® rates oral comprehension for police and sheriff's patrol officers at 4.5 out of 5 in importance and oral expression at 4.25, and the occupation ranks 197th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. How officers speak in routine encounters shapes whether the public sees them as fair, and research suggests it also shapes how often encounters escalate.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for patrol officers include a long list of spoken duties. Officers "interview principals and eyewitnesses" at crime and accident scenes, "testify in court to present evidence or act as witness in traffic and criminal cases," "question individuals entering secured areas to determine their business," and "inform citizens of community services and recommend options to facilitate longer-term problem resolution" <Cite k="onet31" />. The first task listed, providing for public safety, includes "promoting good community relations." Some officers also conduct community programs on topics such as drugs and violence.

The work context ratings reflect constant contact. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.87 out of 5 and telephone use 4.62. The importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.76, and communicating with people outside the organization 4.71. Public speaking is rated 2.65, with 13.16% of officers reporting it daily and 20.72% weekly, which fits court testimony, roll-call briefings, and community meetings.

Police conversations have unusual constraints:

- **The listener may not want to be there.** Stops, arrests, and investigations involve people who did not choose the encounter.
- **Authority changes how words land.** A request from an armed officer may be heard very differently from the same words spoken by anyone else.
- **Many encounters are recorded.** Body-worn cameras mean an officer's words can be reviewed later, by supervisors, courts, and researchers.

## What the research says

**Procedurally just dialogue builds legitimacy.** A Campbell Collaboration systematic review synthesized 30 studies from Australia, the United States, and England on police interventions that use procedural justice: giving citizens a voice, being neutral, treating people with dignity and respect, and conveying trustworthy motives. Police-led interventions aimed at legitimacy had a significant impact on public satisfaction with and confidence in the police, and were associated with increased compliance and cooperation. Effects on reoffending were minor <Cite k="mazerolle2013legitimacy" />.

**Officer language differs by community member's race.** Researchers used computational linguistics to analyze officer language in body-worn camera footage from 981 routine traffic stops in Oakland, California. Officers spoke with consistently less respect toward Black than toward white community members, even after controlling for the officer's race, the severity of the infraction, the location, and the outcome of the stop. The authors noted implications for procedural justice and police-community trust <Cite k="voigt2017respect" />. The finding concerns word choice in everyday stops, not use of force, which is what makes it directly relevant to speaking skills.

**De-escalation training reduced force in a randomized trial.** The Louisville Metro Police Department implemented the Integrating Communications, Assessment, and Tactics (ICAT) de-escalation training developed by the Police Executive Research Forum. Using a stepped-wedge randomized design, researchers found statistically significant reductions after training in use-of-force incidents (28.1%), citizen injuries (26.3%), and officer injuries (36.0%). The authors called for complementary policies, supervision, and community involvement to sustain the changes <Cite k="engel2022deescalation" />. This is one department, but the authors note that no earlier research had shown that de-escalation training reduces force in the field.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Explaining the reason for the stop early.** Procedural justice emphasizes trustworthy motives and transparency. Telling a driver why they were stopped in the first sentence or two removes a source of suspicion.

**2. Giving people a voice.** Asking "Is there anything you want me to know?" before making a decision costs little and addresses one of the four procedural justice components directly.

**3. Respectful language as a default.** In the body camera study, apologizing, expressing gratitude, offering reassurance ("no problem"), and showing concern for the person's safety ("drive safe") all marked respectful speech, while informal titles marked the opposite. Using the respectful forms consistently with everyone is a habit that can be practiced.

**4. De-escalation phrases you can say under stress.** The ICAT program puts communication first in its name. Practice slowing your pace, lowering your volume, asking open questions, and offering clear choices until those phrases come out automatically.

**5. Clear, factual testimony.** In court, answer the question asked, describe what you observed rather than conclusions, and correct yourself immediately if you misspeak.

## How to practice

- **Script your stop opening.** Write the first three sentences you use at a traffic stop, including your name, agency, and the reason for the stop. Run them through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Review your own footage.** If your agency allows it, review a few body camera recordings of routine encounters and note greetings, explanations, and closings.
- **Practice slow, low delivery.** Record yourself giving instructions in a calm voice and check your rate with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator), then practice the slower version until it feels normal.
- **Prepare for testimony.** Practice short, factual answers to likely questions. The [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) can help keep answers concise.
- **Rehearse a community talk.** For community programs, draft a talk, time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator), and practice with the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is procedural justice in policing?

It refers to four elements of how officers treat people: giving them a voice, being neutral, treating them with dignity and respect, and conveying trustworthy motives. A systematic review of 30 studies found that interventions using these principles raised public satisfaction and cooperation.

### Does de-escalation training work?

In a stepped-wedge randomized trial in Louisville, de-escalation training was followed by 28.1% fewer use-of-force incidents and fewer injuries to citizens and officers. More trials in other departments are needed.

### How important is speaking for police officers?

O*NET rates speaking importance at 3.88 out of 5 and oral comprehension at 4.5. Officers rank 197th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score.
