Paralegals rarely give speeches, but they talk with clients, witnesses, court staff, and other professionals all day, and much of the factual record in a case starts with those conversations. O\*NET® data rate speaking 3.62 out of 5 for importance for paralegals and legal assistants, the "quite important" band, and the occupation ranks 403rd of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking is rated only 1.77 out of 5, while face-to-face discussion is rated 4.69 and telephone use 4.54. The most useful spoken skill for the job is interviewing: asking questions that get people to tell what they know.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list asks paralegals to "meet with clients and other professionals to discuss details of cases" and to investigate the facts and law of cases. Supplemental tasks include calling witnesses to testify at hearings, directing law office activity such as the delivery of subpoenas, and helping arbitrate disputes and run real estate closings <Cite k="onet31" />.

Communicating with people outside the organization is rated 4.34 out of 5 for importance, nearly the same as communicating inside it. In practice that means intake calls with new clients, follow-up calls to gather documents, conversations with witnesses, and coordination with court clerks and opposing counsel's staff. Only 2.35% of surveyed paralegals report public speaking daily.

The interviewing research below comes from police and laboratory settings, not law offices. The same skills apply to fact-gathering interviews with clients and witnesses.

## What the research says

**Trained interviewers got much more information from real witnesses.** Fisher, Geiselman, and Amador trained seven experienced detectives in the Cognitive Interview, a technique based on principles of memory retrieval and communication, and compared them with nine untrained detectives. All detectives recorded interviews with real crime victims and witnesses before and after training. Trained detectives drew out 47% more information after training than before, and 63% more than untrained detectives. <Cite k="fisher1989cognitive" />.

**The gain holds across 25 years of research.** Memon, Meissner, and Fraser meta-analyzed 46 published articles on the Cognitive Interview. Compared with control interviews, it produced a large, significant increase in correct details and a small increase in errors, with no difference in the rate of confabulated details. It appeared to help older adult witnesses even more than younger adults <Cite k="memon2010cognitive" />.

**Real interviewers rarely ask open questions.** Snook, Luther, Quinlan, and Milne coded 80 transcripts of Canadian police interviews with suspects and accused persons. On average, fewer than 1% of questions were open-ended, while closed yes-or-no questions made up about 40% and probing questions about 30%. Open-ended questions drew the longest answers. Interviewers asked for a free narrative in only about 14% of interviews, and the "80-20 rule," which says the interviewee should do about 80% of the talking, was broken in every interview <Cite k="snook2012talk" />.

These settings differ from a law office. Snook's interviews involved suspects rather than cooperative witnesses, and most studies in the meta-analysis were laboratory experiments, so the exact gains for a paralegal's client interview are unknown. Together, though, the studies show a gap between what works and what interviewers do by default. Interviewers who stay quiet, invite a full account, and save specific questions for later get more accurate detail.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Open questions.** Prompts such as "Tell me what happened from the beginning" or "Describe the meeting" invite a full account. Closed questions are useful for confirming details after the story is out.
- **Letting people talk.** The 80-20 guideline is a useful check: if you are talking more than the client or witness, the interview is collecting less than it could.
- **Not interrupting.** Let the person finish, then pause before your next question so they have room to add detail.
- **Summarizing back.** Restating what you heard lets the person correct errors and shows you were listening, which matters to clients who are anxious about their case.
- **Clear phone manner.** Many client contacts are by phone. A calm greeting, your name and role, the purpose of the call, and a recap of next steps keep calls efficient and professional.

## How to practice

1. **Rewrite your intake script.** Look at the questions on your firm's intake form and add one open prompt at the top, such as "In your own words, tell me what brings you here."
2. **Count your questions.** Record a practice interview with a colleague playing a client. Count how many of your questions were open, closed, or leading, and aim to raise the share of open ones.
3. **Use the pause.** After the client finishes an answer, wait a few seconds before speaking. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) builds comfort with silence.
4. **Practice the summary.** At the end of each section of an interview, summarize the key facts in two or three sentences and ask, "Did I get that right?"
5. **Plan explanations of procedure.** Clients often ask what happens next. Write a plain explanation of a common step, such as discovery or a hearing, and test it with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) before saying it aloud.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for paralegals?

Quite important. O\*NET rates speaking 3.62 out of 5 for paralegals and legal assistants, and face-to-face and phone conversations are rated close to daily.

### What is the Cognitive Interview?

It is an interviewing method built on research about memory and communication. In a field test with real victims and witnesses, detectives trained in it drew out substantially more information than untrained detectives.

### Why are open questions better for fact-finding?

In a study of real police interviews, open-ended questions produced the longest answers. They let the person give a full account before the interviewer narrows in on specific details.
