Software development is a middle-of-the-pack speaking job. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, raters give speaking an importance of 3.12 out of 5 for software developers, and the occupation ranks 553 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score (57.8 of 100) <Cite k="onet31" />. That is lower than most business and sales roles. The catch is that the talk developers do is concentrated at the points where projects succeed or fail: working out what users need, agreeing on a design, and getting unstuck when the only person who knows the answer is a colleague.

The occupation is large. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 1,687,890 software developers in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $135,980 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list for software developers is mostly about analysis and building, yet several core tasks are spoken by nature <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Confer with systems analysts, engineers, programmers and others to design systems and to obtain information on project limitations and capabilities, performance requirements and interfaces."
- "Analyze user needs and software requirements to determine feasibility of design within time and cost constraints."
- "Consult with customers or other departments on project status, proposals, or technical issues, such as software system design or maintenance."
- "Supervise and assign work to programmers, designers, technologists, technicians, or other engineering or scientific personnel."
- "Train users to use new or modified equipment."

The work context ratings show what kind of talking this is. Public speaking (one person addressing an audience) is rare: it averages 1.62 on a 1 to 5 frequency scale, no respondents reported doing it every day, and 2.01% reported doing it weekly. Telephone conversations average 3.43 and face-to-face discussions average 3.0 on the same scale. Dealing with external customers or the public rates only 1.93 out of 5 for importance. Communicating with supervisors, peers, or subordinates, by contrast, rates 4.23 out of 5. In short, developers rarely present, but they talk with their own team constantly.

## What the research says

Software engineering researchers have studied developer communication for decades, and the findings point in the same direction: missing or garbled conversations cost more than missing code.

**Communication breakdowns are a core design problem.** Curtis, Krasner, and Iscoe interviewed personnel from 17 large software projects. They identified three problems that shaped productivity and quality: the thin spread of application domain knowledge, fluctuating and conflicting requirements, and communication bottlenecks and breakdowns <Cite k="curtis1988field" />. Two of the three are problems of who talks to whom and how well.

**Coworkers are the main information source, and often the bottleneck.** Ko, DeLine, and Venolia observed 17 developers at a large software company and catalogued 21 types of information they looked for. Awareness about artifacts and coworkers was among the most frequently sought. The searches most often deferred involved design knowledge, such as why code was written a particular way. Developers often had to defer tasks because the only source of knowledge was a coworker who was unavailable <Cite k="ko2007information" />.

**Stand-ups help when they are discussions, not reports.** Stray, Sjøberg, and Dybå studied 12 software teams in three companies in Malaysia, Norway, Poland, and the United Kingdom. They interviewed 60 people and observed 79 daily stand-up meetings. Information sharing and the chance to discuss and solve problems drove positive attitudes. Status reporting to the manager, and meetings that felt too frequent or too long, drove negative ones <Cite k="stray2016standup" />.

**Asking questions well beats fancier techniques.** A systematic review of requirements elicitation studies by Davis and colleagues found that interviews, preferably structured ones, appear to be among the most effective elicitation techniques, and that methods such as card sorting or thinking aloud tended to be less effective. The authors caution that the underlying studies had not been replicated, so the results are preliminary <Cite k="davis2006elicitation" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Structured questioning with users.** "Analyze user needs" is a conversation before it is a document. Developers who arrive with a planned sequence of questions, and who follow up on vague answers ("fast enough for what?"), collect better requirements. The elicitation review above is the strongest evidence for this.

**2. Explaining the why behind a design.** Because design rationale is the information developers most often cannot find, the ability to say out loud why a module works the way it does is valuable to every future teammate. It also makes design reviews shorter.

**3. Short, problem-focused updates.** Stand-up research favors updates that surface a blocker or a decision, not a list of finished tickets. A good update takes under a minute and ends with a specific ask.

**4. Translating technical status for non-engineers.** Consulting "with customers or other departments on project status" means explaining risk, delay, and tradeoffs without jargon. The skill is choosing one concrete consequence ("the export will be a week late") over a tour of the code.

**5. Teaching users.** Training users on new software calls for slower pacing, more pauses, and checking for understanding, which is a different register from talking to other engineers.

## How to practice

- **Requirements interview drill.** Pick a feature you know well. Write eight open questions a user might need to answer, then role-play the interview with a colleague playing a vague stakeholder. Notice where you accept a fuzzy answer and practice one follow-up for each.
- **The 60-second design explanation.** Choose a piece of your own code and explain, aloud and without notes, why it is built that way. Write a short script of the same explanation and paste it into the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to see whether it fits in a minute.
- **Clean up your walkthroughs.** Record yourself walking through a pull request, transcribe a few minutes, and run it through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter). Technical explanations often fill with "basically" and "so, um" while the speaker searches for the next step.
- **Plain-language status updates.** Draft the update you would give a product manager about a delay, then check it with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) for long sentences and dense terms.
- **Pause before answering.** In code reviews and stand-ups, a two-second pause before answering a question reads as thoughtful. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps make that habit feel normal.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do software developers need public speaking skills?

Less than most professionals. O*NET respondents rate public speaking at 1.62 on a 5-point frequency scale for this occupation, and none reported doing it daily. Small-group talk with teammates and stakeholders matters far more.

### How does speaking demand for developers compare with other tech jobs?

It is lower than in support roles. [Computer user support specialists](/professions/computer-support-specialists) rank 219 of 910, while software developers rank 553, because support work involves constant live conversations with users.

### What is the most useful speaking skill for a junior developer?

Asking clear questions. Research on developer information needs shows that much of the knowledge a developer needs lives in coworkers' heads, so the ability to ask a precise question, and to explain what you have already tried, saves the most time.
