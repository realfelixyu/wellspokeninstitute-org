Fundraising is a speaking job built on asking. Fundraisers rank 83rd of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, with speaking rated 4.12 out of 5 for importance in O\*NET® data <Cite k="onet31" />. Face-to-face discussion is rated 5.00 out of 5 for frequency, the maximum, and telephone use 4.95. Field experiments with real donors show that single sentences in a spoken request, and the fundraiser's own motivation on the phone, change how much money comes in.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list for fundraisers starts with identifying and building relationships with potential donors and securing commitments from individuals and corporate donors. Fundraisers solicit cash, in-kind gifts, and sponsorships, contact corporate representatives, officials, and community leaders, attend community events to promote the cause, and "explain the tax advantages of contributions to potential donors." They also prepare presentations of fundraising data, write speeches, and recruit sponsors and volunteers <Cite k="onet31" />.

Dealing with the public is rated 4.67 out of 5 for importance, and communicating with people outside the organization 4.71. Formal public speaking is rated 3.14, with 19.05% of surveyed fundraisers reporting it weekly. Most of the job happens in one-on-one meetings, calls, and events. A first meeting, a thank-you call, a site visit, and a renewal conversation may all come before a large gift, and in each one the donor is deciding whether to trust the person asking. In May 2025, 111,040 people were employed as fundraisers <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

**One sentence made more people give.** Cialdini and Schroeder ran two experiments during a door-to-door charity drive. A solicitor who added "Even a penny will help" to a standard request significantly increased the share of households that donated, without reducing the size of the average donation. The second experiment replicated the result and supported the explanation: legitimizing a very small gift makes it hard to say no, without encouraging people to give only a token amount <Cite k="cialdini1976penny" />.

**Mentioning another donor's gift raised pledges by phone.** Shang and Croson ran a field experiment during a public radio station's on-air fund drive. When listeners called in to pledge, researchers answering the phone as station volunteers told some callers, "We had another member, they contributed" a specific amount, before asking how much they would like to pledge. The most effective amount, $300, which sat at the 90th percentile of the station's past gifts, raised contributions by 12% on average ($13). The effect was significant for new members but not renewing ones, and it did not reduce giving the following year <Cite k="shang2009social" />.

**Callers who met a beneficiary raised far more.** Grant and colleagues worked with 39 callers in a university fundraising call center whose donations funded student scholarships. Callers who briefly met and talked with a scholarship recipient were compared with callers who read a letter from him or had no exposure. One month later, the group that met the beneficiary had increased its phone time by 142% and the money it raised by 171%, while the control groups did not improve <Cite k="grant2007impact" />. In follow-up experiments, the authors traced this kind of effect to a stronger sense that one's work makes a difference.

These studies are field experiments with real donors and real money, which makes them unusually strong evidence for this occupation. Each tested a specific setting, so results may differ for major gifts or corporate sponsorships.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Wording the ask.** Small phrases change behavior. A legitimizing line such as "Any amount helps" lowers the barrier to saying yes.
- **Using social information honestly.** Mentioning what a real donor gave, at a level above the typical gift, can raise pledges. It only works if the example is true.
- **Telling the beneficiary's story.** Fundraisers who had met a beneficiary worked longer and raised more. Being able to describe a real person helped by the gift, in your own words, carries into every call.
- **Explaining clearly.** Donors ask practical questions about tax treatment, pledges, and how gifts are used. Short, accurate answers build trust.
- **Speaking at events.** Galas, community meetings, and donor briefings call for short, organized remarks with one clear request.

## How to practice

1. **Script your ask.** Write your standard request in three sentences: the need, what a gift does, and the ask. Add one legitimizing line and test both versions on colleagues.
2. **Learn one story by heart.** Meet or talk with someone your organization has helped, with their permission, and practice telling their story in 60 seconds. Time it with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer).
3. **Build a case-for-support pitch.** Use the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) to draft a 30-second version of why your organization deserves support.
4. **Rehearse the tax explanation.** Write a plain two-sentence answer to "Is this tax-deductible?" that is accurate for your organization, checked with your finance team, and practice it aloud.
5. **Plan event remarks.** Draft your event speech and check its length with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Keep the ask in the last minute and say it once, clearly.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for fundraisers?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.12 out of 5 for fundraisers, and face-to-face discussion is rated at the maximum frequency of 5.00 out of 5.

### Does the wording of a donation request matter?

Yes. In a door-to-door experiment, adding "Even a penny will help" increased the share of people who gave without lowering the average gift.

### Does mentioning other donors' gifts work?

In a public radio phone drive, telling callers that another member had given a specific amount raised contributions by 12% in the most effective condition, mainly among new members.
