Child, family, and school social workers spend their days in some of the hardest conversations any profession has: telling a parent there is a concern about their child's safety, interviewing a frightened child, and testifying at a custody hearing. O*NET® rates speaking importance for the occupation at 4.5 out of 5, and it ranks 22nd of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Nearly every O*NET task statement for this occupation involves talk. Social workers "interview clients individually, in families, or in groups, assessing their situations, capabilities, and problems to determine what services are required," "counsel parents with child rearing problems, interviewing the child and family to determine whether further action is required," and "address legal issues, such as child abuse and discipline, assisting with hearings and providing testimony to inform custody arrangements" <Cite k="onet31" />.

They also "serve as liaisons between students, homes, schools, family services, child guidance clinics, courts, protective services, doctors, and other contacts," consult with teachers about truancy and behavior, and refer families to services while giving "concrete information, such as where to go and how to apply." Some lead group counseling sessions and serve on policy committees.

The work context ratings are high across the board. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.95 out of 5, telephone use 4.85, and the importance of dealing with the public 4.27. Public speaking is rated 2.87, higher than for health care social workers (2.48) or mental health and substance abuse social workers (2.68), and 24.67% of these social workers report doing it weekly, which fits court appearances, school meetings, and case conferences.

The audiences change constantly. In one week a school social worker might talk with a 7-year-old, a defensive parent, a principal, a judge, and a housing agency, each requiring a different register.

## What the research says

**Social workers often raise concerns clearly but with little empathy.** In a study of 24 recorded interviews, social workers met with an actor playing a parent in two child protection scenarios. On average they asked many closed questions, raised concerns often, used few reflections, and rarely noted positives. Nearly all achieved clarity about the concern, but most showed low levels of empathy. Empathy was the factor that most influenced the simulated parent: empathic social workers created less resistance and drew out more information, and this did not come at the cost of failing to discuss concerns <Cite k="forrester2008communication" />. The setting was simulated, but the pattern is specific and actionable.

**Motivational interviewing offers a framework for resistance.** A follow-up paper identified five main causes of parental resistance in child and family work, including social disadvantage, the child protection context itself, and the behavior of the social worker. The authors argued that motivational interviewing provides useful skills for reducing the social worker's own contribution to resistance, with one key adaptation: keeping the focus on the child's welfare and safety <Cite k="forrester2012resistance" />. This is a theoretical argument rather than a trial, but it builds on the simulated-interview findings.

**Structured protocols improve interviews with children.** A review of research on the NICHD Investigative Interview Protocol, a structured script for forensic interviews with children, concluded that controlled studies "have repeatedly shown that the quality of interviewing reliably and dramatically improves" when interviewers use the protocol <Cite k="lamb2007nichd" />. The protocol emphasizes open-ended prompts that invite children to describe events in their own words. Interviewers are urged to exhaust free-recall prompts such as "Tell me everything about that" before moving to more directive questions.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Empathy while raising a concern.** The Forrester study shows these are compatible. Acknowledge the parent's position ("I can see how upsetting it is to have me here") and then state the concern plainly. Both parts are necessary.

**2. Reflections over closed questions.** Social workers in the study leaned heavily on closed questions. Replacing some with reflections ("It sounds like the mornings are when things fall apart") invites parents to say more.

**3. Open prompts with children.** Interviews with children work best with open invitations ("Tell me everything about that") rather than yes-or-no or leading questions. Follow your agency's protocol training.

**4. Testimony that is factual and calm.** In court, answer the question asked, use specific observations rather than conclusions, and say "I don't know" when that is the truth.

**5. Referrals with concrete next steps.** The O*NET referral task calls for specifics: where to go, when it is open, what to bring, and who to ask for.

## How to practice

- **Role-play a concern conversation.** With a colleague playing a parent, practice raising a safety concern. Record it and count reflections, closed questions, and positives noted.
- **Rewrite your opening.** Draft the first three sentences you say at a home visit, including an empathic statement and a clear purpose, and run them through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Practice open prompts.** Write a list of open-ended prompts appropriate for children of different ages and say them aloud until they come naturally.
- **Prepare for testimony.** Practice answering likely questions concisely. The [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) helps you keep answers short and on point.
- **Manage pace under stress.** Record yourself explaining a case plan and check your rate with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Use the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) to slow down at difficult points.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can social workers be empathic and still address child safety concerns?

Yes. In a study of simulated child protection interviews, empathic social workers drew less resistance and more information from parents, and empathy did not reduce their ability to raise and discuss concerns.

### What is the NICHD protocol?

It is a structured interview protocol for talking with children about possible abuse, built around open-ended prompts. A research review found it reliably improves the quality of children's interviews.

### How much public speaking do child and family social workers do?

O*NET rates public speaking at 2.87 out of 5, and about a quarter report doing it weekly. Court testimony, school meetings, and case conferences all draw on these skills.
