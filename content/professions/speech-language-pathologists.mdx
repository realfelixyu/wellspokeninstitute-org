Speech-language pathologists use their own speech as a clinical instrument. They model sounds and sentences for clients to imitate, coach families on how to talk with someone who has aphasia, and explain progress to teachers and parents. O*NET® rates oral comprehension for the occupation at 4.5 out of 5 in importance and oral expression at 4.25, and speech-language pathologists rank 92nd of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Face-to-face discussion receives the maximum frequency rating of 5.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for speech-language pathologists describe a job where talk is both the treatment and the topic. They "instruct clients in techniques for more effective communication, such as sign language, lip reading, or voice improvement," "educate patients and family members about various topics, such as communication techniques or strategies to cope with or to avoid personal misunderstandings," and "teach clients to control or strengthen tongue, jaw, face muscles, or breathing mechanisms" <Cite k="onet31" />.

Other spoken tasks face professionals. Speech-language pathologists "consult with and advise educators or medical staff on speech or hearing topics" and "participate in and write reports for meetings regarding patients' progress, such as individualized educational planning (IEP) meetings." In schools, some conduct lessons or therapeutic games to help teachers, and some provide communication instruction to dialect speakers or students with limited English proficiency.

The work context ratings show close, small-group work. Face-to-face discussion is rated 5, telephone use 3.79, and the importance of dealing with the public 3.8. Public speaking is rated 2.06, with 10.58% reporting it weekly, which fits in-service training and IEP meetings with several adults at the table.

This mix asks for unusual range. In one day, a school-based clinician may slow down and exaggerate articulation for a 5-year-old, explain a standardized test score to anxious parents, and summarize progress for a room of teachers and administrators.

## What the research says

**Speech and language therapy improves functional communication in aphasia.** A 2016 Cochrane review included 57 randomized trials with 3,002 participants who had aphasia after stroke. Compared with no therapy, speech and language therapy produced clinically and statistically significant benefits for functional communication (standardized mean difference 0.28), as well as reading, writing, and expressive language. Based on smaller numbers of studies, those benefits were not evident at follow-up <Cite k="brady2016aphasia" />. The review supports the core work while showing that durability needs more study.

**Training communication partners helps.** Communication partner training teaches family members, friends, and caregivers how to support conversation with a person who has aphasia. An updated systematic review of communication partner training found that all 25 newly reviewed studies reported positive changes, bringing the total across two reviews to 56 studies with positive outcomes. The authors recommended that partner training be used to improve partners' skill in supporting the communication of people with chronic aphasia, while calling for higher-quality trials <Cite k="simmonsmackie2016partner" />. This research maps directly onto the O*NET task of educating family members about communication strategies.

**The alliance is built through talk.** A qualitative study interviewed 22 speech and language therapists about how they build therapeutic alliances with people with aphasia after stroke. Three themes emerged: laying the groundwork, which included recognizing personhood, developing shared expectations, and establishing therapeutic ownership; augmenting cohesion through responsiveness and resolving conflict or resistance; and contextual shapers such as family and continuity of care. The authors described this as a "fluid interplay of verbal and non-verbal skills" and called for training in reflexivity, inclusive goal setting, and motivational techniques <Cite k="lawton2018alliance" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Precise modeling.** Clients imitate the model you give them, so aim for clear articulation, a controlled rate, and deliberate stress on the target. Slow down without distorting the natural rhythm of the phrase.

**2. Coaching partners in plain language.** When teaching family members, demonstrate a strategy ("Ask yes-or-no questions, and give him time"), have them try it, and give specific feedback. The partner training evidence supports this kind of coaching.

**3. Setting shared expectations.** The alliance study identified shared expectations as a foundation. Early in therapy, say what therapy will involve, what progress might look like, and how the client's own goals fit in.

**4. Explaining results to non-specialists.** In IEP meetings and medical consultations, translate test scores and diagnoses into functional terms: what the child can and cannot do in class, or what the patient can safely eat.

**5. Adjusting register quickly.** Moving between a young child, a worried parent, and a room of educators requires shifts in vocabulary, pace, and formality within minutes.

## How to practice

- **Record your modeling.** Record yourself producing common treatment targets and check your rate with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Listen for whether your slowed speech still sounds natural.
- **Write a family coaching script.** Draft a short explanation of two or three communication strategies for a family member and run it through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Prepare an IEP summary.** Write a 2-minute spoken summary of a student's progress and time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Practice delivering it without reading.
- **Practice pausing for processing time.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) can help you build longer, comfortable pauses, which give clients with language impairments time to respond.
- **Rehearse an in-service talk.** If you train teachers or staff, use the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) for early run-throughs, then switch to notes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does speech therapy work for aphasia after stroke?

A Cochrane review of 57 trials found speech and language therapy improved functional communication, reading, writing, and expressive language compared with no therapy. Benefits were not clearly maintained at follow-up, based on fewer studies.

### Why do speech-language pathologists train family members?

Family members and caregivers shape everyday conversation for a person with aphasia. A systematic review found 56 studies reporting positive outcomes from communication partner training, and the authors recommend it for chronic aphasia.

### How much public speaking do speech-language pathologists do?

Some. O*NET rates public speaking at 2.06 out of 5 for the occupation, and about 1 in 10 report doing it weekly.
