Lawyers have one of the most speaking-intensive jobs in the United States. They rank 4th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, with speaking rated 4.62 out of 5 for importance and oral expression 4.88 in O\*NET® data <Cite k="onet31" />. The required level of oral expression is 5.00 on a 7-point scale. Research on appellate courts and mock juries links spoken advocacy to results: the quality of an oral argument, the questions a court asks, and the timing of a disclosure all relate to how cases come out.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list includes "interviewing clients and witnesses to ascertain the facts of a case," representing clients in court or before government agencies, advising clients on transactions and legal rights, and negotiating settlements and contracts. Supplemental tasks include presenting and summarizing cases to judges and juries, selecting jurors, arguing motions, and questioning witnesses at trial <Cite k="onet31" />.

Most of this talk is not in a courtroom. Telephone use is rated 4.89 out of 5 for frequency and face-to-face discussion 4.44, while formal public speaking is rated 2.73, with 11.74% of surveyed lawyers reporting it daily. The 754,500 lawyers employed in May 2025 earned a median of 159,670 dollars a year <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. For many of them, the core spoken skills are explaining law to clients and negotiating with opposing counsel, with courtroom advocacy concentrated among litigators.

## What the research says

**Better oral arguments win more votes.** Johnson, Wahlbeck, and Spriggs used a unique source: the private grades Justice Harry Blackmun gave lawyers who argued before the U.S. Supreme Court. Blackmun's grades were partly predicted by conventional markers of a lawyer's credibility and were not simply a product of his ideology, which suggests they captured argument quality. The probability that a justice voted for a party rose sharply when that party's lawyer gave a better oral argument than opposing counsel <Cite k="johnson2006oralargument" />.

**The side that gets more questions tends to lose.** Epstein, Landes, and Posner tested a long-observed pattern in Supreme Court oral arguments. Using regression analysis, they found that both the number of questions a lawyer receives and the length of those questions were negatively correlated with that side's chances of winning. They suggest judges use oral argument partly to express their views and influence colleagues, since formal deliberation is limited <Cite k="epstein2010questioning" />. A heavy round of questions is a signal that a lawyer has to answer well.

**Judges' voices reveal their leanings.** Dietrich, Enos, and Sen analyzed more than 3,000 hours of Supreme Court oral argument audio spanning 30 years. They measured emotional arousal through the vocal pitch in each justice's voice during arguments and used it to predict many of the justices' eventual votes. The predictions held up when a range of legal and political controls were included <Cite k="dietrich2019arousal" />. For advocates, the tone of a question carries information alongside its content.

**Disclosing bad facts first can soften them.** Williams, Bourgeois, and Croyle tested "stealing thunder," revealing negative information about your own client before the other side does. In two simulated trials, one criminal and one civil, with 257 and 148 college student participants, damaging evidence had less impact when the side it hurt raised it first. Path analysis suggested the effect worked through greater credibility <Cite k="williams1993thunder" />. Mock jurors are not real jurors, so the size of the effect in actual trials is uncertain.

The Supreme Court studies are observational and focus on one unusual court. Still, they consistently show that what happens out loud in a courtroom is linked to how judges decide.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Answering hard questions directly.** Since questioning patterns track outcomes, a lawyer's ability to give a short, direct answer and return to the argument is central.
- **Reading the bench.** Noticing which issues draw the most and sharpest questions, and the tone in which they are asked, lets an advocate shift time to the points that matter.
- **Framing weaknesses first.** Raising a damaging fact on your own terms, before the other side does, is a persuasion technique with experimental support.
- **Plain explanation for clients.** Advising clients on rights and risks requires translating legal analysis into clear options and likely outcomes.
- **Negotiation talk.** Settlement and contract negotiations reward careful listening, clear offers, and calm handling of pressure, much of it by phone.

## How to practice

1. **Run a hostile moot.** Ask two colleagues to interrupt your argument with their toughest questions. Practice answering each in one or two sentences before returning to your outline.
2. **Write a 60-second roadmap.** Draft the opening of your argument as a short roadmap of your two or three main points and time it with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer).
3. **Draft your thunder.** For each case, write how you would raise your weakest fact in your own words, then say it aloud until it sounds matter-of-fact rather than defensive.
4. **Rehearse client calls.** Before advising a client by phone, outline the options, a recommendation, and the main risk in plain language. Run the outline through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to catch sentences too dense to follow by ear.
5. **Mind your pace.** Nerves can speed up delivery. Record a practice argument and check it with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator).

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for lawyers?

Extremely important. Lawyers rank 4th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, and O\*NET rates oral expression 4.88 out of 5 for importance.

### Do oral arguments actually change case outcomes?

Research on the U.S. Supreme Court suggests they can. Justices were more likely to vote for the side whose lawyer gave the stronger oral argument, based on one justice's private grades of each argument.

### Should lawyers bring up bad facts themselves?

Mock jury experiments support it. When damaging evidence was raised first by the side it hurt, it had less impact on verdicts, apparently because that side gained credibility.
