Architects design with drawings and models, but they win work, refine designs, and get buildings built by talking about them. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.00 out of 5 for importance for architects, and the occupation ranks 136th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. The required level of oral expression is 4.88 on a 7-point scale. Research on design education and practice shows that presenting a design aloud, taking critique, and talking through options with clients are distinct spoken skills, each with its own conventions.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list asks architects to "meet with clients to review or discuss architectural drawings" and to "consult with clients to determine functional or spatial requirements of structures." They direct technicians preparing drawings, monitor the work of engineers and other specialists, conduct on-site observations of construction, and represent clients in obtaining bids. They also "develop marketing materials, proposals, or presentations to generate new work opportunities" <Cite k="onet31" />.

Telephone use is rated 4.83 out of 5 for frequency and face-to-face discussion 4.61. Communicating with people outside the organization is rated 4.43 out of 5 for importance. Formal public speaking is rated 2.70: no surveyed architects reported it daily, but 26.09% reported it weekly, which fits a job with regular client presentations and design reviews. During construction observations, architects also explain drawings to contractors and answer questions about changes, conversations where a misunderstanding can become an expensive fix.

## What the research says

**The crit is design's most common presentation genre.** Deanna Dannels spent a year observing design studios and interviewing faculty about the oral presentations students performed. She identified four distinct oral genres, each shaped by the prominence of visual and spatial material and by audience feedback, and found that faculty linked success in each genre to specific skills. She described these presentations as ritual performances with a social role in the discipline <Cite k="dannels2005crits" />. Architects learn early that a design is judged partly by how it is presented and defended out loud.

**Feedback in crits may not match professional practice.** In a follow-up study, Dannels and Martin categorized the types of feedback given during critiques in design studios ranging from novice to expert levels and compared how often each type appeared. The feedback tended to socialize students into egalitarian relationships and independent decision-making. The authors suggest this may reflect academic stages or idealized workplaces more than actual professional settings <Cite k="dannels2008critiques" />. New architects may need to adjust when feedback comes from clients, code officials, or senior partners.

**Design progress is negotiated in conversation with clients.** Janet McDonnell analyzed the conversation in two early-stage meetings between architects and the building users who were their clients. Each side contributed from its own expertise, but there were also "tentative excursions," moments when one side invoked the other's knowledge or position to propose or justify a design decision, prompting an expert response. These exchanges showed how design decisions were negotiated together and how shared ownership of a design was built through talk <Cite k="mcdonnell2009negotiation" />.

These are qualitative studies of a small number of settings, so they describe patterns rather than measure effects. They show that the spoken side of architecture has its own rules, from studio rituals to the give-and-take of a client meeting. We did not find studies that measure how these skills affect winning commissions or client satisfaction.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Narrating a drawing.** Walking listeners through a plan or section in a logical order, pointing to what matters, turns a drawing into an argument.
- **Taking critique.** Listening to criticism without defending every line, asking clarifying questions, and summarizing what you will change are the skills studio crits are meant to build.
- **Drawing out client needs.** O\*NET's task of determining functional and spatial requirements depends on open questions about how people will use a building.
- **Negotiating design choices.** Inviting clients to propose ideas, and explaining tradeoffs in their terms, builds shared ownership of the design.
- **Pitching for work.** Interviews for commissions reward a short, clear story about the firm's approach and relevant projects.

## How to practice

1. **Give a three-minute pinup.** Present one sheet of a current project to a colleague in three minutes: the problem, the main idea, and one tradeoff. Time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) before you start drafting what to say.
2. **Practice receiving critique.** After a review, repeat each comment back in one sentence and say whether you will act on it. Avoid explaining your original intent unless asked.
3. **Translate jargon.** Take a paragraph of your project description and rewrite it for a client with no design training. Run it through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and say it aloud.
4. **Script open questions for clients.** Before a programming meeting, write five questions about daily use, such as "Walk me through a typical morning in this space."
5. **Build your interview pitch.** Use the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) to draft a 30-second description of your firm's approach, then rehearse it until it sounds conversational.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for architects?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.00 out of 5 for architects, and the occupation ranks 136th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score.

### What is a design crit?

A crit is an oral presentation of design work to faculty, peers, or guests, followed by critique. Research describes it as the most common presentation genre in design education.

### How do architects and clients reach design decisions?

Studies of recorded meetings show decisions being negotiated in conversation, with architects and building users each contributing expertise and sometimes drawing on the other's knowledge to justify a choice.
