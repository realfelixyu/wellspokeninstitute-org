General and operations managers do much of their managing by talking. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4 out of 5 in importance for this occupation and places it 188th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, at 69.6 of 100 <Cite k="onet31" />. These are the managers who oversee several departments or locations at once, so their tools are the staff meeting, the hallway check-in, the supplier call, and the walk across the floor. Very little of that work can be done in writing alone.

This is one of the largest management occupations in the country: 3,503,020 people held the job in May 2025, at a median annual wage of $105,770 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET description of the role is broad, and many of its tasks depend on spoken coordination <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Prepare staff work schedules and assign specific duties."
- "Perform personnel functions, such as selection, training, or evaluation."
- "Establish or implement departmental policies, goals, objectives, or procedures in conjunction with board members, organization officials, or staff members."
- "Plan or direct activities, such as sales promotions, that require coordination with other department managers."
- "Monitor suppliers to ensure that they efficiently and effectively provide needed goods or services within budgetary limits."
- "Perform sales floor work, such as greeting or assisting customers, stocking shelves, or taking inventory."

The frequency data confirms that the talk is daily. Face-to-face discussions average 4.86 and telephone conversations 4.76 on a 1 to 5 scale where 5 means every day. Dealing with external customers or the public rates 4.49 out of 5 for importance. Public speaking averages 2.46, but that average includes a large group who do it regularly: 30.68% of respondents reported speaking to an audience weekly and another 3.27% daily. For many operations managers, that is the shift huddle or the weekly all-hands.

## What the research says

**Successful managers spend more of their talk outside the chain of command.** Luthans, Rosenkrantz, and Hennessey had trained observers record the behavior of 52 managers in three very different organizations (a state revenue department, a manufacturing plant, and a campus police department) over two weeks. Success was measured by how quickly managers had been promoted. Two activities were significantly related to success: interaction with outsiders and socializing or politicking. Managers in the top third also showed more conflict management behavior than those in the bottom third <Cite k="luthans1985managers" />. The design is correlational, and the authors note that the pattern varied by type of organization.

**Senior managerial work has shifted toward informing groups.** Tengblad repeated Henry Mintzberg's classic study of top executives' work about 30 years later. Compared with Mintzberg's executives, the modern group had a much larger workload, more contact with subordinates in group settings, a greater emphasis on giving information, and less time on administrative work. Their days were also less fragmented than earlier studies had suggested <Cite k="tengblad2006managerial" />. The manager as a speaker to groups, rather than a desk-bound administrator, is the newer picture.

**What gets said in team meetings predicts team and company results.** Kauffeld and Lehmann-Willenbrock videotaped 92 regular team meetings and coded every interaction. Teams that showed more functional interaction, such as problem-solving talk and action planning, were more satisfied with their meetings, and better meetings went with higher team productivity. Constructive meeting interaction was related to organizational success 2.5 years later. Dysfunctional talk, such as criticizing others or complaining, had significant negative relationships with these outcomes, and those negative effects were stronger than the positive effects of good interaction <Cite k="kauffeld2012meetings" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Running problem-solving meetings.** The meeting research points to a concrete habit: steer talk from complaint to "what will we do, and who owns it." Because complaining hurt outcomes more than good interaction helped, stopping a complaint spiral early may be the single most valuable meeting skill.

**2. Briefing groups clearly.** Tengblad's executives spent more time giving information to groups. A good briefing states the change, the reason, and what each listener needs to do, in that order.

**3. Talking with outsiders.** Supplier calls, board conversations, and customer contact connect to the success pattern Luthans observed. These conversations need preparation, a clear ask, and follow-through.

**4. Handling conflict between departments.** Coordinating with other department managers creates disputes over budgets, staff, and priorities. Managers who name the disagreement plainly and propose a decision process avoid having the conflict surface later in front of staff.

**5. Hiring and evaluation conversations.** Selection and evaluation are listed personnel functions. Structured interview questions and specific, behavior-based feedback make these conversations fairer and more useful.

## How to practice

- **Five-minute huddle script.** Write a shift or weekly huddle with one update, one priority, and one recognition. Check that it fits in five minutes with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).
- **Complaint redirect drill.** Prepare three phrases that move a meeting from complaint to action, such as "What would we need to change by Friday?" Practice them aloud until they sound natural.
- **Read your briefing for the ear.** Before announcing a policy change, paste the announcement into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to catch sentences that are easy to read but hard to follow when spoken.
- **Slow down on the key message.** Use the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) to rehearse pausing before and after the one sentence you most need people to remember.
- **Prepare for interviews.** Run through candidate questions with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) from the other side of the table, to get a feel for how long strong answers take.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often do general and operations managers speak in front of groups?

Fairly often. In O*NET data, 30.68% of respondents report speaking to an audience weekly and 3.27% report doing so every day, usually in staff meetings, huddles, and briefings.

### Do managers who network more really get promoted faster?

In the Luthans observation study, interaction with outsiders and socializing or politicking were the activities most related to fast promotion. That is a correlation in 52 managers from three organizations, so it shows a pattern rather than a rule.

### What is the most common mistake managers make in meetings?

The meeting study by Kauffeld and Lehmann-Willenbrock suggests it is letting criticism and complaining run unchecked, because those behaviors had stronger negative effects than problem-solving talk had positive ones.
