Speaking is close to the whole job for elementary school teachers. In O\*NET® data, speaking is rated 4.12 out of 5 for importance and oral expression 4.62, and the occupation ranks 57th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking frequency is rated 4.59 out of 5, and 84.59% of surveyed teachers report doing it every day. For this job the useful question is less whether teachers talk enough and more which kinds of talk help children think.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list for elementary teachers reads like a schedule of conversations. Teachers "instruct students individually and in groups, using teaching methods such as lectures, discussions, and demonstrations." They read books to whole classes and small groups, lead storytelling, music, and games, and state the objectives of each lesson to students. Outside the classroom they meet with parents and guardians to discuss a child's progress, and they confer with counselors, administrators, and other teachers about behavior and academic problems <Cite k="onet31" />.

The work context ratings show how constant this is. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.87 out of 5 for frequency, close to every day. Telephone use is lower, at 3.60. Dealing with the public, which for teachers mostly means families, is rated 4.53 out of 5 for importance.

Most of that talk is aimed at children who are still learning to listen, take turns, and explain themselves. The teacher's speech is the delivery method for the lesson, and it is also the model children copy when they talk to each other.

## What the research says

**Dialogic teaching produced measurable gains in a large trial.** Robin Alexander's dialogic teaching program trained teachers to widen the range of talk in their lessons, then supported them through cycles of planning, target-setting, and review using mentoring and recordings of their own classes. In a randomized controlled trial in UK cities with nearly 5,000 Year 5 students (U.S. fourth grade) and 208 teachers, students in intervention classes were about two months ahead of control-group peers in English, mathematics, and science tests after 20 weeks. Coded video showed large changes in how both teachers and students talked <Cite k="alexander2018dialogic" />.

**Elaboration and challenge matter most when many students take part.** Howe and colleagues video-recorded lessons in 72 classrooms of 10- and 11-year-olds and related features of teacher-student dialogue to student outcomes, adjusting for prior attainment and other classroom factors. When participation was broad, two teacher moves stood out: asking students to elaborate on earlier contributions, and querying those contributions. Both were associated with stronger curriculum mastery, and elaboration was also linked to better attitudes toward learning <Cite k="howe2019dialogue" />. This was an observational study, so it shows association, not proof of cause.

**Children can be taught how to talk together.** Mercer, Wegerif, and Dawes worked with 60 British primary school children aged 9 and 10. Teachers explicitly taught "ground rules" for talking together and raised children's awareness of how they used language. Compared with matched control classes, the children's talk changed in the intended direction, groups that followed the ground rules did better on reasoning problems, and individual scores on a nonverbal reasoning test also improved <Cite k="mercer1999talk" />.

**Read-alouds teach vocabulary when the teacher talks about the words.** Beck and McKeown studied kindergarten and first-grade classes in a low-achieving school, where regular classroom teachers taught sophisticated words drawn from trade books typically read aloud. Children who received the instruction learned significantly more words than children who did not. In a second study, words taught over six days produced gains twice as large as words taught over three <Cite k="beck2007vocabulary" />.

Taken together, these studies favor teacher talk that hands the floor back to children and asks them to say more. Most of the evidence comes from upper elementary grades, and the effect sizes in single studies should be read as estimates, not guarantees.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Asking for elaboration.** Follow-ups such as "Can you say more?" and "What makes you think that?" are the moves Howe's team linked to learning. They also serve the O\*NET task of giving students chances to observe, question, and investigate.
- **Challenging ideas safely.** Querying a child's answer, then giving time to respond, keeps disagreement productive. The tone has to signal curiosity rather than correction.
- **Teaching the rules of talk.** Norms for group discussion, such as giving a reason and asking a partner for theirs, can be taught directly, the way Mercer's teachers did.
- **Reading aloud with explanation.** Pausing a story to define and use one rich word turns storytime into vocabulary instruction.
- **Talking with families.** Parent conferences call for a plain, specific description of a child's progress and one or two concrete next steps the family can take at home.

## How to practice

1. **Audit one discussion.** Audio-record 15 minutes of a class discussion. Tally two things: how often you ask a child to elaborate, and how often one child responds to another. Aim to raise both counts over a month.
2. **Write your follow-up prompts.** Put five elaboration prompts and three challenge prompts on an index card and keep it on your desk during discussions.
3. **Build in silence.** After a question, wait before calling on anyone. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you get used to the feel of a few seconds of quiet.
4. **Plan the words in tomorrow's read-aloud.** Choose three words, write a child-friendly definition and an example sentence for each, and read the passage aloud once at home. Check that your pace leaves room for listening with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator).
5. **Rehearse conference openings.** Draft a 60-second summary of each child's progress and say it out loud before the meeting. The [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) keeps it short.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for elementary school teachers?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.12 out of 5 for this occupation, and it ranks 57th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, inside the top 10%.

### Does more teacher talk mean more learning?

Not by itself. The studies above link learning to talk that asks children to elaborate, question, and give reasons, with many children taking part.

### Can these skills be trained?

Yes. In the dialogic teaching trial, teachers learned new talk practices through training, mentoring, and review of their own recorded lessons, and their students made measurable gains.
