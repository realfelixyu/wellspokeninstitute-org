For clinical and counseling psychologists, conversation is the treatment. O*NET® rates oral expression at 4.62 out of 5 in importance and speaking at 4.12, and the occupation ranks 40th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. A therapy session is an hour of structured talk in which the psychologist listens closely, reflects back, asks questions that open new ground, and occasionally says something hard. The research on what makes therapy work is, in large part, research on how therapists speak.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for clinical and counseling psychologists put talk at the center. Psychologists "counsel individuals, groups, or families to help them understand problems, deal with crisis situations, define goals, and develop realistic action plans," "conduct assessments of patients' risk for harm to self or others," and "collect information about individuals or clients, using interviews, case histories, observational techniques, and other assessment methods" <Cite k="onet31" />.

Other spoken tasks reach beyond the therapy room. Psychologists "provide consulting services, including educational programs, outreach programs, or prevention talks to schools, social service agencies, businesses, or the general public," consult with physicians and other clinicians, and supervise and train interns. Some also prepare evaluations for court.

The work context ratings show mostly one-on-one talk: face-to-face discussion is rated 4.8 out of 5 and telephone use 4.4. The importance of dealing with the public is lower, at 3.44, which fits the private, scheduled nature of most sessions. Public speaking is rated 2.44.

The hardest spoken moments are often the shortest: asking directly about suicidal thoughts during a risk assessment, naming a pattern the client has not seen, or repairing the relationship after a session goes badly.

## What the research says

**The alliance predicts outcome across therapies.** The most recent major meta-analysis of the therapeutic alliance covered 295 independent studies with more than 30,000 patients. The overall correlation between alliance and outcome in face-to-face therapy was r = .278, and internet-based therapy showed nearly the same value (r = .275). The relationship held across assessor perspectives, alliance and outcome measures, treatment approaches, patient characteristics, and countries <Cite k="fluckiger2018alliance" />. The data are correlational, and the authors devote a section to whether the link is causal.

**Empathy is a moderately strong predictor.** A meta-analysis of 82 samples covering 6,138 clients found that therapist empathy correlated with client outcome at r = .28. Client, observer, and therapist perceptions of empathy predicted outcome better than measures of empathic accuracy <Cite k="elliott2018empathy" />. One reading is that empathy does the most good when the client can perceive it.

**Positive regard has a smaller but real link.** A meta-analysis of 64 studies found a small positive association between therapist positive regard and outcome (g = .28), which grew to g = .36 in a multilevel analysis controlling for overlapping samples <Cite k="farber2018regard" />.

**About 1 in 5 clients drop out.** A meta-analysis of 669 studies with 83,834 clients found a weighted dropout rate of 19.7%. Dropout was more likely among younger clients, clients with personality or eating disorder diagnoses, and clients seen by trainee clinicians <Cite k="swift2012dropout" />.

These effect sizes deserve a sober reading. A correlation near .28 means the relationship accounts for a real but modest share of the differences in outcome between clients, so technique, life circumstances, and the problem itself still matter. Still, the alliance and empathy findings have held up across hundreds of studies, and both depend on things a psychologist can practice: how they listen, reflect, and respond.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Accurate reflection.** Reflecting a client's meaning, including the feeling under the words, is how empathy becomes something the client can perceive. The Elliott findings point to perceived empathy as the stronger predictor.

**2. Collaborating on goals out loud.** The alliance includes agreement on goals and tasks. Saying the goals back to the client ("So for the next few weeks, we're working on getting you back to sleeping through the night") checks that agreement.

**3. Asking direct risk questions.** The risk assessment task requires plain questions about suicide and harm. Use a calm, matter-of-fact tone and direct wording ("Are you thinking about killing yourself?") rather than euphemisms.

**4. Repairing ruptures.** When a client seems annoyed or withdrawn, naming it ("I noticed you went quiet when I said that") and inviting a response gives the client a chance to say what went wrong.

**5. Presenting to non-clinical audiences.** Prevention talks to schools and businesses require a different register: shorter claims, concrete examples, and no clinical jargon.

## How to practice

- **Review session recordings with consent.** Count reflections versus questions in a recorded session, or in a role-play with a colleague. A session heavy on questions and light on reflections is worth revisiting.
- **Script risk questions.** Write out your risk assessment questions in plain words and say them aloud until they come out evenly. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) can help you wait after a hard question.
- **Check pace.** Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) on a recorded explanation of a treatment approach to clients. Psychoeducation delivered too quickly is easy to miss.
- **Prepare an outreach talk.** For prevention or education talks, draft and time your talk with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator), and check it for jargon with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Practice explaining therapy itself.** One O*NET task is advising clients on how counseling could help. Write a 60-second explanation of what therapy with you involves and rehearse it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What matters most in how a therapist communicates?

Meta-analyses link the therapeutic alliance (r = .278) and therapist empathy (r = .28) to client outcomes across therapy types. Both depend on how well the therapist listens and reflects.

### How common is dropping out of therapy?

A meta-analysis of 669 studies found a weighted dropout rate of 19.7%, or about 1 in 5 clients. Rates were higher with younger clients and trainee clinicians.

### Do psychologists need public speaking skills?

Some do. O*NET rates public speaking at 2.44 out of 5, and psychologists may give prevention talks, outreach programs, and staff training.
