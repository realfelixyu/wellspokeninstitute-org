Flight attendants speak in public more often than anyone else in the workforce. In O*NET® data, public speaking is rated 4.94 out of 5 for frequency for flight attendants, the highest of all 910 occupations, and 97.2% report doing it every day <Cite k="onet31" />. Speaking importance is rated 4.12, and the occupation ranks 265th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score. Most of that speaking happens through a cabin PA system, to a buckled-in audience whose attention to safety briefings researchers describe as poor.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for flight attendants include several spoken duties that are also legal requirements. Flight attendants "announce and demonstrate safety and emergency procedures, such as the use of oxygen masks, seat belts, and life jackets," "direct and assist passengers in emergency procedures, such as evacuating a plane following an emergency landing," and "reassure passengers when situations, such as turbulence, are encountered" <Cite k="onet31" />. They also announce delays and descent, answer passengers' questions, greet boarding passengers, determine special assistance needs, and attend preflight briefings on crew coordination.

Federal regulations specify much of what must be said. Under 14 CFR 121.571, all passengers must be "orally briefed by the appropriate crewmember" before each takeoff on smoking rules, the location of emergency exits, the use of safety belts, and the location and use of any required flotation equipment. A crewmember must also give an individual briefing, before each takeoff, to each person who may need help moving quickly to an exit, covering routes to the exits and asking how best to assist them <Cite k="cfr121571" />. Printed cards supplement the oral briefing but do not replace it.

The work context ratings beyond public speaking are also high. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.73 out of 5, and the importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.87. Telephone use is lower, at 3.16.

Flight attendant speech comes in three modes:

- **Scripted PA announcements,** delivered dozens of times a week, where the risk is sounding rote.
- **One-on-one service and safety talk,** from exit row briefings to handling a disruptive passenger.
- **Emergency commands,** which crews train for and hope never to use.

## What the research says

**Entertainment can cost recall.** In an experiment, 82 participants watched one of three pre-flight safety briefing videos: a standard version, a humorous version, or a movie-themed version. The humorous and movie-themed briefings improved participants' mood, but there was a trade-off: the greater the entertainment value, the poorer the retention of key safety messages <Cite k="tehrani2015briefings" />. The study used videos rather than live crew, but the lesson applies to live announcements that lean on jokes.

**Noise and attention are real obstacles.** A second experiment with 101 participants tested recall of a pre-recorded safety briefing with and without in-cabin aircraft noise and subtitles. The authors began from the observation that "passenger attention to pre-flight safety briefings on commercial aircraft is poor." Participants who recognized the celebrity presenter recalled more messages, and subtitles helped, but only when realistic cabin noise was present <Cite k="molesworth2016celebrity" />. For live announcements, the finding suggests that cabin noise competes with every word, which makes clear delivery and repetition worth more than style.

**Cockpit and cabin crews do not always communicate well.** An early study of cockpit-cabin communication noted that several dramatic accidents had exposed weaknesses in coordination between pilots and flight attendants. Surveys at two airlines led the authors to conclude that the two crews represent "two distinct and separate cultures" and that this separation inhibits teamwork <Cite k="chute1995cockpit" />. The study is old, but it is a reminder that the interphone call to the flight deck is a safety conversation too, and it works best when the caller leads with the problem and what they need.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Making the routine briefing sound live.** Passengers tune out a briefing that sounds recited. Vary pace, look at the cabin, and stress the one item most likely to matter on this aircraft, such as the nearest exit.

**2. Clear PA delivery over noise.** Speak slower than conversational pace, keep the microphone at a steady distance, and pause between items. Announcements that are hard to hear do not count for much, however well written.

**3. Individual briefings.** Exit row and special assistance briefings are short conversations. Explain, confirm the passenger understood and is willing, and ask how best to help when the rule calls for it.

**4. Reassuring during turbulence.** Calm, brief announcements that say what is happening and what passengers should do can head off a wave of individual questions.

**5. Emergency commands.** Use your airline's exact wording, keep commands short and loud, and repeat them. Drill them until they come out without thought.

## How to practice

- **Time your announcements.** Paste your standard announcements into the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to see how long they run at a clear PA pace.
- **Measure your PA pace.** Record a briefing and check it with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Try a rate a little slower than conversation and ask a colleague at the back of the cabin whether every item came through.
- **Practice pauses between items.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you leave a beat between safety points so each one registers.
- **Rehearse emergency commands aloud.** Practice your airline's evacuation commands at full volume in a safe setting until the wording is automatic.
- **Check custom announcements for clarity.** If you write your own delay or welcome announcements, run them through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to catch long sentences.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are flight attendants required to give safety briefings out loud?

Yes. Federal rules under 14 CFR 121.571 require an oral briefing before each takeoff on smoking, exits, seat belts, and flotation equipment, plus individual briefings for passengers who may need help reaching an exit.

### Do funny safety announcements work?

They lift mood, but an experiment found that the more entertaining a safety briefing video was, the fewer key safety messages viewers remembered.

### Which occupation does the most public speaking?

In O*NET data, flight attendants have the highest public speaking frequency of all 910 occupations, rated 4.94 out of 5, with 97.2% doing it daily.
