Journalism is often thought of as a writing job, but most reporting starts with a spoken question. News analysts, reporters, and journalists rank 52nd of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, with speaking rated 4.25 out of 5 for importance and oral expression and oral comprehension each 4.38 in O\*NET® data <Cite k="onet31" />. Telephone use is rated 4.50 out of 5 and communicating with people outside the organization 4.64. Two spoken skills matter most: designing questions that get answers, and, for broadcast work, delivering the news at a pace listeners can follow.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list asks journalists to "gather information and develop perspectives about news subjects through research, interviews, observation, and experience." They arrange interviews, build relationships with credible sources, conduct taped or filmed interviews, and discuss priorities with editors. They communicate with readers, viewers, and the public by phone, and transmit stories from remote locations by phone. Supplemental tasks for broadcast journalists include presenting live or recorded commentary and anchoring news programs <Cite k="onet31" />.

Public speaking frequency is rated 2.80 out of 5, with 18.22% of surveyed journalists reporting it daily and 15.59% weekly. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.20 out of 5. For many reporters the core speaking task is the interview, by phone or in person, rather than any on-air appearance.

## What the research says

**Journalists' questions can be measured, and they have changed.** Clayman and Heritage developed a system for analyzing the questions journalists ask public figures. They coded 10 features of question design as indicators of four dimensions: initiative, directness, assertiveness, and hostility. Comparing the press conferences of Presidents Eisenhower and Reagan, they found significant differences on every indicator, all in the direction of more adversarial questioning. Journalists had become far less deferential toward the president <Cite k="clayman2002presidents" />.

**Different parts of aggressive questioning move differently.** Clayman, Elliott, Heritage, and McDonald applied the same approach to a sample of presidential news conferences from Eisenhower through Clinton. Every dimension showed a long-term decline in deference. Directness rose gradually and steadily, while initiative, assertiveness, and adversarialness were more volatile: they rose from the late 1960s through the early 1980s, fell from the mid-1980s through the early 1990s, and rose again at the end of the century <Cite k="clayman2006trends" />. The authors connect these swings to the political and social context of each period. For working reporters, the value of this research is a vocabulary for checking a question before asking it: how much initiative it takes, how direct it is, whether it asserts a view, and whether it is hostile. Each is a choice, and each shapes the answer a source can give.

**Speech rate shapes how radio news is perceived.** Emma Rodero compared speech rate and pauses in 40 news bulletins from public broadcasters in the United Kingdom (BBC), France, Italy, and Spain. Most authors who write about radio recommend 160 to 180 words per minute, and only the BBC bulletins fell within that range. Italian and Spanish bulletins were faster and had fewer pauses. In a second part of the study, listeners' assessments of the news varied with speech rate <Cite k="rodero2012speechrate" />.

These studies cover a narrow slice of journalism: presidential news conferences and radio bulletins. They offer a framework for thinking about question design and delivery rather than a full account of how reporters interview everyday sources.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Question design.** Choosing how direct and how assertive to be, and whether to build a premise into a question, determines what kind of answer is possible. The four dimensions in the Clayman research are a useful checklist.
- **Follow-up.** An evasive answer is a cue for a second question. Planning that follow-up in advance makes it easier to ask under time pressure.
- **Rapport with sources.** Building relationships with credible sources, a core O\*NET task, depends on listening, keeping promises about attribution, and asking respectfully.
- **Phone interviewing.** Without facial cues, phone interviews need clear introductions, explicit ground rules (on or off the record), and short questions.
- **Broadcast delivery.** Reading copy aloud at a measured pace, with pauses between ideas, gives listeners time to absorb each item.

## How to practice

1. **Map your questions.** Before an interview, label each question on your list as open or closed, and mark which ones contain a premise the source might reject. Rewrite any question that does two jobs at once.
2. **Prepare the follow-up.** For your three most important questions, write the follow-up you will ask if the answer avoids the point, and practice saying it calmly.
3. **Time your read.** Record yourself reading a 60-second script and check your pace with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Compare it with the 160 to 180 words per minute range recommended for radio.
4. **Write for the ear.** Run broadcast copy through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to find long sentences and tongue-twisting phrases before you read on air.
5. **Rehearse on prompter.** Practice reading scripts from the [free teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) so you can keep your eyes up and your phrasing natural.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for journalists?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.25 out of 5 for news analysts, reporters, and journalists, and the occupation ranks 52nd of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score.

### Have journalists become more aggressive when questioning presidents?

Studies of presidential news conferences found a long-term decline in deference from Eisenhower to Clinton, though some dimensions of aggressiveness rose and fell with the political climate.

### What is a good speaking rate for radio news?

Writers on radio style commonly recommend 160 to 180 words per minute. In a comparison of four European public broadcasters, only BBC bulletins fell within that range.
