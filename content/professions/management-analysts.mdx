Management analysis is a speaking-intensive profession. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4 out of 5 in importance for management analysts and the level of oral expression required at 4.75 on a 7-point scale. The occupation ranks 169 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, with a score of 70 <Cite k="onet31" />. The category includes program analysts and management consultants. Whether they work inside a company or for a consulting firm, analysts learn how an organization really works by asking people, and they get their recommendations adopted by persuading people.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported 898,280 management analysts employed in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $101,860 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements describe a cycle of listening, then explaining <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Interview personnel and conduct on-site observation to ascertain unit functions, work performed, and methods, equipment, and personnel used."
- "Review forms and reports and confer with management and users about format, distribution, and purpose, identifying problems and improvements."
- "Confer with personnel concerned to ensure successful functioning of newly implemented systems or procedures."
- "Prepare manuals and train workers in use of new forms, reports, procedures or equipment, according to organizational policy."

The work context data puts phone calls at the center: telephone conversations average 4.89 on a 1 to 5 frequency scale, essentially daily, and face-to-face discussions average 4.74. Public speaking averages 2.76, and 14.29% of respondents said they present to an audience weekly, though none said daily. Communicating with people outside the organization rates 4.33 out of 5 for importance, a sign of how much of the job is client work.

## What the research says

Research on consultants and change agents is mostly qualitative, built on interviews and case studies rather than experiments. It is still useful for understanding what the spoken side of the job involves.

**Consulting has long been studied as persuasion, but clients push back.** Fincham reviewed the critical literature on consultancy, which emphasizes consultants' symbolic strategies and treats consultancy as a powerful system of persuasion. Drawing on case studies of consultants' role in managing organizational change, he argued that the consultant-client relationship has no fixed shape. Clients with market power, or projects with real interdependence, produce very different relationships, so the exchange is contingent rather than dominated by the consultant <Cite k="fincham1999consultant" />. For the analyst, this means persuasion has to be fitted to each client's power and expectations.

**Consultants describe their own role in modest terms.** Kakabadse, Louchart, and Kakabadse interviewed 17 business consultants working in fields such as change management, IT, corporate finance, and human resources. In contrast to earlier critical accounts, these consultants appeared humble about their relationships with clients, saw moving the client forward as their ultimate goal, and were aware of how much control and discretion clients handed to them <Cite k="kakabadse2006consultant" />.

**A lot of consulting happens outside the meeting room.** Sturdy, Schwarz, and Spicer studied business dinners and backstage consulting work. They found that these informal settings were highly structured and strategic, shaped by norms of eating and socializing and by patterns of inclusion and exclusion. In their words, business meals remain a traditional practice where "official secrets" continue to grease the wheels of commerce <Cite k="sturdy2006dinner" />. Informal conversation, in other words, is part of the work.

**Change efforts involve two kinds of talk.** Lewis surveyed the implementers of 89 planned change efforts across a range of sectors about the channels they used to disseminate information to staff and to solicit input from them, and whether their channel use predicted how successful they judged the change to be <Cite k="lewis1999change" />. The framing itself is useful: an analyst rolling out a new procedure has to both announce it and listen to the people who will live with it.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Discovery interviewing.** Interviewing personnel about how work really gets done is the analyst's main data source. It calls for neutral, open questions, follow-ups on specifics ("Walk me through the last time that happened"), and the patience to let silence draw out more detail.

**2. Presenting recommendations to people with power.** Recommendations land differently depending on who holds power in the relationship, which Fincham's case studies illustrate. Strong presenters lead with the client's problem in the client's words before offering the fix.

**3. Informal, relationship-building conversation.** The research on business dinners shows that trust and information often move in unofficial settings. Being at ease in that kind of talk, without overselling, is part of the job.

**4. Soliciting input during change.** When confirming that a new system is "functioning successfully," the analyst needs to ask in ways that make honest complaints safe to voice.

**5. Training front-line staff.** Teaching workers a new form or procedure requires a different register from a steering committee briefing: concrete examples, slower pace, and checks for understanding.

## How to practice

- **Interview rehearsal.** Draft a 10-question discovery interview guide. Practice it with a colleague who answers vaguely, and work on one follow-up question per answer that asks for an example.
- **Recommendation in 90 seconds.** Write your main recommendation as a spoken summary, then time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Senior clients often give you less time than you planned for.
- **Make it easy to hear.** Consulting slides tend to be dense. Paste your talk track into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to find long sentences and stacked jargon that listeners will lose.
- **Practice pausing for input.** In change conversations, ask a question and wait. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you get comfortable holding several seconds of silence.
- **Introduce yourself well.** Analysts meet new stakeholders constantly. Use the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) to prepare a 30-second introduction that explains why you are there and what you need from them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do management analysts give many presentations?

Some do, but phone calls and one-on-one conversations dominate. O*NET respondents rated telephone conversations at 4.89 out of 5 for frequency, while 14.29% reported presenting to an audience weekly and none reported doing so daily.

### Is the research on consultant communication strong?

It is mostly qualitative. The studies cited here rely on interviews, case studies, and observation, which describe how consulting talk works but do not measure its effect on project outcomes the way experiments would.

### How does this compare with project management?

Management analysts rank 169 of 910 on speaking demand, while project managers rank 449. Analysts spend more time interviewing people outside their own team and persuading clients, which raises the speaking demand.
