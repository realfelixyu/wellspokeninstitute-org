Health care managers rarely touch a patient, but what they say shapes how safely patients are treated. O*NET® rates speaking importance for medical and health services managers at 4.12 out of 5, and the occupation ranks 170th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. With 597,080 people in the role in May 2025 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />, managers run the meetings, phone calls, and briefings that connect clinicians, administrators, and boards.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for medical and health services managers include several roles that depend on speaking. Managers "maintain communication between governing boards, medical staff, and department heads by attending board meetings and coordinating interdepartmental functioning," "consult with medical, business, and community groups to discuss service problems, respond to community needs, enhance public relations, coordinate activities and plans, and promote health programs," and "develop instructional materials and conduct in-service and community-based educational programs" <Cite k="onet31" />.

They also direct, supervise, and evaluate staff, run recruitment and hiring, and "manage change in integrated health care delivery systems, such as work restructuring, technological innovations, and shifts in the focus of care." Much of change management happens in conversation, as managers explain why something is changing and hear objections.

The work context ratings put the phone at the center. Telephone use is rated 4.93 out of 5, face-to-face discussion 4.61, and the importance of dealing with the public 4.07. Public speaking is rated 2.96, and 14.29% of managers report doing it daily, which fits a role full of staff meetings, board presentations, and community events. Communicating with supervisors, peers, or subordinates is rated 4.63 in importance.

A manager's audiences often care about different parts of the same decision. Physicians, nurses, finance staff, and board members may each need the message framed differently.

## What the research says

**Leader inclusiveness makes it safe to speak up.** In a study of 23 neonatal intensive care units engaged in quality improvement, researchers defined leader inclusiveness as "words and deeds exhibited by leaders that invite and appreciate others' contributions." Survey data supported their hypotheses: staff with higher professional status felt more psychologically safe, leader inclusiveness predicted psychological safety and softened the effect of status differences, and psychological safety predicted engagement in quality improvement work <Cite k="nembhard2006inclusiveness" />. The study was cross-sectional, so it shows associations, but it ties specific leader talk to whether lower-status staff speak up.

**Executive walk rounds helped the nurses who took part.** In a randomized study across 23 clinical units, hospital executives visited units once every four weeks for three visits and asked staff about their patient safety concerns and what could be done. At the unit level, safety climate scores did not differ significantly after the visits. But nurses who actually participated in a walk round reported better safety climate than nurses in control units who did not (72.9% vs 52.5% positive) <Cite k="thomas2005walkrounds" />. That comparison is not randomized, so self-selection may explain part of the difference.

**The broader evidence is promising but limited.** A systematic review of 21 studies on strategies to improve hospital patient safety culture found some evidence that leadership walk rounds and multifaceted unit-based programs may improve safety climate, but concluded that overall there is "limited evidence to support definitive impacts" <Cite k="morello2013safetyculture" />.

Taken together, the studies associate leaders who ask for input with staff who feel safer and more engaged, though the evidence falls short of proof.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Asking questions that invite bad news.** Inclusiveness starts with questions like "What's the next thing that could hurt a patient on this unit?" and with thanking the person who answers, especially if they are junior.

**2. Closing the loop.** In the walk-round trial, staff suggestions were tabulated and acted on when possible. Report back on what changed, or explain why something could not.

**3. Framing one message for several audiences.** The board needs the risk and the cost, clinicians need the effect on patients and workflow, and staff need what changes on Monday. Prepare each version in advance.

**4. Presenting change honestly.** When restructuring work, state what is changing, why, and what is not yet decided. Saying what is still undecided avoids promises you may have to walk back.

**5. Running a tight phone call.** With phone use rated near the maximum, a habit of opening calls with the purpose and closing with the next step saves time across hundreds of calls.

## How to practice

- **Write three inclusive questions.** Draft questions you could ask on a unit visit that invite concerns, then practice asking them aloud in a neutral, curious tone.
- **Prepare a board update.** Write a 3-minute spoken update on a quality initiative and time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Rehearse with the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) before moving to notes.
- **Check clarity for staff.** Paste a change announcement into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and simplify anything that would be hard to follow if heard once at a shift huddle.
- **Record a meeting opening.** Record how you open a staff meeting and run the transcript through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter).
- **Practice holding silence after a question.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you wait long enough for staff to answer, instead of answering for them.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do health care managers encourage staff to speak up about safety?

Research in neonatal intensive care units found that leader inclusiveness, meaning words and actions that invite and appreciate input, predicted psychological safety and engagement in improvement work.

### Do executive walk rounds work?

In one randomized study, unit-level safety climate did not change significantly, but nurses who participated in walk rounds reported a better safety climate. A systematic review found the evidence promising but limited.

### Do health care managers give presentations?

Many do. O*NET rates public speaking at 2.96 out of 5 for the occupation, and 14.29% of managers report doing it daily.
