Human resources work is conducted almost entirely through conversation: interviews, orientations, policy explanations, complaints, and exits. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4.12 out of 5 in importance for human resources specialists, and the occupation ranks 165 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score with a score of 70 <Cite k="onet31" />. The job combines heavy one-on-one talk with frequent group speaking. HR specialists rate telephone conversations at 5.0 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale, face-to-face discussions at 4.92, and public speaking at 3.33.

The occupation employed 912,430 people in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $75,940 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for HR specialists show how many kinds of conversations the job requires <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Interview job applicants to obtain information on work history, training, education, or job skills."
- "Inform job applicants of details such as duties and responsibilities, compensation, benefits, schedules, working conditions, or promotion opportunities."
- "Interpret and explain human resources policies, procedures, laws, standards, or regulations."
- "Address employee relations issues, such as harassment allegations, work complaints, or other employee concerns."
- "Schedule or conduct new employee orientations."
- "Conduct exit interviews and ensure that necessary employment termination paperwork is completed."

The group speaking numbers stand out: the public speaking rating of 3.33 is higher than that of 827 of the 910 occupations in our dataset. In the O*NET survey, 15.28% of HR specialists reported speaking to an audience every day and another 34.73% weekly, which reflects orientations, benefits sessions, and manager training. Communicating with supervisors, peers, or subordinates rates 4.66 out of 5 for importance.

## What the research says

Employment interviewing is one of the most researched topics in work psychology. Three meta-analyses, each combining dozens of studies, speak directly to how HR specialists should talk with candidates.

**Structured interviews predict job performance better.** McDaniel, Whetzel, Schmidt, and Maurer analyzed 245 validity coefficients from 86,311 people. Interview validity depended on the content of the interview, how it was conducted, and what it was used to predict. Situational interviews, which ask candidates what they would do in job scenarios, had higher validity than job-related interviews, which in turn beat psychologically based ones. Structured interviews had higher validity than unstructured ones. Validity was similar for predicting job and training performance and lower for predicting tenure <Cite k="mcdaniel1994interviews" />.

**Unstructured interviews reward polish over substance.** Barrick, Shaffer, and DeGrassi conducted a meta-analysis of how candidates' self-presentation, including appearance, impression management, and verbal and nonverbal behavior, relates to interviewer ratings and later job performance. They concluded that what interviewers see in the interview may not be what they get on the job, and that unstructured interviews were especially affected by these tactics <Cite k="barrick2009selfpresentation" />. A confident speaking style can sway an unstructured interview without predicting performance.

**How recruiters behave affects who wants the job.** Chapman and colleagues meta-analyzed 667 coefficients from 71 studies of recruiting outcomes. Applicants' attraction to organizations, and their intentions to pursue and accept jobs, were predicted by job and organization characteristics, recruiter behaviors, perceptions of the recruiting process, perceived fit, and hiring expectancies. Recruiter demographics were not significant predictors <Cite k="chapman2005attraction" />. What recruiters say and how they treat candidates mattered more than who the recruiters were.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Running structured interviews.** Asking every candidate the same job-related questions, in the same order, and probing consistently is the skill most clearly tied to better hiring decisions. It also protects candidates from being judged on charm alone.

**2. Listening past the performance.** Because self-presentation tactics influence unstructured ratings more than they predict job success, interviewers need to listen for evidence, such as specific examples and results, rather than fluency.

**3. Presenting the job honestly and warmly.** Informing applicants about duties, pay, and working conditions is recruiting talk. Recruiter behavior predicts applicant attraction, so a clear, respectful, well-informed conversation is part of competing for talent.

**4. Explaining policy in plain language.** Interpreting laws and benefits rules for employees means trading legal phrasing for concrete examples of what the policy means for them.

**5. Handling sensitive conversations.** Harassment complaints, grievances, and exit interviews require a calm voice, careful questions, and clear statements of what happens next, without promising outcomes HR cannot guarantee.

## How to practice

- **Build a structured interview guide.** Write five situational questions for a role you hire for, with a rating guide for each. Practice asking them aloud in the same words every time.
- **Time candidate answers.** Use the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) to get a feel for how long strong answers take, which helps you decide when to probe and when to move on.
- **Orientation rehearsal.** Half of HR specialists present at least weekly. Rehearse your orientation talk with the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter), then check its length with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).
- **Plain-language policy scripts.** Paste your explanation of a benefits or leave policy into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and shorten anything an employee would have to hear twice.
- **Practice the pause in difficult meetings.** In complaint and exit conversations, a pause after a hard question invites the full answer. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps build that habit.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much public speaking do HR specialists do?

More than most occupations. In O*NET data, 15.28% of HR specialists report speaking to an audience every day and 34.73% weekly, mostly in orientations, trainings, and benefits sessions.

### Are structured interviews really better than conversational ones?

Meta-analytic research says yes for predicting job performance. Structured interviews had higher validity than unstructured ones, and unstructured interviews were more influenced by candidates' self-presentation tactics.

### Does the recruiter's behavior affect whether candidates accept offers?

A meta-analysis of 71 studies found that recruiter behaviors predicted applicants' attraction to organizations and their intentions to pursue and accept jobs, while recruiter demographics did not.
