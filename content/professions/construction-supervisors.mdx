Construction supervisors depend on conversation to assign crews, coordinate trades and teach work methods. In O*NET®, speaking importance is 3.75 out of 5 for First-Line Supervisors of Construction Trades and Extraction Workers. Face-to-face discussions receive the highest frequency rating, 5 out of 5. Public speaking scores 2.73. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 63.7 out of 100, a score shared by the occupations listed 420th through 425th of 910. A conversation with two people about a change to this morning's work is a good example of speaking in this role <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Suppose Esme, a fictional foreman, is scheduling a delivery. The delivery crew expects to use the east entrance at nine. Another crew has a job planned in the same place. Telling both crews “There's a change to the schedule” leaves each responsible for guessing what the other has heard. Esme can identify which entries on the work plan conflict, bring the two leads together and ask who can confirm an agreed revision. Until someone does, the time printed on either crew's plan is still in question.

The O*NET task statements explain why that conversation belongs with the supervisor. The role includes assigning work, coordinating it with other construction activities, training workers and consulting contractors or technical staff to resolve problems. A supervisor may know that two assignments conflict without having authority to approve a change to either one. In that situation, the crew still needs to hear which work is affected and who is being asked to decide <Cite k="onet31" />.

## What the research says

**A foreman can speak to workers several times and never discuss a particular risk.** In a Danish study of five construction crews, researchers asked workers about recent conversations and separately observed conditions on site. They coached the foremen of two crews and gave them feedback on everyday conversations. Workers reported a statistically significant increase in safety talk in one coached group on its own and across the two groups combined. Observers also recorded improvements in site safety conditions in both groups. The measurements concerned conversations and conditions. They did not count prevented injuries <Cite k="kines2010safety" />.

**Crews can describe what changed after a briefing.** Near St. Louis, 84 residential construction foremen took an eight-hour course in fall prevention and communication. It included preparing toolbox talks about the work at hand and giving crew members feedback. Foremen and their crews subsequently reported improvements in safety communication that persisted at six months. There was no concurrent control group. The improvement came from survey ratings, and the study did not establish that the course reduced actual falls <Cite k="kaskutas2016residentialforemen" />.

A separate U.S. project tells a less tidy story. Twenty subcontracting companies were assigned to receive leadership training earlier or later. Supervisors in the early group reported better understanding and use of leadership skills two and four weeks after training. The researchers detected no significant changes in outcomes reported by the crews overall. If a supervisor wants to know whether a new briefing has changed what reaches workers, the supervisor's own impression answers only half that question <Cite k="schwatka2019frontline" />.

**Give people something specific to discuss.** At eight construction sites around Boston, researchers compared four sites using a communication and recognition program with four comparison sites. Foremen received reports of the previous week's observations, including what workers had done well, at weekly meetings. The program also used posters and recognition. Worker surveys showed a modest improvement in safety climate relative to the comparison sites. The study evaluated the whole program. It could not tell a foreman what effect reading out a report would have by itself <Cite k="sparer2016bostonconstruction" />.

In Denmark, 57 foremen from 12 companies tried a program built around planning tasks and discussing their risks. The researchers revised it between groups and drew on interviews, observations and questionnaires. Only 49 attended enough sessions to qualify for the evaluation. Among the foremen who evaluated the course, most found the topics useful. Participants described changes in how they worked with crews and others. A participant finding a discussion useful is a different result from an independent observer finding that the work became safer <Cite k="jeschke2017foremen" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Make the question narrow enough to answer.** Esme could ask, “What time does your copy show for the east entrance?” If each lead gives a time from their own document, she can establish where the plans disagree. “Everyone clear on tomorrow?” might get two nods from people holding different plans. One pointed question also gives a new crew member a way into the conversation without having to contradict the entire briefing.

**Keep the observations and decisions separate.** “Both sheets list nine” is an observation. “Use the west entrance” is an instruction and needs the site's authorization and checks. If Esme has only established the conflict, she can tell the crews that and name the person resolving it. When she gets an approved plan, she can go back to each lead with the revision and ask what other assignments it affects.

**Respond to the person who spoke first.** Suppose a worker points out that the second crew hasn't received the change. A supervisor can answer, “Thanks, I sent it to the delivery lead only. I'll call the other lead now.” That response tells the worker what happened to their observation. No evidence reviewed here establishes a particular script as the best wording.

## How to practice

Use two paper calendars and two chairs. This is an untested desk exercise. Give one person Esme's calendar with the delivery at nine and give the other person a crew calendar with the east entrance already in use then. Allow one minute for a conversation. Each person then writes down what is known, who is checking the conflict and whether a revised time has been approved. Compare the notes before inventing a solution. One person writing “move at ten” when the other wrote “ask about ten” is the error to catch.

For a second round, imagine the conflict has been settled by the person responsible and both calendars are updated. One partner joins the conversation late. Ask the other to explain what changed and how the late arrival can check which plan is current. These examples use fictional timing and paperwork. Actual assignments and site controls belong in the employer's procedures and work plan.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a toolbox talk need a long speech?

The studies include everyday exchanges as well as scheduled meetings. In the Danish coaching study, researchers asked about safety in the ordinary conversations foremen were already having with workers <Cite k="kines2010safety" />.

### Can I tell how well a briefing went from the number of people who nodded?

A nod alone leaves open what the listener understood. In the U.S. leadership study, supervisors reported changes that the crew surveys did not detect. Asking workers to describe the specific change gives the supervisor another source of information <Cite k="schwatka2019frontline" />.

### Are the wage figures just for residential foremen?

BLS estimated 812,210 U.S. jobs and a median annual wage of $79,920 in May 2025 for the full category of first-line supervisors of construction trades and extraction workers. The residential carpentry study covers a narrower setting <Cite k="blsoews2025,kaskutas2016residentialforemen" />.
