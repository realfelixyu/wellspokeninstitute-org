General office clerks talk all day, but rarely to an audience. The speaking is in short exchanges: a caller who needs a coworker who is out, a visitor at the counter with a form, a manager who wants a file by noon. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 3.62 out of 5 for importance in this job. Telephone conversations score 4.88 out of 5 for frequency and face-to-face discussions 4.63, while public speaking scores 1.83. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score for the occupation is 60.1 out of 100, which places it 501st of 910 occupations <Cite k="onet31" />. Most of what follows is about getting a message from one person to another without losing a detail.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

O*NET lists the core spoken tasks plainly. Clerks "answer telephones, direct calls, and take messages." They "communicate with customers, employees, and other individuals to answer questions, disseminate or explain information, take orders, and address complaints." They also deliver messages and run errands, and some train other staff or take minutes at meetings <Cite k="onet31" />.

Here is a typical moment. A caller says, "This is Rosa at Pine Hill Supply. The invoice you sent us has the wrong purchase order. Can Tom call me?" Tom is at lunch. If the note says "Rosa called, call back," Tom has to start from zero. A note he can act on names the caller and company, the problem, the number to call and any deadline: "Rosa, Pine Hill Supply, says invoice 2214 lists the wrong PO. Wants a call back before 4 today at 555-0148." Before hanging up, read the number and invoice back to Rosa. She can catch a wrong digit while she is still on the line.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 2,464,940 general office clerk jobs in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $45,010 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

Little research follows general office clerks by that title. The closest studies watch administrative staff in medical practices and schools, and office workers whose days are broken up by talk.

**Requests that do not fit the form.** Deborah Swinglehurst and colleagues spent 395 hours observing staff in four urban UK general practices, including 56 reception or administrative staff. More than half of the requests for repeat prescriptions were classed by receptionists as "exceptions," most often because the drug, dose or timing differed from the electronic record. Staff handled many demands at once (patients at the desk, the telephone ringing, doctors' requests), and they used brief encounters with clinicians to ask for advice. In one practice, where clerks sent written queries to doctors, staff had learned to include enough information that the query would make sense to whoever was on the rota <Cite k="swinglehurst2011repeatprescribing" />. That is the same test as Tom's phone note: could someone else pick it up and finish the job?

**The school office.** Abdullah Bayat and Aslam Fataar followed three administrative clerks in South African public schools for 12 weeks, with later interviews and observation. The clerks were in their offices to answer the phone, welcome guests and deal with queries from teachers, parents and education officials. Students came to them for messages from parents who had phoned. The authors argue that clerks' testimony was often treated as less credible than that of principals and teachers, and that the clerks built credibility through close working relationships and steady presence <Cite k="bayat2018schoolclerks" />. An earlier interview study by Bayat described school clerks moving beyond the narrow roles their schools assigned them <Cite k="bayat2012clerkspace" />.

**Interruptions.** In a study of 28 information workers in the retail industry, social conversation unrelated to work took up 9.7% of observed activity and set off about 21% of the interruptions that came from other people, mostly started by colleagues <Cite k="arora2011informalinterruptions" />. Gloria Mark and colleagues found that people finished interrupted tasks faster with no loss of quality, but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort <Cite k="mark2008interruptedwork" />. Neither study shows that chat should be cut. They suggest a clerk should expect to stop and restart many times a day.

**Asking in person.** In experiments by M. Mahdi Roghanizad and Vanessa Bohns, people expected the channel they used to ask for help to matter little. When they actually made requests, the differences were large. Participants underestimated how much more often people agreed to a face-to-face request than to one sent through any other channel <Cite k="roghanizad2021askchannel" />. These were help requests made in experiments, not requests between office colleagues, so treat the finding as a hint.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**A message someone else can use.** Name, organization, the problem in one sentence, a number and a time. Read the number back. This covers the core task of answering phones and taking messages.

**Explaining a rule or a delay.** When a visitor is told a form is missing a signature, the useful part is what happens next: "I can't send this without the manager's signature. If you leave it with me, I'll have it signed and mailed by Thursday." Say what you will do and when.

**Asking a coworker for the missing piece.** Requests that do not fit the system need a quick question to the person who knows. Ask the specific question, not "Can you look at this?" Try "This order shows 40 boxes but the packing slip says 30. Which is right?"

**Getting back on track after an interruption.** Saying where you stopped ("I'm on row 14 of the April invoices") before you turn to a visitor makes it easier to restart.

## How to practice

None of these drills has been tested in a study. They are exercises built around the tasks above.

**Message relay.** Work in threes. One person reads a voicemail script with five details: name, company, problem, number and deadline. The second writes a message. The third, who never heard the call, reads the note aloud and says what they would do next. Count how many of the five details reached the third person. Switch roles and try a caller who speaks quickly. Ask the caller to repeat what you missed. The Institute's guide on [asking someone to repeat what you missed](/learn/how-to-ask-someone-to-repeat-what-you-missed) has phrasing to try.

**Counter explanation.** A partner plays a visitor whose form cannot be processed today. Explain the problem in two sentences or fewer, then say the next step and the day. The partner asks one follow-up question. Time yourself. If the explanation runs past 30 seconds, cut it down and try again.

**The specific ask.** Write three requests you would normally send by email, such as a missing date, an unclear amount or a wrong address. Turn each into one spoken question you could ask at a coworker's desk. Practice saying them aloud. Each should be answerable in a few words.

Receptionists face many of the same front-desk situations. See the Institute's page on [receptionists](/professions/receptionists), or compare occupations in the [speaking by occupation data](/data/speaking-by-occupation).

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a phone message include?

The caller's name and organization, what they need in one sentence, a callback number and any deadline. Read the number back to the caller before you hang up.

### Is it better to ask a coworker in person or by email?

For a quick fact you need now, ask in person or by phone if you can. Experiments with help requests found that people underestimated how much more often face-to-face requests succeed <Cite k="roghanizad2021askchannel" />. Use email when the other person will need the details in writing.

### How do I handle a visitor who is upset about something I cannot fix?

Say what you can do and who can do the rest. Give a name or a time, not only "someone will call you." If you promise a callback, write a message that the person returning the call can act on without asking the visitor to start over.
