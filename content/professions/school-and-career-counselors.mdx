School and career counselors do most of their work in conversation, one student or family at a time. In O\*NET® data, speaking is rated 4.12 out of 5 for importance and oral comprehension 4.25, and the occupation ranks 101st of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Formal public speaking is uncommon: only 0.33% of surveyed counselors report it daily. Face-to-face discussion, rated 4.98 out of 5, and telephone use, rated 4.95, are close to the maximum. Field experiments show that this personal contact, more than information alone, is what changes students' decisions.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list centers on counseling conversations. Counselors advise students on course selection, scheduling, truancy, study habits, and career planning, and counsel individuals or groups on personal, social, or behavioral problems. They provide crisis intervention, conduct follow-up interviews to check whether needs were met, and teach job search and interview skills. They also present information sessions and orientation programs, address community groups and staff about counseling services, and confer with parents and teachers <Cite k="onet31" />.

Dealing with the public is rated 4.09 out of 5 for importance. Much of that public is families: parents weighing college costs, guardians worried about attendance, students in crisis. The near-maximum telephone rating suggests that many of these conversations happen by phone.

## What the research says

**Personal help changed college outcomes, and information alone did not.** Bettinger, Long, Oreopoulos, and Sanbonmatsu ran a randomized experiment with low-income families receiving tax preparation help. Families offered immediate, personal assistance completing the federal financial aid form (FAFSA), along with aid estimates, filed more applications. High school seniors whose parents got this help were 8 percentage points more likely to complete two years of college, rising from 28% to 36%. Families who received only aid information, without help filling out the form, saw no improvement <Cite k="bettinger2012fafsa" />.

**Hands-on mentoring worked where incentives and information did not.** Carrell and Sacerdote ran a series of field experiments in college coaching and mentoring for students deciding whether to go to college. Only the treatments using intensive, in-person help raised college attendance and persistence. Financial incentives and information alone did not appear to work. For women, assignment to mentoring raised the college-going rate by 15 percentage points, while effects for men were much smaller. The authors concluded the mentoring substituted for skilled time and encouragement from parents or teachers that many students lacked <Cite k="carrell2017collegegoing" />.

**Regular coaching calls kept college students enrolled.** Bettinger and Baker evaluated a student coaching service in which coaches contacted students regularly to clarify goals, connect daily activities to long-term plans, and build skills such as time management and self-advocacy. Most participants were nontraditional college students. Students randomly assigned to a coach were more likely to persist during the coaching period and more likely to still be enrolled a year after it ended <Cite k="bettinger2014coaching" />.

**One motivational conversation helped middle schoolers.** Strait and colleagues randomly assigned 103 students in grades 6 to 8 to a single motivational interviewing session or a waitlist. Compared with the control group, students who had the session showed significant improvements in class participation and overall academic behavior and earned higher fourth-quarter math grades. The authors call for replication before drawing firm conclusions <Cite k="strait2012mi" />.

The common thread is that conversation, guidance, and follow-through did more than handing over facts. The college studies tested coaching and assistance programs, not school counselors directly, but the activities they tested match the core of the counselor's job.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Open questions and reflective listening.** Counseling and follow-up interviews work best when students do most of the talking. Questions that start with "what" and "how" draw out goals and obstacles.
- **Turning information into a next step.** The FAFSA study suggests that explaining aid is not enough; walking a family through the form is what made the difference.
- **Persistent, warm follow-up.** Regular contact was built into the coaching program that kept students enrolled. Keep each check-in short and tied to one specific task.
- **Calm crisis communication.** Crisis intervention calls for slow, simple sentences, clear safety steps, and careful listening.
- **Presenting to groups.** College nights, career workshops, and staff briefings need short, organized talks with time for questions.

## How to practice

1. **Rewrite your openers.** List the first questions you usually ask in a scheduling or career meeting. Rewrite any yes-or-no question as an open one, such as "What would make next year feel successful for you?"
2. **Practice a reflection.** In your next three meetings, after the student explains a problem, summarize it back in one sentence before offering any advice.
3. **Script a follow-up call.** Write a 30-second phone check-in that names one task and one deadline, such as submitting a financial aid form, and practice it until it sounds natural.
4. **Time your information session.** Draft your college or career night talk and check its length with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Leave at least a third of the slot for questions.
5. **Run mock interviews with students.** When you teach interview skills, have students practice answers with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer) and give feedback on one habit at a time.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for school counselors?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.12 out of 5, and the job ranks 101st of 910 occupations for speaking demands. Most of that speaking happens face to face and by phone, not on a stage.

### Is giving students information enough to change their plans?

Several field experiments suggest it is not. In studies of financial aid and college-going, information alone had little effect, while personal assistance and mentoring raised enrollment.

### What is motivational interviewing?

It is a structured counseling conversation that helps a person work through mixed feelings about a change. In one randomized trial with middle school students, a single session improved class participation and math grades.
