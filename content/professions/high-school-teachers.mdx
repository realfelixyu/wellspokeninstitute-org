High school teachers explain complex material out loud for most of the working day, so clarity and vocal stamina matter as much as subject knowledge. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.00 out of 5 for importance and oral expression 4.12, and the job ranks 147th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking frequency is rated 4.52 out of 5: 71.75% of surveyed teachers report public speaking every day and another 18.8% weekly. In May 2025, 1,065,210 people worked in the occupation <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Secondary teachers "instruct through lectures, discussions, and demonstrations in one or more subjects," according to the O\*NET task list. The required speaking level for the job is 4.12 on a 7-point scale, and the required level of oral expression is 4.88, reflecting how abstract high school content can be. Teachers also communicate lesson objectives, guide and counsel students with academic problems, meet with parents and guardians about progress, sponsor clubs and academic contests, and plan visits by guest speakers <Cite k="onet31" />.

Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.73 out of 5 for frequency and telephone use 3.56. A typical day involves repeating the same explanation to several class sections in a row, which raises two separate demands: explaining clearly every time, and keeping the voice healthy across the day.

## What the research says

**Clear explanations are linked to learning.** Titsworth and colleagues ran two meta-analyses on teacher clarity. The first pooled 144 reported effects covering 73,281 participants and estimated that clarity accounted for about 13% of the variance in student learning. Clarity had a larger link to affective learning (how students feel about a course) than to cognitive learning, and results varied a good deal across studies. The authors call for more studies that measure specific teacher behaviors rather than students' perceptions, and the samples were not limited to high schools, so the estimate is an average across settings <Cite k="titsworth2015clarity" />.

**Authentic questions open up classroom talk.** Nystrand and colleagues coded more than 33,000 questions in over 200 eighth- and ninth-grade English and social studies classrooms in 25 middle and high schools. Using event-history analysis, they found that authentic teacher questions (questions without a single prespecified answer), uptake of a student's previous answer, and student questions all made open discussion more likely. Student questions had an especially large effect <Cite k="nystrand2003questions" />.

**Secondary science discussions often stay short.** Pimentel and McNeill analyzed whole-class discussions led by five secondary science teachers with 116 students. Student contributions were usually single phrases or short sentences without reasoning. Teachers rarely asked probing questions or "tossed back" a student's idea for others to respond to, and their framing of lessons tended to keep answers brief. In interviews, the same teachers said teacher-driven discussion was not ideal, yet they kept an authoritative stance <Cite k="pimentel2013sciencetalk" />.

**Teaching is hard on the voice.** Roy and colleagues interviewed 1,243 teachers and 1,288 nonteachers chosen at random in Iowa and Utah. Teachers were more likely to report a current voice problem (11.0% vs. 6.2%) and a voice disorder at some point in their lives (57.7% vs. 28.8%). They were also more likely to have seen a physician or speech-language pathologist about their voice (14.3% vs. 5.5%) <Cite k="roy2004voice" />.

The questioning studies describe patterns and associations rather than controlled experiments. Read together, they suggest high school talk improves when teachers explain crisply, then use real questions and follow-ups to hand the reasoning to students.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Structured explanation.** Previewing the steps, giving an example, and summarizing the point make an explanation easier to follow. They also serve the O\*NET task of establishing and communicating clear objectives.
- **Authentic questions.** Questions with more than one good answer invite students to think out loud instead of guessing what the teacher wants.
- **Uptake and toss-back.** Building the next question from a student's answer, or asking another student to respond to it, keeps discussion moving.
- **Voice care.** Teachers report more voice problems than other workers, so managing volume and planning quieter stretches of the day are part of the job.
- **Talking with parents and colleagues.** Conferences about grades and behavior call for specific evidence and a calm, direct tone.

## How to practice

1. **The 90-second explanation.** Pick the hardest concept in this week's unit and explain it out loud in 90 seconds with one example. Time it with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer), then cut anything that does not serve the example.
2. **Rewrite your questions.** Take five questions from a recent lesson plan and rewrite each so it has more than one defensible answer.
3. **Practice the toss-back.** In your next discussion, respond to at least three student answers by asking another student, "What do you think of that?" before adding your own view.
4. **Check your lecture pace.** Read a planned explanation aloud and measure it with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Slowing down for definitions and new terms helps note-takers.
5. **Protect your voice.** Use a gesture or signal to quiet the room instead of raising your voice, drink water between classes, and plan quieter activities after a lecture-heavy period. Persistent hoarseness is worth raising with a physician or speech-language pathologist.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for high school teachers?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.00 out of 5 for this occupation, and it ranks 147th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score.

### Are teachers more likely to have voice problems?

Yes. In a random sample of teachers and nonteachers, teachers reported current voice problems at 11.0% compared with 6.2%, and lifetime voice disorders at 57.7% compared with 28.8%.

### What is an authentic question?

It is a question the teacher asks without a single expected answer, such as "Which cause mattered most, and why?" In studies of eighth- and ninth-grade classrooms, these questions made open discussion more likely.
