Landscaping and groundskeeping workers do relatively little public speaking. O*NET® rates it 1.06 out of 5 for frequency, compared with 3.00 for face-to-face discussions. Speaking importance is 2.38 out of 5. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score of 34 out of 100 is shared by the occupations in positions 908 and 909 of 910. The conversations worth practicing are often short: which area the crew will work in, what has changed on the property and which observation the lead needs before the equipment starts <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Imagine a groundskeeper, Inez, starting at a property the crew visits every week. The resident says a temporary fence has gone up “near the garden.” Someone who heard that in person might know exactly which fence they mean. The coworker with the mowing assignment might picture the garden on the other side of the house. Before they start, Inez can have the resident identify the area on a sketch and show the crew lead where it is. The lead can then incorporate the change into the site's work plan.

The O*NET task list covers mowing and edging lawns, watering plants and maintaining tools and structures. A crew assigned to a single property can therefore be doing quite different things. The person carrying a watering hose may have information that the person assigned to a mower never heard. Naming the work area and the change gives both of them something to check on the ground <Cite k="onet31" />.

## What the research says

**A fixture the crew doesn't know about can matter.** In North Carolina, a lawn-care worker trimming grass was fatally injured when a coworker's mower struck a metal pet tie-out stake. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health identified fixtures or debris in the yard and the direction of the discharge deflector as contributing factors. Investigators recommended examining the area before mowing and marking fixed objects that cannot be removed. In the fictional fence example, asking the resident what has changed can bring something to the crew's attention. It does not replace the inspection <Cite k="socias2016mowerstake" />.

**The background noise changes with the work.** Researchers measured groundskeepers' personal noise exposure at three public universities in North Carolina. Riding mower use was associated with higher noise exposure. The measurements covered work shifts and the researchers also measured sound from equipment. They did not test whether a worker could hear a particular warning. Discussing the plan in a suitable quiet place beforehand is a practical application that doesn't depend on guessing what a coworker can hear over a mower <Cite k="balanay2016groundsnoise" />.

**A briefing can leave a question unanswered.** A New Jersey investigation concerned a tree-service crew, a more specialized setting than routine lawn maintenance. The employer gave the crew instructions at the start. Later a worker on the ground walked into the area beneath a tree, unknown to the crew leader working above, and a falling section struck and killed him. Investigators wrote that the method of communication may not have been clear, even though the crew had been briefed. They recommended that the briefing cover the work zone and how people aloft and on the ground would communicate. The investigation does not establish exactly what each worker heard <Cite k="ohagan2001treebriefing" />.

**Workers can tell a supervisor whether a briefing is usable.** A pilot survey of 67 landscaping and groundskeeping workers employed by public universities in North Carolina found that just over half said they had regular safety meetings. This describes one group of university workers <Cite k="kearney2017groundsmeetings" />. In a separate study, 37 workers and 23 supervisors from small landscaping and tree-care businesses evaluated short safety talks. Sessions took place mainly at North Carolina job sites. Participants gave feedback on terminology, layout and examples. Workers responded well to stories drawn from incidents, and supervisors reported greater confidence in delivering the talks. The researchers assessed how usable the materials were, without measuring injury rates <Cite k="kearney2025landscapetalks" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Give a location someone else can find.** “There's a fence over there” works only for a listener looking at the same spot. “The resident showed me a new fence around the bed beside the south gate” gives the crew lead a place to check. If several crew members use different names for the gate, have them point to it on the same sketch before anyone divides up the work.

**Ask what changed since the last visit.** For the fictional job, Inez might ask whether the temporary fence is the only change in that part of the yard. Repeat back what the resident actually tells you. “The resident identified a fence at the south gate” preserves the source of the report. “The rest of the yard is clear” adds a conclusion no one has established.

**Bring uncertainty back to the lead.** A worker may see that today's assignment overlaps another crew's area and have no authority to choose an alternative. Give the lead the two assignments and say where they overlap. For any actual work around equipment, follow the site's designated work zones, controls and communication procedure. Inventing a new signal as the crew is getting under way leaves everyone else to guess its meaning.

## How to practice

At a table, draw a yard with two gates and two planting beds. Give one partner a fictional customer note saying that a new fence surrounds the bed nearest the south gate. Have that person deliver the message without showing the note. The listener marks the affected bed and writes down one thing that still needs to be checked. Compare the drawing and the note. If the listener chose the opposite bed, try using a fixed landmark instead of “on your left.”

Change roles and give the new speaker a different note: “Another crew is scheduled for the same gate. Its arrival time is unknown.” After hearing the message, the listener says which part is confirmed and who they would ask for the missing time. These are untested paper exercises. They do not involve equipment or substitute for a worksite briefing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should the crew rely on the property owner to identify every obstacle?

NIOSH recommended that employers require an examination of the area before mowing and marking of fixtures that cannot be removed. Information from the property owner can inform that process <Cite k="socias2016mowerstake" />.

### Does a low public-speaking score mean there are no crew meetings?

In the North Carolina university pilot, 56.7% of the 67 groundskeeping and landscaping workers reported regular safety meetings. Reporting how often one addresses an audience measures something different from attending a work briefing <Cite k="kearney2017groundsmeetings,onet31" />.

### What occupation does the wage figure cover?

BLS estimated 952,640 U.S. jobs and a median annual wage of $39,150 for Landscaping and Groundskeeping Workers in May 2025. The estimates describe that occupation across covered employers <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.
