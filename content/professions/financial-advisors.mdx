Financial advice is a spoken service. Clients rarely read a full plan, but they remember what their advisor said in the meeting. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4 out of 5 in importance for personal financial advisors, and the level of oral expression required at 4.5 on a 7-point scale. The occupation ranks 241 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, with a score of 69 <Cite k="onet31" />. Advisors interview clients about income, goals, and risk tolerance, explain strategies and products, and come back periodically to review how things have changed. How those conversations go shapes whether clients follow the advice at all.

In May 2025, 266,800 people worked as personal financial advisors, and the median annual wage was $105,070 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Most of the O*NET task statements for this occupation are conversations with clients <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Interview clients to determine their current income, expenses, insurance coverage, tax status, financial objectives, risk tolerance, or other information needed to develop a financial plan."
- "Answer clients' questions about the purposes and details of financial plans and strategies."
- "Explain to clients the personal financial advisor's responsibilities and the types of services to be provided."
- "Prepare or interpret for clients information, such as investment performance reports, financial document summaries, or income projections."
- "Contact clients periodically to determine any changes in their financial status."
- "Conduct seminars or workshops on financial planning topics, such as retirement planning, estate planning, or the evaluation of severance packages."

Telephone conversations average 4.6 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale and face-to-face discussions 4.12. Public speaking averages exactly 2.0, and no respondents reported presenting daily or weekly, so seminars are an occasional part of the job rather than a routine one. Communicating with people outside the organization rates 4.21 out of 5 for importance.

## What the research says

**Clients judge advisors by the order of the advice.** Agnew and colleagues ran an incentivized experiment on how people assess the financial advice they receive. Participants more often followed advisers who gave good advice first and bad advice later than those who did the reverse. The authors showed that clients' opinions of adviser quality could be manipulated with an easily repeated strategy that depends on the quality, difficulty, and order of advice topics. Clients also learned from their own experience and used professional credentials to guide their choices <Cite k="agnew2018first" />. The setting was online advice, but the lesson about first impressions applies to any advisory conversation.

**Advisors often reinforce clients' biases.** Mullainathan, Noeth, and Schoar used an audit method in which trained auditors met with financial advisers and presented different portfolios. Advisers generally failed to correct their clients' biases and often reinforced biases that served the advisers' financial interests. They encouraged returns-chasing and pushed actively managed funds with higher fees, even when the client started with a well-diversified, low-fee portfolio <Cite k="mullainathan2012advice" />. The study is a working paper, but it is a sobering look at what advisors actually say in first meetings.

**Picking up on what money means to a client strengthens the relationship.** Lozza, Castiglioni, Bonanomi, and Poli surveyed 186 pairs of financial advisors and their clients. Advisors were often able to detect the emotional associations their clients attached to money, and that ability was linked to stronger relationships in terms of satisfaction, trust, willingness to refer others, and loyalty. The authors describe the study as exploratory and based on a convenience sample <Cite k="lozza2022money" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Structuring a first meeting deliberately.** The first impressions experiment suggests that clients weigh early advice heavily. Opening with clear, well-supported points, and saving complex or uncertain topics for after trust is established, matches how clients form judgments. The same research is a reminder that order can be used to manipulate, so the goal is clarity, not salesmanship.

**2. Discovery questions about feelings, not just figures.** "Interview clients to determine ... risk tolerance" involves more than a questionnaire. Asking what a client's money is for, what worries them, and what happened the last time markets fell helps uncover the emotional associations that the dyadic study linked to trust and loyalty.

**3. Correcting a client's mistake respectfully.** The audit study found advisors rarely de-biased clients. Telling a client that their favorite strategy has a problem, and explaining why in plain terms, is a difficult but central skill.

**4. Explaining reports without jargon.** Interpreting performance reports and income projections means turning percentages into what they mean for the client's goals, such as how many years of retirement spending a portfolio supports.

**5. Explaining your own role and incentives.** Explaining "the personal financial advisor's responsibilities and the types of services to be provided" includes being direct about how you are paid, which supports informed trust.

## How to practice

- **Plan the first 10 minutes.** Write the opening of your next new-client meeting and order the topics on purpose. Time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).
- **Feelings questions.** Add three open questions about the meaning of money to your intake routine, such as "What would financial security let you do?" Practice asking them until they sound natural rather than scripted.
- **Translate one report.** Take a real performance report and explain it aloud in under two minutes as if to a client with no finance background. Check your script with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **The difficult correction.** Role-play telling a client that their concentrated stock position is risky. Use the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) to slow down after the key sentence and let the client respond.
- **Seminar rehearsal.** For occasional workshops, rehearse with the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter), then cut your slides down to prompts.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do financial advisors need to be strong public speakers?

Mostly they need strong one-on-one conversation skills. O*NET data shows public speaking averages 2.0 out of 5 for frequency, with no respondents presenting daily or weekly, while phone and in-person client conversations happen most days.

### Why does the order of advice matter so much?

In an experiment by Agnew and colleagues, people were more likely to keep following advisers who gave good advice before bad, which suggests clients form lasting judgments from early interactions.

### Is there research showing that advisors' advice can be biased?

Yes. An audit study by Mullainathan, Noeth, and Schoar found that advisers often reinforced client biases that benefited the adviser, such as chasing past returns and buying higher-fee funds. Similar concerns appear in research on [insurance sales agents](/professions/insurance-agents).
