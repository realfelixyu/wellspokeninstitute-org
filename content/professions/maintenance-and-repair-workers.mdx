General maintenance and repair workers speak more than most trade jobs suggest. They take reports from the people who use a building or a machine, explain what they found, and hand unfinished work to the next shift. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 3.00 out of 5 for importance in this occupation. Face-to-face discussions score 4.76 out of 5 for frequency and telephone conversations 4.61, and communicating with supervisors, peers or subordinates rates 4.36 out of 5 for importance. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 50.8 out of 100, placing the job 657th of 910 occupations <Cite k="onet31" />. The highest-stakes talk is about who is working on what, and whether a machine is safe to start.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

O*NET's core tasks for the occupation include several that run on conversation. Workers "diagnose mechanical problems and determine how to correct them," estimate repair costs, order parts from suppliers and "train or manage maintenance personnel or subcontractors." They maintain equipment in cafeterias, laundries, hospitals, stores, offices and factories <Cite k="onet31" />. Each of those places has people who notice a problem first and describe it in their own words.

Picture a call from a school kitchen: "The dish machine is making a noise again." Before walking over, a maintenance worker can ask three questions that save a trip for the wrong part. What kind of noise? When did it start? Is it still running? At the machine, the worker who locks it out tells the kitchen staff before the power goes off and again before it comes back on.

BLS estimated 1,529,700 general maintenance and repair jobs in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $49,590 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

**Handovers and planned maintenance.** The UK Health and Safety Executive notes that many accidents have followed a failure of communication at shift handover and that most of these involved planned maintenance work. It cites a communication failure between shifts in the 1983 Sellafield beach incident, and the Cullen Report's finding that a failure to pass on information at shift handover was one of many factors in the Piper Alpha disaster. HSE recommends that handover be face-to-face, two-way with both people sharing responsibility, and done with both verbal and written communication, with the incoming person cross-checking the information <Cite k="hse2024shifthandover" />.

**Lockout requires telling people.** Under OSHA's control of hazardous energy standard, affected employees must be notified when lockout or tagout devices are applied and removed, before the controls go on and after they come off. Shift or personnel changes require specific procedures for the orderly transfer of lockout or tagout protection between off-going and oncoming employees <Cite k="osha1910lockout" />.

**Where lockout breaks down.** Prasanna Illankoon and colleagues interviewed technicians and machine operators at a manufacturing organization, held a focus group and observed operators. They describe tagging as keeping employees aware of what is happening with a machine. They found problems in perceiving, understanding and anticipating what was going on at different phases of lockout and tagout <Cite k="illankoon2019lockout" />. Telling an operator what is locked, why and for how long may help with all three, though the study did not test this.

**Stories as a repair tool.** Julian Orr studied technicians who repair photocopiers. Diagnosis involved narrating the process, including the state of the machine. Retelling these stories to colleagues turned one person's experience into shared expertise, and technicians drew on the anecdotes when diagnosing other difficult problems or asking for help <Cite k="orr1986narratives" />. Copier technicians are a different occupation from general maintenance workers, but the habit of telling what you tried, in order, may carry over.

**Hearing from occupants too late.** Shelby Ruiz and colleagues interviewed 72 building operators in seven countries, working in offices, hospitals, campuses, hotels, retail spaces, laboratories and apartment buildings. Operators reported hearing about problems only after occupants had become quite uncomfortable. Those with better feedback channels and direct communication with occupants handled complaints with more grace and understanding <Cite k="ruiz2022operators" />.

**Trust and communication.** A 2019 review of aviation maintenance research by Anna Chatzi and colleagues treated communication and trust as linked preconditions of human error. It examined trust as a prerequisite of effective communication and found little research on the two together <Cite k="chatzi2019maintenancetrust" />. The evidence on how maintenance teams talk is thin, especially outside aviation and process plants.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Taking a problem report.** Ask what it sounds, smells or looks like, when it started and whether it is still running. Repeat the answer back before you choose tools.

**Handing over unfinished work.** Say what is done, what is not, what is locked out and who holds the lock. Then let the incoming worker ask questions and repeat the key points.

**Announcing a lockout.** Tell the people who use the equipment before you shut it down and again before you restart it. "I'm locking out the dish machine now to replace the pump. It'll be down until about 2. I'll tell you before I turn it back on."

**Explaining what you tried.** When you ask a coworker or a supplier for help, give the steps in order: what you saw, what you checked and what happened.

**Giving an estimate a manager can use.** A cost and a time, plus what changes if the first fix does not work.

## How to practice

These drills have not been tested in a study. They follow the tasks and guidance above.

**Face-to-face handover.** Write a job card for an unfinished repair: an air handler with a new belt, but the motor bearing still noisy and the unit locked out. Hand it over aloud to a partner in under two minutes. The partner must repeat back what is locked out, who holds the lock and the next step. Swap roles with a different job card.

**Lockout announcement.** Say a lockout notice aloud to a partner playing a busy operator who keeps working. Include what is being shut down, why, for how long and how they will know it is back on. Repeat the restart notice at the end.

**Ninety-second repair story.** Choose a repair you have done. Tell it to a partner in order: the symptom, what you checked first, what you ruled out and what fixed it. The partner asks one question about a step you skipped. The Institute's guide on [asking a follow-up question](/learn/how-to-ask-a-follow-up-question) has examples. Pausing between steps helps listeners keep track, as explained in [why pausing helps listeners](/learn/why-pausing-helps-listeners).

**Occupant call-back.** A partner plays a tenant who reports "the heat's weird." Ask three questions that turn the complaint into something you can check, then tell the tenant when you will come and what you will look at first.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a maintenance shift handover include?

What was done, what is still open, anything locked or tagged out and who holds the locks, plus any hazards. HSE advises doing it face-to-face, in both directions, with written notes to back up the conversation <Cite k="hse2024shifthandover" />.

### Who needs to be told when equipment is locked out?

The people who operate or work around the equipment. OSHA's standard requires notifying affected employees before lockout or tagout devices are applied and after they are removed <Cite k="osha1910lockout" />. Check your site's written procedure for who else must be told.

### How do I explain a repair problem to a supervisor who is not technical?

Start with the effect ("the walk-in cooler won't stay below 41 degrees"), then the likely cause and what you need: a part, a time and a cost. Keep the technical detail for whoever approves the purchase.
