Veterinarians practice medicine on patients who cannot describe their symptoms, so nearly all the history, the explanation, and the decision-making runs through a conversation with someone else. O*NET® rates speaking importance for veterinarians at 3.88 out of 5 and oral expression at 4.12, and the occupation ranks 155th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Face-to-face discussion receives the maximum frequency rating of 5, and telephone use is close behind at 4.97.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for veterinarians include some of the most emotionally loaded conversations in health care. Veterinarians "counsel clients about the deaths of their pets or about euthanasia decisions for their pets" and "advise animal owners regarding sanitary measures, feeding, general care, medical conditions, or treatment options" <Cite k="onet31" />. They also "educate the public about diseases that can be spread from animals to humans" and train or supervise staff who handle animals.

The importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.65 out of 5. Public speaking is rated 2.29, but 20.94% of veterinarians report doing it daily, a higher daily share than 79 of the 84 health care practitioner occupations in the data.

The veterinary conversation has three features that make it unusual:

- **The client is the historian.** Everything about appetite, behavior, and symptoms at home comes from the owner's observations and interpretation.
- **Cost is part of the clinical plan.** Many recommendations depend on what the client can afford, so money is discussed alongside diagnosis.
- **End-of-life decisions are routine.** Euthanasia conversations require the veterinarian to give a medical opinion and support grief in the same visit.

## What the research says

**Vets use two distinct communication patterns.** Researchers videotaped 300 appointments with 50 companion animal veterinarians in Ontario. 58% of appointments followed a biomedical pattern focused on clinical questions, and 42% followed a broader "biolifestyle-social" pattern. Wellness visits were mostly biolifestyle-social (69%), while problem visits were mostly biomedical (85%). The median relationship-centered care score, the ratio of client-centered to veterinarian-centered talk, was more than twice as high in biolifestyle-social appointments (1.10 vs 0.40) <Cite k="shaw2006vetcommunication" />.

**Vets often skip the client's agenda.** A study of 334 appointments with 20 veterinarians coded how each appointment began. Only 37% included a question inviting the client's concerns. When vets did ask, they interrupted the answer in 55% of cases, mostly with closed questions, after a median of 11 seconds. When the opening question was skipped, the odds of a new concern coming up at the end of the appointment were 4 times as great <Cite k="dysart2011solicitation" />. That leaves the veterinarian choosing between running late, ignoring the concern, or asking the client to come back.

**Clear recommendations get followed.** A follow-up study reviewed 83 videotaped appointments that included a dental or surgical recommendation and checked medical records about six months later. Only 30% of clients had followed through. The odds of adherence were 7 times as great when the veterinarian gave a clear recommendation rather than an ambiguous one. Clients who adhered were also more satisfied and had appointments that scored higher for relationship-centered care <Cite k="kanji2012adherence" />. The study was cross-sectional and small, but the clarity effect was large.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Opening with the client's concerns.** Ask "What would you like to cover today?" and let the client finish. Then ask "Anything else?" before examining. The Dysart data show how much this saves at the end of the visit.

**2. Stating a clear recommendation.** The Kanji study points to one simple change: say what you recommend and why. "I recommend a dental cleaning in the next month because of the infection I'm seeing on this tooth" is clearer than "You might want to think about a dental at some point."

**3. Discussing cost as part of the plan.** When treatment options differ in price, present them plainly with what each would accomplish. Clients can make a decision only if the trade-off is said out loud.

**4. Guiding euthanasia conversations.** These talks call for a slower pace, plain words ("die" rather than euphemisms when the client is ready), and space for the client to decide without pressure. Explain what will happen during the procedure before it begins.

**5. Explaining zoonotic risk calmly.** When educating owners about diseases that can pass to people, give specific precautions without alarming language.

## How to practice

- **Rewrite your recommendations.** Take five recommendations you give often and rewrite each as one clear sentence with a reason. Check them with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
- **Practice an opening question.** Choose one agenda-setting question and use it in every appointment for a week. Notice how often a late concern still appears.
- **Script an end-of-life conversation.** Write out how you explain the euthanasia procedure, read it aloud slowly, and use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) to make sure your pace leaves room for emotion.
- **Practice pausing after hard news.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you hold silence after delivering a diagnosis so the client can respond.
- **Prepare a client education talk.** If you speak to groups about zoonotic disease or preventive care, draft a short talk and time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does how a vet communicates affect whether clients follow advice?

In one study, clients who received a clear recommendation had 7 times the odds of following through on dental or surgical advice. Appointments that led to adherence also scored higher on relationship-centered care.

### How often do veterinarians ask about the client's concerns?

In a study of 334 companion animal appointments, only 37% began with a question about the client's concerns. Skipping that question made new concerns at the end of the visit more likely.

### Is public speaking part of a veterinarian's job?

For some. O*NET rates public speaking at 2.29 out of 5 overall, but about 1 in 5 veterinarians report doing it daily.
