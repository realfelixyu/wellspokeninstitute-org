“Two chicken sandwiches, one with no pickles. Make one spicy.” Before you enter the change, you need one piece of information. “Is the spicy sandwich the one without pickles?” Both sandwiches are accounted for, and the customer can answer without starting the order over.

Taking orders and passing special orders to the kitchen are part of a fast-food or counter worker’s job. O*NET gives Speaking an importance rating of 2.62 out of 5 for this occupation. The frequency rating for face-to-face discussions is 4.36 out of 5. The Institute’s Speaking Demand Score for these workers is 39.7 out of 100 <Cite k="onet31" />.

## When the customer orders by number

If someone asks for “a number four,” say the item name when you check the order. “The fish sandwich meal?” gives the customer a chance to tell you they were looking at the breakfast board. If they’ve already asked for a small iced tea, you can move straight to the choice you still need.

Researchers recorded six real counter transactions at two fast-food restaurants in Honolulu. In one, a worker answered a meal number with the name of the food, and the customer confirmed it. In another, the customer specified size and other details while ordering iced coffee. The worker moved on without asking for those choices again. Workers recapped orders in two of the six exchanges. In one recap, the customer asked about food the worker had left out <Cite k="nguyen2012fastfood" />.

## What if they use another restaurant’s name for an item?

A customer at the drive-through asks for a burger you don’t sell. Ask what they wanted from it. “Was it the one with two patties, or were you looking for the spicy sauce?” Once they answer, name what your kitchen can make and let them choose. If the menu has changed since their last visit, it can help to explain the difference in a few words.

Researchers examined 768 McDonald’s drive-through encounters and 261 Checkers walk-up encounters, alongside orders at coffee shops. At Checkers, they described a customer using McDonald’s terminology and a worker translating the request into the restaurant’s own menu terms. Knowing the other chain’s name gave the worker a place to start. The customer still had to choose from the Checkers menu <Cite k="schau2007codeswitching" />.

## Practice the change on the ticket

Ask a coworker to order two similar items and change one detail as you enter them. The customer can say, “An egg wrap and a bean wrap. Cheese on the bean one. Actually, put the cheese on the egg one instead. Salsa on the side, please.” Once you change the ticket, check it aloud with the customer. “Egg wrap with cheese, bean wrap without cheese, and a side of salsa.” Let your coworker check the ticket against the order they meant to place.

Switch roles and do it again without pointing at the menu. This time the customer can change their mind after the first wrap has been entered. See whether the two of you can identify which line on the ticket needs to change.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I say if someone just names an item?

“Coffee” can be a complete request from the customer’s point of view. If you need a size to ring it up, ask for the size. In a Midwestern college café, researchers recorded 330 customer requests and the responses in field notes. Short requests that left out the verb were common alongside full questions <Cite k="fink2015caferequests" />. On a full order, “Anything else?” and “Was that the sandwich without pickles?” do different jobs. The first checks whether the person has finished ordering. The second checks what belongs on a particular item.

### What is the U.S. job outlook for fast-food and counter workers?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 5.8% employment growth from 2025 to 2035, with an average of 827,400 openings a year. Its separate May 2025 wage survey estimated 3,854,050 jobs and median annual pay of $31,200 for the federal fast-food and counter worker category. That category includes baristas.
