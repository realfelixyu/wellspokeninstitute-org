Your pickup sheet says ten. The shipping clerk tells you the load won't be released until noon. Before you call dispatch, find out what noon means. “Is noon when the freight will be ready, or is that my new appointment?” A driver who clears that up can give dispatch a useful update. The dispatcher may still need to get a different delivery time from the customer.

## Where speaking fits in the job

Heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers score 47 out of 100 on the Institute's Speaking Demand Score. O*NET rates speaking importance at 3 out of 5. Face-to-face discussion has a frequency rating of 4.06 and telephone conversations 3.97, compared with 1.8 for public speaking <Cite k="onet31" />. The Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 2,062,040 U.S. jobs in this occupation in May 2025, with median annual pay of $58,640 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. Its projections show 3.8% employment growth from 2025 to 2035 <Cite k="blsoutlook2035" />.

## What does dispatch need to hear about a delay?

In a U.S. freight study, separate focus groups included company drivers, independent drivers, dispatchers and shippers. Participants described deliveries that kept their original deadline after an earlier appointment held the driver up. Shippers said they preferred being told when a carrier couldn't meet a schedule to getting a promise the carrier couldn't keep <Cite k="duke1997shippers" />. Those were participants’ accounts in a 1997 study. An individual customer can have its own procedure for changing an appointment.

Suppose the shipping clerk says noon is the earliest the freight might be ready. From the parked truck you tell dispatch, “I checked in for the ten o’clock pickup. Shipping says the freight might be ready at noon. Can you check whether the receiver will hold the delivery appointment?” The dispatcher now knows who gave the estimate and which question to take to the receiver. Saying “They moved me to noon” would turn an estimate into a booking.

Researchers in Quebec conducted 17 individual interviews and three group discussions with drivers, dispatchers and other industry participants from 11 organizations. Participants described how much drivers and dispatchers depended on one another and how listening figured in that relationship <Cite k="fournier2012truckdispatch" />. In the pickup example, dispatch has something the driver needs to hear, too. If the receiver asks for another update by eleven, settle who will make that call.

## How do you clear up a mix-up at a shipping desk?

Researchers observed and interviewed five Polish drivers and six Dutch logistics workers at two warehouses in the Netherlands. Drivers described rephrasing a question, drawing what they meant or asking a freight forwarder to help when they couldn't find a shared language. One driver recalled calling his forwarder during a job in Denmark to find someone who spoke Danish <Cite k="dijkstra2021truckers" />.

If a shipping clerk tells you they can't find your number, try looking at the same piece of information together. “This is the number dispatch gave me. It's marked ‘order.’ Does your list use a different number for pickup?” If so, you can ask dispatch for the particular number the clerk needs. Reading the original digits again wouldn't resolve a difference between two kinds of reference number.

## Practice a call about a pickup time

Rehearse off duty with a partner. Write down a ten o’clock pickup and a delivery appointment later that afternoon. Give the driver a note from the shipping clerk that says, “Freight might be ready at noon. Call me back at eleven.” Give the partner playing dispatch a separate note that says, “Receiver wants to know whether to change the delivery slot.”

Make the call. Let the dispatcher write down what they understood and read it back to the driver. Compare the two notes. Did either person hear a confirmed noon pickup? Who will check with shipping at eleven, and who will update the receiver? Repeat the call with one change. This time the clerk has *booked* the noon pickup. Listen for the sentence that tells dispatch the status has changed. The exercise uses a scheduling problem reported by drivers and other freight workers <Cite k="duke1997shippers" />.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need CB radio lingo to talk to dispatch?

For a pickup discrepancy, use the terms on the appointment or order. “Does noon replace my ten o’clock pickup?” lets someone answer a specific question. These studies looked at freight scheduling, dispatcher relationships and warehouse conversations. They didn't test CB vocabulary.

### What if dispatch and the shipping clerk give me different instructions?

Identify each source and the point that differs. “My pickup sheet says the west office. The shipping clerk just sent me to the east office. Can you confirm which one handles this order?” Once you get an answer, give the other person the corrected detail so they know what you were told.
