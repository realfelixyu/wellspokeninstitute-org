For preschool teachers, speaking is less about addressing a group and more about the thousands of small conversations that shape how young children learn language. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.00 out of 5 for importance, the "very important" band, and oral expression 4.00 <Cite k="onet31" />. The occupation ranks 404th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, lower than K-12 teaching, mainly because formal public speaking is rated only 2.53 out of 5 for frequency. Research suggests the informal talk is what counts: a preschool teacher's words and sentences show up in children's language and reading years later.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list for preschool teachers includes teaching "basic skills, such as color, shape, number and letter recognition, personal hygiene, and social skills." Teachers read books to whole classes or small groups, lead storytelling, music, and games, demonstrate activities, and greet arriving children and help them settle into the room. They meet with parents to discuss progress and suggest ways to support learning at home, and they discuss children who show signs of developmental or health problems with parents and specialists <Cite k="onet31" />.

Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.29 out of 5 for frequency, and 25.02% of surveyed teachers report public speaking every day. Telephone use is rated 2.74. Much of the day's talk happens one-on-one or in small groups during play, meals, and transitions.

The job pays a median of 38,140 dollars a year, with 478,780 people employed in May 2025 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. The research below suggests that what these teachers say has effects that last for years.

## What the research says

**Teacher vocabulary in preschool predicted reading in fourth grade.** Dickinson and Porche followed 57 children from low-income families from preschool into fourth grade. Researchers observed classrooms and audio-recorded teacher and child talk. Teachers' use of sophisticated vocabulary during free play predicted children's fourth-grade reading comprehension and word recognition, with the effect passing through children's kindergarten language skills. In large-group settings, teachers' attention-getting utterances were directly related to later comprehension, and analytic talk about books predicted later vocabulary <Cite k="dickinson2011preschool" />. The sample was small and the design correlational.

**Complex sentences from teachers went with faster syntax growth.** Huttenlocher and colleagues studied how adult speech relates to children's grammar. In their second study, children made greater syntactic growth over a year of preschool in classes where teachers' speech was more syntactically complex <Cite k="huttenlocher2002syntax" />. The finding is correlational, but it fits the idea that children learn sentence structure partly from the sentences they hear.

**Training teachers in conversation strategies raised children's vocabulary.** Wasik, Bond, and Hindman trained teachers in 10 Head Start classrooms in specific book reading and conversation strategies. By the end of the year, children in intervention classrooms scored significantly higher than children in control classrooms on a receptive vocabulary test (Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test) and an expressive vocabulary test. Trained teachers also used language-building strategies more often during book reading and other activities <Cite k="wasik2006headstart" />.

**Responsive caregivers get children talking.** Girolametto, Weitzman, and Greenberg randomly assigned 16 child care providers to in-service training or a control group. Trained caregivers learned to respond to children's initiations, engage children in turn-taking, model simplified language, and encourage peer interaction. Afterward, they waited for children to start conversations and included uninvolved children more often, and the children in their care talked more, produced more word combinations, and talked to peers more often <Cite k="girolametto2003daycare" />. It was a small, exploratory study.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Back-and-forth conversation.** Waiting for a child to start, then taking several turns on the child's topic, is the core move in the caregiver training studies.
- **Rich vocabulary in play.** Naming things precisely ("a funnel," "pouring," "overflowing") during free play is the pattern Dickinson and Porche linked to later reading.
- **Complex sentences.** Using "because," "when," and "if" clauses gives children models of multi-part sentences.
- **Interactive book reading.** Asking open questions and talking about words and pictures during a read-aloud turns listening into conversation.
- **Hard conversations with parents.** Raising a developmental concern calls for specific observations, calm language, and a clear next step, such as a referral to a specialist.

## How to practice

1. **Count turns, not words.** During free play, pick one child and try to keep a conversation going for five back-and-forth turns on the child's topic. Repeat with a different child each day.
2. **Plan three words for play.** Before choice time, pick three precise words that fit the materials out that day, and use each one in conversation at least twice.
3. **Stretch your sentences.** When describing what a child is doing, add a reason or a condition: "The tower fell because the bottom block was small."
4. **Rehearse the read-aloud.** Mark two places in the book to stop and ask an open question. Read it aloud once beforehand and check your pace with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator).
5. **Script a concern conversation.** Write down what you observed, when, and what you suggest, in plain words. Paste it into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to catch sentences that are hard to say or hear, then say it out loud before the meeting.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for preschool teachers?

O\*NET rates speaking 4.00 out of 5, which is "very important." The occupation ranks 404th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score because formal public speaking is less frequent than in older grades.

### Does the way preschool teachers talk affect children later?

Studies link it to later outcomes. In one study, preschool teachers' use of sophisticated vocabulary during play predicted children's reading comprehension in fourth grade.

### Can preschool teachers learn to talk in ways that build language?

Yes. In two training studies, teachers and caregivers learned book reading, conversation, and responsiveness strategies, and children in their classrooms showed vocabulary or talking gains compared with control groups.
