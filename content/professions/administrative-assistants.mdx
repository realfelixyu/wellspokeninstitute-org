When a caller asks, “Is the workshop still on Friday?”, saying yes may get them only halfway there. Friday is confirmed, but the venue has changed. An administrative assistant who knows both facts can answer, “Yes, Friday at ten. It's at the library this time. Do you have the address?” The caller has enough information to get to the right place, and the next person who picks up the office phone can give the same answer.

## What the job asks you to say

General secretaries and administrative assistants schedule appointments and provide information to callers. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, telephone conversations have a frequency rating of 4.93 out of 5 for this occupation. Face-to-face discussion scores 4.65, while public speaking scores 1.58 on the same scale. Speaking importance is 4 out of 5. Administrative assistants score 67.2 out of 100 on the Institute's Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />.

These figures cover general administrative assistants and secretaries. The federal classification counts executive, medical and legal secretaries separately. For the general occupation, the Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 1,706,790 jobs and a median annual wage of $47,540 in May 2025 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. BLS projects a 6.0% decline in employment from 2025 to 2035 in its national employment projections <Cite k="blsoutlook2035" />.

## What happens after someone takes the call?

In a 1984 survey of graduates of Arizona State University's office systems and administration program, 51 respondents were classified as secretaries. Of those, 98% counted answering the phone as a moderate or major part of their work, and 89% said the same about writing messages <Cite k="golen1984secretaries" />. The figures describe those graduates' jobs at the time. The distinction between the two tasks is still worth practicing. A caller may have given you enough detail to understand a problem but too little for a colleague to return the call.

A later field study looked at what happened when parents or carers contacted ten school offices. None of the schools had a formal procedure for checking whether someone had followed up on a query passed along by the office <Cite k="thomson2004frontoffice" />. A parent could know who took the message and still have no idea where to ask about it the next day. In the workshop example, a note saying “Lee called about Friday” leaves the next person guessing. “Lee needs to know whether the library entrance opens before ten. Robin at the library is checking. Lee asked for a call back” tells a colleague where to start.

The assistant may also be answering to several people at once. In interviews with six women working as administrative assistants at U.S. colleges and universities, participants described difficulty speaking up and handling demands from faculty <Cite k="listermann2024assistants" />. Suppose two instructors give different answers about whether late arrivals can enter. An assistant can tell the coordinator, “Students have heard both answers. Which one should our office give?” A useful decision is one the next shift can repeat without having to reconstruct the disagreement.

## Practice a schedule change across two shifts

Form a group of three. Choose who will play the caller, the assistant and the colleague coming on shift. On a slip of paper write down a confirmed change, one unresolved detail and the person who can settle it. For example, the workshop has moved to the library, the early opening time is unknown, and the library coordinator handles the doors.

Take the first call. Give the caller the confirmed change. If they need the unresolved detail, find out how they would like to receive the answer. Agree on what you can actually do, such as contacting the coordinator and giving the caller an update that afternoon. Leave the incoming colleague the message you would normally leave.

Have the caller ring again before the coordinator replies. Can your colleague explain what's confirmed and who is checking on the entrance? Let the caller point out any promise from the first conversation that disappeared in the handoff. Try the message again with that detail restored. This drill uses the follow-up problem documented in school offices as a practice scenario <Cite k="thomson2004frontoffice" />.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I say if a caller wants an answer I don't have?

Give them the part you can verify and explain what you will do about the rest. “Your reservation is for Friday. I'll ask the bookings desk whether we can move it to Monday. What number should I use to get back to you?” leaves the reservation status clear.

### How do I tell people about a change without sending them the wrong way?

Repeat the part that stayed the same alongside the change. “Same day and time, different building” makes sense to someone holding the old invitation. Give the new address and update the information your colleagues use when answering calls.

### What should I say if someone wants to speak only to the decision maker?

Ask what decision they need and when they need it. Then explain how you can get their request to the right person. If your work mainly involves managing access to one senior leader, the [executive assistant guide](/professions/executive-assistants) covers that role.
