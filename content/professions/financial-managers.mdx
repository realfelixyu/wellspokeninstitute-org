Financial managers turn numbers into explanations, and much of that explaining is spoken. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4 out of 5 in importance for financial managers, with the level of oral expression required at 4.62 on a 7-point scale. The occupation ranks 139 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, at 70.6 out of 100 <Cite k="onet31" />. The category covers controllers, treasurers, branch managers, and finance directors. They answer questions from executives, boards, lenders, investors, and customers, often in real time and with money riding on how the answer sounds.

In May 2025, 841,710 people worked as financial managers, and the median annual wage was $166,570 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Several O*NET task statements for financial managers are conversations by design <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Communicate with stockholders or other investors to provide information or to raise capital."
- "Establish and maintain relationships with individual or business customers or provide assistance with problems these customers may encounter."
- "Network within communities to find and attract new business."
- "Plan, direct, or coordinate the activities of workers in branches, offices, or departments of establishments, such as branch banks, brokerage firms, risk and insurance departments, or credit departments."
- "Oversee training programs."
- "Recruit staff members."

On the O*NET frequency scale from 1 (never) to 5 (every day), face-to-face discussions average 4.91 and telephone conversations 4.58. Public speaking averages 2.65, with 13.51% of respondents speaking to an audience weekly and 4.13% every day. Dealing with external customers or the public rates 3.34 out of 5 for importance, which reflects the mix of inward-facing roles such as controller and outward-facing roles such as bank branch manager.

## What the research says

The best-studied spoken setting in finance is the quarterly earnings conference call, where a company's executives, usually including its chief financial officer, present results and then take questions from analysts. Four large studies show what that talk does.

**The unscripted Q&A tells investors more than the prepared remarks.** Matsumoto, Pronk, and Roelofsen examined more than 10,000 conference call transcripts. Both the presentation and the discussion segments carried information beyond the accompanying press release, but the discussion period was relatively more informative. When firm performance was poor, managers disclosed more in their presentations, yet relatively more information still came out during the discussion <Cite k="matsumoto2011calls" />.

**Declining to answer is read as bad news.** Hollander, Pronk, and Roelofsen studied what happens when managers leave questions unanswered on conference calls. They found that managers regularly left participants "in the dark" by not answering, and that investors interpret silence negatively, treating no news as bad news <Cite k="hollander2010silence" />.

**Language patterns can signal problems.** Larcker and Zakolyukina built classification models from the words CEOs and CFOs used in conference call discussions, labeling calls as deceptive based on later financial restatements. The models beat a random guess by 6 to 16% out of sample. Executives in calls later linked to restatements made more references to general knowledge, used fewer moderate positive emotion words, and referred less to shareholder value. A portfolio of firms with the highest deception scores from CFO narratives earned an annualized alpha between -4% and -11% <Cite k="larcker2012deceptive" />.

**Even the time of day changes the tone.** Chen, Demers, and Lev used time-stamped earnings calls and linguistic measures of mood. The tone of call discussions became markedly more negative over the trading day, for both executives and analysts, and that time-induced negative tone was followed by temporary stock mispricing <Cite k="chen2018diurnal" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Answering the question that was asked.** The Q&A studies make a plain point: listeners extract the most information from unscripted answers, and they punish evasion. Financial managers who answer directly, then add context, come across as more credible than those who pivot.

**2. Saying "I don't know yet" well.** When a full answer is not possible, a specific statement of what is known, what is not, and when it will be known is better than silence, which investors read negatively.

**3. Concrete, specific language.** The deception research found that vague references to general knowledge were a warning sign. Using exact figures, named drivers, and clear time frames is both more honest and more persuasive.

**4. Explaining numbers to non-finance listeners.** Board members, branch staff, and customers need the "so what" of a variance or a loan decision, not the full model. Translating one key number into a consequence is a core skill.

**5. Relationship conversations.** For branch and commercial banking managers, maintaining customer relationships and networking for new business are spoken, person-to-person work.

## How to practice

- **Hostile Q&A rehearsal.** Before a board meeting or investor call, list the five questions you least want to hear and answer each aloud in under 45 seconds. Time yourself with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer), which works for any timed spoken answer.
- **Script the numbers for the ear.** Prepared remarks full of figures are hard to follow when heard. Run your script through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and break long sentences so each one carries one number.
- **Pace your prepared remarks.** Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) to check that you are not rushing through results. A slower pace gives listeners time to absorb each figure.
- **Mind the clock.** Given the time-of-day research, schedule high-stakes calls and presentations when you are fresh, and rehearse the day before rather than in the hour before.
- **Cut the hedges.** Record a practice answer, transcribe it, and paste it into the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter). Hedging phrases and fillers often cluster around the weakest part of the story.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do financial managers have to speak in public?

Some regularly do. O*NET data shows 13.51% speak to an audience weekly and 4.13% every day, but most of their speaking happens in meetings and calls with executives, staff, and customers.

### What happens if an executive refuses to answer a question on an earnings call?

Research by Hollander and colleagues found that investors tend to interpret unanswered questions as bad news. A partial answer with a clear follow-up plan usually serves a company better than silence.

### Is there research on chief executives' voices too?

Yes. Studies of earnings call audio show that vocal emotion carries information about future performance. See the page on [chief executives](/professions/chief-executives) for that research.
