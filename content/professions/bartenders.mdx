If you've decided to stop serving a guest alcohol, tell them what you've decided before offering anything else. “I can't serve you another beer tonight. I can get you a soda if you'd like.” If you start with “How about a soda?” the guest could reasonably think you're making a suggestion and that the beer is still an option.

O*NET lists taking orders from guests and serving staff among a bartender's duties. Its Speaking importance rating for bartenders is 3.12 out of 5, and face-to-face discussions have a frequency rating of 4.20 out of 5. The occupation scores 56 out of 100 on the Institute's Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Finding out who wants to order

Someone is standing at the bar looking toward you. Are they next, or are they waiting for a friend? In recordings from clubs in Germany, researchers examined 105 moments just before bartenders invited customers to order. Where customers stood and whether they looked toward the bar were useful clues. In a follow-up experiment, people viewing still pictures sometimes mistook a customer looking toward the bar for someone who wanted service <Cite k="loth2013barorders" />.

A short question can settle it. “Are you waiting to order?” If you're halfway through another order, you can acknowledge the person and tell them when you'll be back. Once they've ordered, repeat the detail that changes what you pour. If one guest wants the alcohol-free lager and their friend wants the regular one, say which is which when you set the bottles down.

## How do you cut someone off as a bartender?

Start with a decision you've already made under your workplace's service procedure. A guest might say, “Just make it a small one.” You can answer their actual request. “I can't serve you alcohol tonight, including a small one. Would you like the soda?” Let them decline the soda. If they ask for the manager, tell your colleague what you've already said so the guest gets a consistent answer.

In a U.S. field study, actors portraying intoxicated guests tried to order alcohol. Staff declined 20 of 63 first requests. Across all the refusals the observers recorded, the most common response was to say directly that the person would not be served. Some staff also offered a drink without alcohol. The researchers observed what servers did. They didn't compare scripts to see which one worked best <Cite k="toomey1999barpatrons" />.

## Practice the refusal and the handoff

One person plays the guest, one the bartender and one the coworker covering the break. Give the guest one request the bartender hasn't heard yet. For example, they'd like a glass of water while they wait for their friends. Have the guest ask for another beer first, then tell the bartender what else they want.

After the bartender steps away, have the coworker pick up the conversation. Ask the guest what they understood about the beer and whether they still expect someone to bring the water. If the bartender promised water and the coworker didn't know, repeat the handoff with that detail. This exercise assumes the service decision has already been made.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does the next bartender need to hear?

Suppose you're due for a break and the guest you just spoke with is still at the bar. A useful handoff might be, “I told the guest in the green jacket at the far end that we won't serve them alcohol tonight. They asked to speak with Marta. I've called her, and she said she'll come to the bar.” This gives whoever takes over the guest's outstanding request along with the decision.

Ask your manager before the shift who handles that call. In a randomized study of bars and restaurants, managers rated a statement about whether they had trained staff to refuse alcohol to intoxicated guests. In the training group, 37% strongly agreed at the start and 70% strongly agreed six months later. In the comparison group, those figures were 43% and 56%. Those answers don't tell us how staff handled a particular guest <Cite k="lenk2018managers" />.

### What is the U.S. job outlook for bartenders?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 5.0% employment growth from 2025 to 2035, with an average of 121,200 openings a year. Its separate May 2025 wage survey recorded 756,390 bartender jobs and median annual pay of $34,340 <Cite k="blsoutlook2035,blsoews2025" />.
