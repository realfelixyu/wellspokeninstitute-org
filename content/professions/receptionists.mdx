A receptionist is often the first voice a caller hears and the first person a visitor meets. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 3.88 out of 5 in importance for receptionists and information clerks, and the occupation ranks 436 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, with a score of 63.3 <Cite k="onet31" />. The required level of speaking skill is modest, at 3.0 on a 7-point scale, which fits a job where many exchanges are short and routine. The volume is not modest. Telephone conversations average 5.0 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale, the top of the scale, and face-to-face discussions average 4.38. Those many brief exchanges shape how people feel about the whole organization.

There were 910,180 receptionists and information clerks in May 2025, and the median annual wage was $38,010 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements for receptionists center on calls, greetings, and questions <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Operate telephone switchboard to answer, screen, or forward calls, providing information, taking messages, or scheduling appointments."
- "Greet persons entering establishment, determine nature and purpose of visit, and direct or escort them to specific destinations."
- "Provide information about establishment, such as location of departments or offices, employees within the organization, or services provided."
- "Hear and resolve complaints from customers or the public."
- "Calculate and quote rates for tours, stocks, insurance policies, or other products or services."

Dealing with the public rates 4.32 out of 5 for importance. Public speaking averages 2.05, but 12.13% of respondents said they speak to an audience every day, perhaps reflecting announcements to waiting rooms or groups of visitors, while only 0.46% said weekly.

## What the research says

Most published research on receptionists' spoken work comes from medical practices, where front desk conversations affect access to care. The findings still describe what any receptionist does: manage a queue of people, under time pressure, with incomplete information.

**Routine phrases keep things moving but can block problem-solving.** Hewitt, McCloughan, and McKinstry recorded and analyzed 283 front desk encounters between 16 receptionists and patients at three general practices in Scotland. Most of the talk consisted of verbal routines shaped by the administrative tasks being done, and receptionists stuck to these routines even in non-routine situations. Within those routines, receptionists used three main styles: task-centered, conventionally polite, and rapport-building. No style was more efficient than the others, but task-centered styles could sound over-direct, and excessive reliance on routine phrasing had an adverse effect on solving problems <Cite k="hewitt2009frontdesk" />.

**Receptionists make difficult judgment calls with little support.** Hammond and colleagues conducted 200 hours of ethnographic observation in the reception areas of seven practices in England, involving 45 receptionists. Receptionists had to prioritize patients with little time, information, or training, and they felt responsible for protecting the most vulnerable patients, which protocols and patients "playing" the system sometimes made difficult. The authors argued that calls for more training, without attention to how practices are run, risk blaming individuals for systemic problems <Cite k="hammond2013dragon" />.

**The front desk shapes how welcome people feel.** Neuwelt, Kearns, and Browne held focus groups with 14 receptionists from 11 practices in New Zealand. Receptionists sat at the edge of the practice team yet carried a complex frontline role as de facto managers of the waiting room. Their agency was most visible in how they shaped the social atmosphere of that space and made the experience positive for people who are often marginalized. The authors concluded that well-supported receptionists can improve how acceptable and accessible care feels to patients <Cite k="neuwelt2015receptionists" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. A warm, efficient greeting.** "Determine nature and purpose of visit" is the first task in every encounter. A brief greeting plus one open question gets visitors to the right place quickly and makes them feel noticed.

**2. Knowing when to leave the script.** The front desk study found that routine phrasing works for routine requests but slows down unusual ones. Recognizing when a caller's problem does not fit, and switching to plain, flexible questions, is a key skill.

**3. A friendly phone voice.** With calls rated at the top of the frequency scale, tone carries the organization's reputation. A steady pace, clear enunciation of names and numbers, and confirming what the caller asked for prevent most phone frustration.

**4. Accurate message taking.** Taking messages and scheduling appointments depend on reading back names, numbers, and times to confirm them before ending the call.

**5. Calm complaint handling.** Hearing and resolving complaints at a front desk often happens in front of other visitors. Lowering your voice, acknowledging the problem, and stating what you will do next keeps the situation from escalating.

## How to practice

- **Record your phone greeting.** Say your standard greeting into a recorder and listen back. Check your pace with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator); greetings often get rushed after the hundredth repetition.
- **Read-back drill.** Practice taking a message from a colleague who speaks quickly. Read every name, number, and time back before hanging up.
- **Off-script scenarios.** Write five unusual requests your front desk receives and practice responding to each with open questions instead of a stock phrase.
- **Pause before transferring.** Tell the caller who you are transferring them to and why, then pause briefly before connecting. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) can help you slow down.
- **Short announcements.** For waiting room or lobby announcements, script them and check them with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) so they are easy to understand on first hearing.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for receptionists?

Very, in terms of frequency. Receptionists rate telephone conversations at 5.0 out of 5 and face-to-face discussions at 4.38, even though the level of speaking skill in the O*NET data for the job is moderate.

### Is the research on receptionists only about medical offices?

Most of it is. Studies from general practices in Scotland, England, and New Zealand describe front desk talk in detail, and their observations about routines, prioritizing, and setting the tone apply to many reception settings.

### Should receptionists follow a script?

Scripts help with routine calls. Research on front desk talk found that sticking too rigidly to routine phrasing made non-routine problems harder to solve, so it helps to know when to drop the script. Assistants who support senior leaders face related challenges; see [executive assistants](/professions/executive-assistants).
