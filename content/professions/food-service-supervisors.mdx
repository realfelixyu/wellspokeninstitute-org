Supervising a kitchen or dining room depends on speaking, including more talking to a group than most supervisory jobs involve. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, first-line supervisors of food preparation and serving workers rate speaking 3.88 out of 5 for importance. Their Speaking Demand Score is 68 out of 100, list position 301 of 910. Public speaking scores 3.58 out of 5 for frequency. In a small O*NET sample (25 respondents), roughly 44% reported public speaking every day and about a quarter said they never do <Cite k="onet31" />. For many supervisors this is likely the pre-shift meeting, though O*NET does not say who the audience is. Much of the rest is one to one with staff and guests.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list for this occupation starts with resolving customer complaints about food service. It also includes training workers in food preparation, service, sanitation, and safety; assigning duties and work stations; greeting and seating guests; and observing workers to make sure standards are met, including disciplinary write-ups. Some supervisors hold meetings with other staff about menu planning and serving arrangements <Cite k="onet31" />. Teaching rates 3.95 out of 5 for importance, and phone calls score 4.70 out of 5 for frequency <Cite k="onet31" />.

Here is a pre-shift briefing at a 90-seat restaurant, delivered in about two minutes. "We're out of the short rib, so steer people to the braised chicken. Tonight's special has cashews in the sauce, so if anyone mentions a nut allergy, come get me before you ring it in. Jess is on the patio, Omar has the bar side. Big party of 14 at 7:30." Each item tells someone what to do differently tonight.

## What the research says

**Many restaurant staff haven't been trained on food allergies.** CDC's Environmental Health Specialists Network interviewed personnel at 278 restaurants. Fewer than half of the 277 managers (44.4%), the 211 food workers (40.8%), and the 156 servers (33.3%) reported receiving food allergy training. For those who were trained, common topics included the major allergens and what to do when a customer has a food allergy <Cite k="radke2017allergy" />. A supervisor who names the allergens in the night's dishes at the pre-shift meeting is filling part of that gap out loud.

**Employees look to supervisors for consistent food safety messages.** Arendt and colleagues surveyed foodservice employees and held focus groups about their supervisors' role in food safety. Four themes emerged: consistency, training, managers' behaviors, and employees' behaviors <Cite k="arendt2014foodsafetysupervisors" />. In the version of the paper we read, more than half of focus group participants said they preferred to be trained by a manager or supervisor rather than by a coworker or an outsider <Cite k="arendt2014foodsafetysupervisors" />.

**Workers often come in sick, and staffing pressure is one reason.** In workplace interviews with 491 food workers at 391 restaurants in nine states, almost 60% recalled working while ill at some point. One in five said they had worked a shift with vomiting or diarrhea in the previous year, symptoms for which the U.S. Food and Drug Administration recommends excluding workers. Concern about leaving coworkers short staffed and fear of losing the job were among the factors linked to working while sick <Cite k="carpenter2013workingill" />.

**Managers are often not part of the decision.** A companion study interviewed 426 restaurant managers. In many restaurants, managers were not actively involved in deciding whether an ill worker should work, and a third of restaurant policies did not say when sick workers should be excluded. Almost 70% of the managers said they had worked while ill themselves <Cite k="norton2015illworkers" />. A supervisor who asks directly about symptoms, and says plainly that the shift will be covered, is addressing the reasons workers gave for coming in.

**Some difficult encounters start with the customer.** Bitner, Booms, and Mohr analyzed 774 critical service encounters reported by employees in hotels, restaurants, and airlines. Along with the sources customers had already described, employees identified another cause of dissatisfaction: the customer's own misbehavior <Cite k="bitner1994employeeview" />. Supervisors are often the ones called to the table when that happens.

**Fixing a complaint fully is linked to satisfaction and to guests saying they'll return.** Susskind coded restaurant patrons' accounts of times they complained about service. The extent to which the failure was corrected was important to customer satisfaction, and satisfaction with the remedy was connected to the desire to return to the restaurant <Cite k="susskind2005restaurantcomplaints" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Running a short pre-shift briefing.** Cover what is out, what changed, who is where, and any allergen in a special, in two or three minutes.
- **Teaching a procedure by showing and saying.** Demonstrate the step, name why it matters, and have the worker do it back. This fits the O*NET task of training workers in sanitation and safety.
- **Asking about illness directly.** Use plain words about symptoms and tell the worker what happens to the shift if they go home.
- **Handling a complaint at the table.** Listen to the whole complaint, say what you will do, and make sure it happens.
- **Giving correction privately and specifically.** Name the step that was missed and what you need next time.

## How to practice

Treat these drills as untested ideas. They draw on the studies above, but no one has measured their effect.

**The two-minute lineup.** Write tonight's briefing for an invented restaurant with one 86'd item, one special containing a major allergen, section assignments, and one large reservation. Deliver it to a colleague, then ask them to repeat the allergen and their section. Use the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to keep the draft near two minutes.

**The allergy relay.** One person plays a guest who says, "I'm allergic to shellfish. Is the risotto okay?" Another plays the server, and you play the supervisor they bring the question to. Practice what you say to the guest, what you say to the kitchen, and what you say back to the server. Then switch the allergen and repeat. The [restaurant servers](/professions/restaurant-servers) and [restaurant cooks](/professions/restaurant-cooks) pages cover the other two ends of this exchange.

**The sick call.** A partner plays a line cook who arrives and says, "I threw up this morning, but I feel fine now." Practice a response that asks about symptoms, states the policy, and says who will cover: "Thanks for telling me. With vomiting this morning you can't work the line today. I'll call Dev to cover, so the station's handled."

**The table visit.** A partner plays a guest whose steak came out well done instead of medium rare after a 25-minute wait. Let them finish, then state the fix and the timing. Ask your partner afterward whether they felt the problem had been corrected or only apologized for.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do food service supervisors have to speak in front of groups?

Often. In a small O*NET sample (25 respondents), roughly 44% reported public speaking every day and about a quarter said they never do <Cite k="onet31" />. BLS estimated 1,223,240 jobs in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $44,080 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

### How do I talk to a worker who might be sick?

Ask about specific symptoms and tell them how the shift will be covered. Workers interviewed in nine states named leaving coworkers short staffed and fear of job loss among the reasons for working while ill <Cite k="carpenter2013workingill" />.

### What should I say to a guest who complains?

Hear the full complaint, then say what you will fix and when. In a study of restaurant complaints, the extent of correction mattered to satisfaction, and satisfaction with the remedy was connected to wanting to return <Cite k="susskind2005restaurantcomplaints" />.
