Janitors and building cleaners rarely speak to groups, and the speaking that matters is reporting: telling a supervisor about a leak, a broken lock or a cleaning product that irritates your skin, and making sure a change to your route reaches you correctly. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 3.00 out of 5 for importance in this job. Face-to-face discussions score 4.40 out of 5 for frequency, while public speaking scores 1.54. Communicating with supervisors, peers or subordinates rates 4.15 out of 5 for importance. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 43.4 out of 100, which ranks the occupation 854th of 910 <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Several O*NET tasks for janitors depend on a clear spoken report. Workers "notify managers concerning the need for major repairs or additions to building operating systems." They monitor building security and safety, follow procedures for chemical cleaners and power equipment, and requisition supplies or equipment <Cite k="onet31" />.

A typical evening might go like this. At 9:40 p.m. a janitor finds water spreading under the sink in the third-floor women's restroom. A text that says "leak 3rd floor" leaves the building manager guessing. A spoken or voicemail report can carry everything needed: "Third-floor women's restroom, water coming from the pipe under the second sink. I shut the valve under the sink, mopped up and put out a wet-floor sign. The valve is holding, but the pipe needs a plumber before the morning." Location, what you saw, what you did and what still needs doing.

BLS estimated 2,209,760 janitor and cleaner jobs in May 2025, excluding maids and housekeeping cleaners, with a median annual wage of $36,840 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

**Who janitors are, and what goes unreported.** A Washington State survey led by Naomi Anderson completed 620 interviews with janitors, offered in eight languages. Twenty percent reported a work-related injury or illness diagnosed by a health care provider in the previous 12 months, and half of those injured did not file a workers' compensation claim. Almost half (48.8%) had a regular shift starting between 5 and 9 p.m. About 20% said specific cleaning products had caused eye, skin or breathing problems. Among injured janitors who did not file, the most common theme was employer suppression or retaliation, actual, threatened or feared. Many injured janitors never filed a claim, and the survey did not ask whether product reactions were reported at all <Cite k="anderson2021janitorsurvey" />.

**Messages that pass through several people.** Maiju Strömmer followed an office cleaner in Finland whose company provided outsourced cleaning. He was usually the only cleaner on the client's premises, and his contact with the people around him was mostly limited to greetings. Employees with requests usually went to their manager instead of telling him. When the cleaning routine changed, the client's contact emailed the cleaning company's manager, who called his supervisor, who then explained the change to him, often by phone <Cite k="strommer2016cleaningaffordances" />. Repeating the change back may help; the study did not test this.

**Showing and pointing.** In a second study at two cleaning work sites, Strömmer described coworkers supporting colleagues who were still learning Finnish. A supervisor demonstrated how to lift floor tiles and pointed to cords and a table leg. When a team discussed swapping evening shifts, the coworker who asked restated the question more briefly and simply and pointed to the weeks on a calendar, and the cleaner being asked repeated the suggestion to make sure he had understood it. Strömmer suggested that cleaning work could be done with limited Finnish when guided support was available, though the two workers she followed spoke intermediate Finnish <Cite k="strommer2016materialscaffolding" />.

**Cleaning a school.** Kirsten Hviid and colleagues studied immigrant cleaners at a Danish school during a workplace intervention that included language courses. Cleaners described classrooms that were not ready to clean, with chairs not put up on the tables and dirt or water left on the floor. Teachers said they mostly greeted the cleaners in passing rather than talking with them. One cleaner said she felt noticed when the cleaning manager knew everyone's names and asked how they were doing <Cite k="hviid2012cleanersvisibility" />.

**Cleaning while others work.** Karin Sardadvar studied daytime cleaning in Austria, where cleaners work while the client's employees are present. Those employees, though not formally in charge, shaped cleaners' working conditions by monitoring them, imposing their own schedules and reproducing inequality in everyday interaction <Cite k="sardadvar2026daytimecleaning" />. Karen Messing's interviews with hospital cleaners in Quebec described the same invisibility and a lack of respect from others in the building, with consequences for cleaners' and patients' health <Cite k="messing1998hospitaltrash" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**A four-part problem report.** Where, what you saw, what you did, what is still needed. This fits the task of notifying managers about repairs.

**Reporting a product reaction or an injury.** "When I used the floor stripper in the gym tonight my eyes burned and I started coughing. I stopped, opened the doors and finished the hallway instead." Name the product, the symptom and the time.

**Confirming a change to your route.** Repeat it back with the room and the day: "So I skip 214 on Thursday and do the library twice. Who do I tell if it's locked?"

**Speaking up with the people in the building.** A short, polite request to the person in an office ("I'll be vacuuming here in 10 minutes. Is that okay, or should I come back after 6?") makes the next visit easier.

## How to practice

These drills are untested. They come from the tasks and studies above.

**Repair voicemail.** Choose a problem from a list: a leak, a burned-out exit sign, a door that will not lock. Leave a 30-second practice voicemail on your own phone using the four-part report. Play it back and check that someone who has never seen the room could find the problem.

**Relay chain.** Work in groups of four. The first person gets a written route change with three details (room, day, replacement task). It passes by voice from person to person. The last person repeats it to the first. Compare. Then try again, with each listener repeating the message back before passing it on.

**Classroom request.** A partner plays a teacher who has left chairs down for the third night in a row. Ask for a change in two sentences, without blaming, and suggest something concrete, such as chairs up on Fridays. The Institute's guide on [asking someone to repeat what you missed](/learn/how-to-ask-someone-to-repeat-what-you-missed) is useful if the teacher answers quickly.

Hotel housekeepers deal with many related situations. See the Institute's page on [maids and housekeeping cleaners](/professions/maids-and-housekeeping-cleaners).

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a janitor include when reporting a repair?

The exact location, what you saw, anything you did to make it safe and what still needs fixing. A report with all four lets a manager decide whether to call someone tonight or in the morning.

### What if I get a rash or cough from a cleaning product?

Stop using it, tell your supervisor which product, what happened and when, and ask what to use instead. In the Washington survey, about 20% of janitors said specific products had caused eye, skin or breathing problems <Cite k="anderson2021janitorsurvey" />.

### Do janitors need strong English to do the job?

Not necessarily at the start. In a study at Finnish work sites, the author suggested cleaning work can be done with limited Finnish when coworkers demonstrate and point; the two workers she followed spoke intermediate Finnish <Cite k="strommer2016materialscaffolding" />. Reporting problems and injuries is where clear speaking matters most.
