A dietitian who asks “What do you have for breakfast?” may picture someone who just got out of bed. A night-shift worker might use “breakfast” for the meal she shares with her children just before she goes to sleep. Ask her to walk you through the last shift she worked. When she has finished, tell her which part of the day you understood her to mean and give her time to correct it.

## Ask about a day the patient remembers

Suppose the patient says she usually has lunch at work. You ask, “When was lunch on your last shift?” She says it was two in the morning. “What was happening at work around then?” gives her room to explain that a coworker covered the desk so she could take her break. You now know what would have to happen for that break to be possible on another shift. You can also record what she calls the meal without quietly changing the time to 2 p.m.

Questions sometimes carry an explanation the speaker didn't intend to put there. Researchers in the Netherlands analyzed recorded consultations between seven dietitians and 17 older clients with malnutrition or at risk of it. When the question suggested a reason for weight loss, clients sometimes offered a different account. Some explained why previous advice had been impractical. Their longer answers contained information that a simple agreement would have missed <Cite k="barkmeijer2024intake" />.

In an Australian community clinic, researchers examined video recordings of 20 dietitian-client consultations. Clients sometimes described what they ate in terms of being good or bad. The dietitian's responses included reassurance and sequences of questions that left the circumstances unexplored <Cite k="meyer2026casetaking" />. In our shift example, if the patient says “I was terrible on Thursday,” a question you could try is “What happened on Thursday?” Let her decide where to begin.

## Check what the patient will take away

Imagine you've asked this patient to describe one work shift at the next visit. She agrees. Before you part, try “I want to check how I explained that. Which shift would you tell me about, and where would your account start?” She might reply, “I thought you needed a week of menus.” That answer gives you a chance to clear up the amount of work you asked her to do. You can agree on one shift and ask how she'd prefer to remember it.

A Canadian study recorded 19 consultations, each involving a different dietitian and patient. Three raters assessed the conversations. Exploring what patients expected was one of the higher-scoring behaviors. Asking how the patient preferred to receive information was among the lowest. The raters also looked for attempts to check the patient's understanding <Cite k="vaillancourt2012involvement" />. The question about the next shift is an Institute exercise for practicing that last skill.

## Rehearse with two clocks

Draw a line across a sheet of paper and mark midnight in the middle. One person plays a patient who starts work at 8 p.m. and finishes at 6 a.m. On a separate sheet, the patient chooses when a break happens, what they call the meal and who covers their station. Keep that sheet folded.

The dietitian has two minutes to draw the shift on the first sheet using the patient's answers. Read the result aloud before opening the original. If the times disagree, circle the first word that sent the conversation astray. Was it “yesterday,” “lunch” or “after work”? Ask one more question about that word. For the second round, switch roles and change the break time. Finish by asking the patient to describe what they will bring up at the next visit.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if there is no typical day?

Start with a recent day the patient can place. Once you have that account, ask for a day that went differently. “Who was covering the desk that night?” can uncover a difference in routine that an average mealtime would hide.

### How do I check understanding without putting the patient on the spot?

Tell them which part of your own explanation you're checking. “What would you bring to our next conversation?” gives the patient something concrete to answer. When their answer differs from what you meant, acknowledge which part you left unclear and try again.
