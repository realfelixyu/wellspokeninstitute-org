Speaking is a large part of supervising an office. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, first-line supervisors of office and administrative support workers rate speaking 4.00 out of 5 for importance, and both face-to-face discussions and phone calls score 4.93 out of 5 for frequency. Their Speaking Demand Score is 69 out of 100, list position 226 of 910 occupations <Cite k="onet31" />. Most of that talk is short and one to one: correcting an error, explaining a rule, or taking over a call a team member can't settle.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list for this occupation reads like a list of conversations. Supervisors discuss job performance problems with employees to find causes, interpret and communicate procedures and policies to staff, guide employees through difficult problems and escalated complaints, and resolve customer complaints themselves. They also train staff, interview job candidates, and coordinate with supervisors in other departments <Cite k="onet31" />.

Formal speeches are rare. Public speaking scores 2.04 out of 5 for frequency, far below face-to-face talk <Cite k="onet31" />. The skill lies in brief exchanges that need to work the first time.

Picture a claims office. Dana, a team lead, notices that three of the last ten files Marcus closed went out without a signed release. She could say, "You keep missing releases." Or she could start with the record and a question: "Three of these ten went out without the release. Can you walk me through how you close a file?" The second version points at the task and asks for his account before she decides what went wrong.

## What the research says

**Critical feedback feels awkward even when it's expected.** Asmuss examined how supervisors gave critical feedback in performance appraisal interviews, where it mostly took the form of negative assessments. Participants treated that feedback as socially problematic despite the formal setting. The more a supervisor signaled that a negative assessment was delicate, the harder it became for the employee to deal with it <Cite k="asmuss2008appraisal" />.

**Feedback can backfire.** Kluger and DeNisi's meta-analysis covered 607 effect sizes and 23,663 observations. Feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41), yet more than a third of them lowered performance. Their moderator analyses suggested feedback worked less well as it pulled attention away from the task and toward the self <Cite k="kluger1996feedback" />. For a supervisor, that favors talking about the file, the step, or the deadline rather than the person.

**Having a say goes with better reactions.** Cawley, Keeping, and Levy pooled 27 field studies with 32 samples on participation in performance appraisals. The corrected relationship between participation and employee reactions was .61, and it was strongest for satisfaction. Participation for the sake of being heard had a stronger link with most reactions than participation aimed at changing the result <Cite k="cawley1998participation" />. These are correlations from field settings, so they show association rather than proof that inviting comment causes the better reactions.

**An explanation changes how a decision lands.** Shaw, Wild, and Colquitt meta-analyzed 54 independent samples and found strong effects of explanations on fairness judgments and on responses such as cooperation, retaliation, and withdrawal. Explanations helped more after unfavorable outcomes, and excuses helped more than justifications <Cite k="shaw2003explanations" />. When a supervisor turns down a schedule request, the reason given is part of the message.

**Listening counts in supervisor conversations.** Kluger and Itzchakov's review describes listening as associated with, and a likely cause of, outcomes such as job performance, trust, and well-being. They treat it as something that benefits both people in a pair, including a supervisor and an employee <Cite k="kluger2022listeningwork" />.

**Front-line staff absorb a lot of customer anger.** In a study of 198 call center employees, staff reported customer verbal aggression about 10 times a day on average. Both how often it happened and how stressful employees judged it were related to emotional exhaustion <Cite k="grandey2004aggression" />. When you take over a hostile call, consider a short check-in afterward with the person who passed it along.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Opening a correction with the record.** State what you saw, with a count or an example, then ask for the employee's account. This matches the O*NET task of discussing performance problems to identify causes.
- **Giving the reason with the decision.** When you announce a new procedure or decline a request, state the reason in a sentence or two.
- **Taking over a hard call.** Introduce yourself, show you already know the details so the customer doesn't have to repeat them, and say what you can and can't do.
- **Asking, then waiting.** In appraisals and check-ins, let the employee finish before you summarize.
- **Asking another department for something specific.** When another unit's delay is holding up your team, say what you need and by when.

## How to practice

These drills are untested. They apply the findings above to everyday office situations, and no study has measured whether they work.

**The two-sentence correction.** Write down an invented error from your area, such as an invoice coded to the wrong cost center. Draft an opening that states the observation in one sentence and asks a question in the second. Say it aloud to a colleague playing the employee, and ask which words made them feel defensive. Rewrite it and try again.

**The policy-change briefing.** Pick a change, such as moving the cutoff for same-day requests from 3 p.m. to 2 p.m. Give a colleague a one-minute explanation of what changes, when it starts, and why. The [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) can tell you whether your draft fits in about a minute. Ask your listener to repeat the new cutoff and the reason back to you.

**The warm handoff.** One person plays a caller upset about a refund that was promised for the 10th and still hasn't arrived. Another plays the staff member who brings in the supervisor mid-call. The supervisor's first line should show they know the case: "Hi, this is Priya, I supervise this team. I understand your refund was due on the 10th and hasn't come through." Swap roles and compare how much the caller had to repeat. The [customer service representatives](/professions/customer-service-representatives) page covers the same call from the front-line side.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much of an office supervisor's job is speaking?

A large share. Face-to-face discussions and phone calls each score 4.93 out of 5 for frequency in O*NET data <Cite k="onet31" />. BLS estimated 1,436,680 jobs in the occupation in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $69,500 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

### Should I soften critical feedback?

Some softening is normal. Research on appraisal interviews found that the more a supervisor treated a negative assessment as delicate, the harder it was for the employee to respond <Cite k="asmuss2008appraisal" />. A plain, specific observation followed by a question gives the employee something concrete to answer.

### Do I need to explain every decision to my team?

Routine ones rarely need much. For decisions people won't welcome, a meta-analysis found explanations had larger effects after unfavorable outcomes <Cite k="shaw2003explanations" />.
