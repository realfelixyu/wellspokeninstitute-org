Librarians spend much of the day in short spoken exchanges with the public, and the quality of those exchanges shapes what patrons walk away with. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.00 out of 5 for importance for librarians and media collections specialists, and the occupation ranks 142nd of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.93 out of 5, close to every day. Research on the reference interview shows that how a librarian asks and listens affects both the answer a patron gets and how the patron feels about asking.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list puts conversation with patrons at the center. Librarians answer reference questions and "analyze patrons' requests to determine needed information." They explain how to use library facilities, resources, and services, respond to complaints, and teach classes on information literacy, library instruction, and technology. They plan and deliver programs such as storytelling for children, confer with teachers about course materials, and represent the library on committees <Cite k="onet31" />.

Dealing with the public is rated 4.44 out of 5 for importance. Formal public speaking is less frequent, at 2.85 out of 5, and no surveyed librarians reported it as a daily task, although 18.52% reported it weekly. Telephone use is rated 3.68. Most library talk is one-on-one at a desk, in the stacks, or in a classroom, plus regular performances for children.

## What the research says

**Users remember the relationship, librarians remember the content.** Marie Radford analyzed 47 critical incidents recalled by 27 academic library users and 9 librarians, using a framework from interpersonal communication theory. Relationship themes, such as attitude, approachability, and the quality of the interaction, were major themes in the incidents users recalled. Librarians gave relatively more weight to the information provided, although they also saw the relationship as important. Radford argued that reference work should be seen as both an exchange of information and a relationship <Cite k="radford1996reference" />.

**The way a librarian asks "why" matters.** Dewdney and Michell examined a long-standing dilemma: a patron's reason for asking often clarifies what they really need, yet librarians are commonly advised never to ask "Why do you want this information?" Drawing on linguistics and speech act theory, they showed how the ambiguity of "why" questions can provoke unproductive or even hostile answers. They proposed two strategies: explaining the reason for the question first (contextualization), and using neutral questions that ask about the situation behind a request <Cite k="dewdney1997why" />. This is a theoretical analysis, not an experiment.

**Interpersonal skills carry over to chat.** In a later study, Radford analyzed 245 randomly selected transcripts from a statewide chat reference service, along with 44 transcripts nominated for an award. Interpersonal skills known to matter at the desk appeared in modified form online, including rapport building, signs of deference and respect, face-saving tactics, and greeting and closing rituals. Librarians also used strategies to make up for the lack of nonverbal cues <Cite k="radford2006chat" />. The relational habits that make a desk encounter work also show up in text-based service.

**What happens at storytime is linked to children's early literacy.** The VIEWS2 project, led by Campana and colleagues, collected data at 120 public library storytimes, observing about 1,440 children from birth to 5 years old. In the first year of data, the early literacy content of a storytime was correlated with children's early literacy behaviors during the program. Children responded to the literacy content that storytime providers built in <Cite k="campana2016storytimes" />. The design was quasi-experimental, so the findings show association.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Approachability.** Eye contact, a greeting, and a tone that invites a question are part of the service. Relationship themes were prominent in what Radford's users remembered.
- **Open and neutral questions.** Questions like "What are you working on?" or "How will you use this?" draw out the real need behind a vague request without sounding like a challenge.
- **Checking understanding.** Restating the request before searching, and asking at the end whether the answer fully meets the need, prevents a polite but unhelpful exchange.
- **Teaching out loud.** Library instruction sessions require short demonstrations and plain explanations of search strategies.
- **Storytime delivery.** Reading expressively and pausing to talk about words, sounds, and pictures builds the early literacy content linked to children's responses.

## How to practice

1. **Build a neutral question bank.** Write five open, neutral questions you can use when a request is vague, and keep them at the desk for a week. Note which ones get the most useful answers.
2. **Add a context line before "why."** Practice saying "So I can find the best sources, can you tell me a bit about the project?" until it comes out naturally.
3. **Use a closing question.** End every reference exchange for one week with "Does this answer your question?" and track how often the answer is no.
4. **Rehearse storytime.** Mark two places in each book to pause and talk about a word or sound. Read it aloud at the pace you plan to use and check it with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator).
5. **Time instruction sessions.** Script the demonstration portion of your next class and measure it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator), leaving most of the session for hands-on practice.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for librarians?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.00 out of 5 for this occupation, and it ranks 142nd of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score.

### What is a reference interview?

It is the conversation a librarian has with a patron to find out what information they actually need. A short opening request often differs from the real question, so the librarian asks follow-up questions before searching.

### Should librarians ask patrons why they want information?

Researchers have argued that "why" questions can help but are easily heard as a challenge. Explaining the reason for asking, or using neutral questions about the patron's situation, can get the same information more smoothly.
