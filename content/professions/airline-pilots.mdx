Airline pilots speak less than teachers or lawyers, but what they say has to be exact. O\*NET® data rate speaking 3.75 out of 5 for importance for airline pilots, copilots, and flight engineers, and the occupation ranks 376th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking is rated 3.73 out of 5 for frequency, and 56.68% of surveyed pilots report it every day; their task list includes crew briefings and passenger announcements. Research on cockpit and radio communication shows that indirect phrasing and long, complex messages are linked to errors, and that crew communication training changes behavior.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list for pilots includes contacting "control towers for takeoff clearances, arrival instructions, and other information, using radio equipment." Pilots work as part of a flight team, especially during takeoffs and landings, brief crews on destinations, duties, and responsibilities, direct crew activities during flights, and respond to and report in-flight emergencies. They confer with dispatchers and weather forecasters, instruct other pilots, and make announcements over the public address system <Cite k="onet31" />.

Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.62 out of 5 for frequency. Oral expression is rated 4.00 out of 5 for importance, with a required level of 4.38 on a 7-point scale. Much of a pilot's talk follows standard phraseology and checklists, which is why small departures from the standard, such as an unclear readback or a hint instead of a direct statement, are worth studying.

## What the research says

**Indirect suggestions to the captain fail more often.** Charlotte Linde analyzed transcripts from eight aviation accidents and fourteen flight simulator sessions, rating how mitigated, or softened, each utterance was. Speech going up the chain of command was more mitigated than speech going down. Utterances that introduced a new topic were more likely to fail when mitigated than when direct, and suggestions from a crew member to the captain were more likely to fail when mitigated <Cite k="linde1988mitigation" />. A hinted concern from a first officer is easier for a captain to miss than a plain statement.

**Readback problems are rare but tied to long messages.** Morrow, Lee, and Rodvold coded voice tapes of routine controller-pilot communication during terminal operations. They looked at procedural deviations, such as partial readbacks, and inaccuracies, such as incorrect readbacks. Both kinds of problem were infrequent, but they were associated with the length and composition of air traffic control messages and with traffic level <Cite k="morrow1993controller" />. The link with message length suggests that how an instruction is packaged affects how reliably it is heard and repeated.

**Crew resource management training changes reactions, learning, and behavior.** Salas, Burke, Bowers, and Wilson reviewed 58 published accounts of crew resource management (CRM) training, which teaches communication, coordination, and decision-making on the flight deck. CRM generally produced positive reactions, improved learning, and promoted the desired behavioral changes. The authors could not determine whether it improved safety, because many evaluations did not collect data at that level <Cite k="salas2001crm" />.

**A meta-analysis confirmed large effects.** O'Connor and colleagues meta-analyzed 16 CRM evaluation studies. Trainees rated the training positively, averaging 4 on a 5-point scale. CRM had large effects on attitudes and behaviors and a medium effect on knowledge. The authors called for more rigorous reporting so future reviews could measure effects more precisely <Cite k="oconnor2008crm" />.

The evidence supports direct, standardized, and well-structured speech in the cockpit. The link from training to fewer accidents is plausible but harder to prove, because accidents are rare.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Speaking up directly.** Stating a concern plainly, with the problem and a proposed action, is less likely to be missed than a hint. Linde's findings apply to both captains inviting input and first officers giving it.
- **Complete, accurate readbacks.** Reading back every element of a clearance lets controllers catch errors. Asking for a long instruction to be split or repeated is part of good practice.
- **Clear crew briefings.** A briefing that covers the plan, threats, and each person's role sets up the team before workload rises.
- **Calm announcements.** Cabin announcements should be short, slow, and plain, especially during delays or unusual events.
- **Teaching and debriefing.** Instructing other pilots and reviewing a flight afterward both depend on specific, respectful feedback.

## How to practice

1. **Rewrite hints as statements.** Take three phrases you might use to raise a concern, such as "Do you think we're a bit high?", and rewrite them as direct statements with a proposed action. Practice saying them in a level tone.
2. **Drill readbacks.** Have a partner read realistic clearances of increasing length, and read back each one in full. Note the length at which errors start to creep in.
3. **Time your briefing.** Write a standard departure briefing and time it with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer). Cut anything that does not change what the crew will do.
4. **Pace your announcements.** Record a cabin announcement and check it with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). A slower pace is easier to hear over cabin noise.
5. **Practice from a script.** Rehearse delay and diversion announcements from the [free teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) until you can deliver them calmly without reading word for word.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for airline pilots?

O\*NET rates speaking 3.75 out of 5, which is "quite important." The occupation ranks 376th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, but daily public speaking is common because of briefings and announcements.

### What is mitigated speech in the cockpit?

It is softened or indirect phrasing, such as hinting at a problem instead of stating it. In accident and simulator transcripts, mitigated suggestions to the captain were more likely to fail.

### Does crew resource management training work?

Reviews find that it improves pilots' reactions, learning, attitudes, and behaviors. Whether it reduces accidents is harder to show, because many evaluations did not measure safety outcomes.
