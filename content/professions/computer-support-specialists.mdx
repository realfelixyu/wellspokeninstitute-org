Speaking is close to the center of IT support work. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking and oral expression both rate 4 out of 5 in importance for computer user support specialists, and the occupation ranks 219 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score with a score of 69 <Cite k="onet31" />. Support specialists diagnose problems they usually cannot see, using only what a stressed user can describe, and then talk that user through a fix. The technical knowledge matters, but it reaches the user only through conversation.

The occupation employed 717,190 people in May 2025, at a median annual wage of $61,860 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Several of the O*NET task statements for this occupation describe conversations directly <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Answer user inquiries regarding computer software or hardware operation to resolve problems."
- "Read technical manuals, confer with users, or conduct computer diagnostics to investigate and resolve problems or to provide technical assistance and support."
- "Confer with staff, users, and management to establish requirements for new systems or modifications."
- "Develop training materials and procedures, or train users in the proper use of hardware or software."
- "Refer major hardware or software problems or defective products to vendors or technicians for service."

The work context data shows how constant this talk is. Face-to-face discussions average 4.83 on a 1 to 5 frequency scale, and telephone conversations average 4.61, both close to "every day." Formal public speaking is uncommon at 2.03, with no respondents reporting it daily. Dealing with external customers or the public rates only 2.01 out of 5 for importance, which fits the many support specialists who serve colleagues inside their own organization rather than outside customers. Training others is part of the role too: training and teaching others rates 3.38 out of 5 for importance.

## What the research says

**The first two minutes predict the caller's satisfaction.** Hall and colleagues analyzed the opening two minutes of 84 calls that employees made to their company's help desk, handled by 47 technical support staff. New participants listened to or read those openings and rated the support person while imagining themselves as the caller. Their ratings, especially of a positive, caller-centered style, predicted how satisfied the real callers were. Support staff who used more first-person singular words ("I") also had more satisfied callers <Cite k="hall2014techsupport" />. This is a correlational study of one organization, but it suggests that callers form a durable impression very early.

**How the user describes the problem shapes how fast it gets fixed.** Das studied technical support work using call resolution time and escalation as measures of productivity. The mix of problem-solving moves that support staff used depended strongly on how the person requesting help formulated the task. The author concluded that knowledge efforts need to target users as well as support providers <Cite k="das2003techsupport" />. In practical terms, a support specialist who helps the caller restate the problem precisely is doing productive work, not small talk.

**Handoffs are a skill of their own.** Pentland spent six months as a participant observer at two software support hot lines. He identified a core set of "moves" specialists use to respond to calls. When a specialist cannot resolve a call alone, they must get help from others or give the call away, and moves such as transferring or escalating a call reflect how the organization divides its work <Cite k="pentland1992moves" />. Every escalation is a spoken briefing to a colleague and an explanation to the user about what happens next.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. A calm, caller-centered opening.** Greeting the user, acknowledging the problem, and taking ownership ("I'll stay with you on this") is where satisfaction is largely decided. The research on first-person language suggests that personal commitment from the specialist lands better than a scripted company voice.

**2. Diagnostic questioning.** Users describe symptoms, not causes. Short, specific questions ("What does the message say, word for word?" "Did this work yesterday?") turn a vague complaint into a problem statement, which the Das study links to faster resolution.

**3. Step-by-step instructions without visual aids.** On the phone, the user cannot see your screen and you often cannot see theirs. Clear instructions use one action per sentence, name what the user should see after each step, and wait for confirmation.

**4. Clean escalations.** A good escalation gives the next tier a 30-second summary (symptom, what was tried, what changed) and tells the user plainly who will contact them and when.

**5. Teaching without condescension.** Training users on new hardware or software means explaining the reason behind a step, at a slower pace, and checking understanding with questions rather than "Does that make sense?"

## How to practice

- **Script and rehearse your opening.** Write the first 30 seconds of your ideal call. Read it aloud, then run it through the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). On the phone, instructions usually need a slower pace than normal conversation.
- **Blind walkthrough drill.** Sit back to back with a colleague and talk them through a task, such as changing a display setting, without looking at their screen. Count how many times they get lost and which words caused it.
- **Escalation summaries.** After a real ticket, write the summary you would give the next tier and check it in the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Aim for under 30 seconds spoken.
- **Leave room for the user to act.** Instructions fail when the specialist talks over the user's clicking. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you build in silence after each step.
- **Listen back for fillers.** Record a practice call, transcribe a minute, and paste it into the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter). Fillers spike when you are searching a knowledge base while talking.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is IT support a speaking-heavy job?

Yes. Computer user support specialists rank 219 of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, and O*NET respondents report both face-to-face and phone conversations close to every day. Software developers, by comparison, rank 553.

### Does saying "I" instead of "we" really matter on support calls?

In the help desk study by Hall and colleagues, support staff who used more first-person singular words had more satisfied callers. The finding is correlational, but it fits experiments on customer service language in which agents who referred to themselves as "I" were seen as more empathetic and more willing to act on the customer's behalf <Cite k="packard2018pronouns" />.

### How do I explain technical fixes to non-technical users?

Give one action per sentence, describe what the user should see next, and confirm before moving on. Avoid acronyms unless the user used them first.
