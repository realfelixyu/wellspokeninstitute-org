Freight, stock and material movers who work by hand speak less on the job than most U.S. workers, yet the talk they do is often about safety. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 2.75 out of 5 for importance in this occupation and telephone conversations score only 2.03 out of 5 for frequency. Face-to-face discussions score 4.08. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 40.8 out of 100, placing the job 883rd of 910 <Cite k="onet31" />. The spoken moments that matter are brief: hearing an instruction correctly, calling "stop" during a lift, telling a driver which door to back into, and saying that you are hurt.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

O*NET's task list for this occupation is short, and one core task is explicitly spoken: workers "read work orders or receive oral instructions to determine work assignments or material or equipment needs." Supplemental tasks include sorting cargo before loading and unloading, attaching slings or hooks to lift cargo and guide loads, and recording the number of units moved <Cite k="onet31" />. Communicating with supervisors, peers or subordinates rates 3.80 out of 5 for importance <Cite k="onet31" />.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 2,950,280 jobs in this occupation in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $40,240 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. The work happens on loading docks, in warehouses, at moving jobs and on construction sites.

## What the research says

Few studies record what hand laborers say to each other. The closest evidence comes from loading docks, construction sites and safety rules.

**Agreed words for lifts.** When voice signals are used to direct cranes on U.S. construction sites, OSHA requires the operator, the signal person and any lift director to agree on the voice signals before work starts. Each signal must give the function (such as hoist or boom), then the direction, then distance or speed, and then the function and a stop command. The operator, signal person and any lift director must be able to communicate effectively in the language used <Cite k="osha1926voicesignals" />. The rule covers cranes in construction, not every dock or warehouse, but the same structure (agree first, then always the same order) may be useful for a team lift.

**The loading dock.** Bianca Dijkstra observed and interviewed Polish international truck drivers and Dutch warehouse workers at three companies in the northern Netherlands. They often had to settle the practical details of a transport under time pressure without a shared language. They relied on routines for standard exchanges and improvised for unexpected ones. Gestures mattered as much as speech, and more so in harder situations, alongside objects, writing, drawing, translation apps and asking an interpreter. Some workers learned just enough words in several languages to manage the standard situations <Cite k="dijkstra2023logistics" />.

**Coworkers as interpreters.** In an ethnographic study of UK construction sites, Morwenna Fellows and colleagues looked at bilingual workers who interpret informally. These workers were hired as laborers or supervisors, were not usually trained or paid for language work, and interpreted during inductions, toolbox talks and daily task communication. Their work sometimes went beyond translating words into mediating between people <Cite k="fellows2022informalinterpreters" />.

**Language, and more than language.** Interviews with Polish and Norwegian construction workers by Kinga Wasilkiewicz and colleagues found that the industry treated language as the main issue for migrant workers' safety. The authors concluded that cultural differences were at least as important and received less attention <Cite k="wasilkiewicz2016polishworkers" />. Kamilla Kraft's case study of a Polish construction worker in Norway found that his language skills made him valued as indispensable, while the same valuation kept the line between "migrant" and "local" workers in place <Cite k="kraft2019languagebroker" />.

**Reporting an injury.** Stephanie Premji and colleagues interviewed 39 injured workers who faced language barriers and 70 stakeholders in Ontario and Quebec. Almost all of the workers (34 of 39) eventually filed a compensation claim, but most first delayed reporting to their employer or to workers' compensation. Language barriers amplified every obstacle the workers described <Cite k="premji2022languagereporting" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Checking an oral instruction.** Repeat it back with the numbers: "Twelve pallets from door 4 to the cooler, the blue-tagged ones first?" A repeated instruction lets the supervisor catch a mistake before the first pallet moves.

**Calling a team lift.** Agree on the words before you lift: who counts, what the word for "stop" is, and which direction you are going. Use the same order every time.

**Directing a driver or a coworker you cannot see well.** Short words with gestures. Name the direction, then the distance, then "stop."

**Saying you are hurt, the same day.** Name what happened, when and where. "I twisted my back lifting the washer at 2 in bay 6. It still hurts." If you need an interpreter, ask for one.

## How to practice

These drills have not been tested in a study. They borrow structure from the sources above.

**Lift call.** With two partners and an empty box, agree on four words: lift, walk, set down and stop. One person calls each move in the same order every time: the action, the direction, the distance, then stop. Halfway through, the caller shouts "stop" without warning. Check that everyone froze. Then swap callers.

**Repeat the work order.** A partner reads a work order aloud once, with three locations and two quantities. Repeat it back before moving. Score one point for each detail you got right. Try again with a new order and a noisy fan running.

**Dock exchange with few words.** One person plays a driver who speaks a different language. The other must tell them which door to use and how many pallets are coming off, using only numbers, pointing and a written note. The Institute's article on [gestures and second-language listening](/learn/do-gestures-help-second-language-listening) explains what research says about gestures and comprehension.

**Injury report script.** Write out the four facts you would give: what happened, when, where and what hurts. Say it aloud to a partner playing a supervisor who is busy and tries to wave it off. Practice saying it a second time.

Truck drivers are on the other side of the dock conversation. See the Institute's page on [heavy truck drivers](/professions/heavy-truck-drivers).

## Frequently asked questions

### What English do material movers need on the job?

Numbers, directions, the names of the equipment and loads you handle, and the words your crew uses for stop and go. Being able to repeat back an instruction and report an injury matters more than general fluency.

### What if nobody on the crew shares my language?

Agree on a few key words and gestures before the work starts, and keep using them the same way. On the loading docks Dijkstra studied, workers combined routines, gestures, writing and translation apps to get transports done <Cite k="dijkstra2023logistics" />.

### Should I report a minor injury right away?

Report it the same day to your supervisor. In the Ontario and Quebec study, most injured workers delayed reporting, and language barriers made each obstacle worse <Cite k="premji2022languagereporting" />.
