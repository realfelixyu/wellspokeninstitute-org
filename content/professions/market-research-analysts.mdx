A market research analyst may talk someone through a survey question in the morning and explain to a manager that afternoon why the answers leave a decision open. For Market Research Analysts and Marketing Specialists, O*NET® rates speaking importance at 3.88 out of 5. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score is 67 out of 100. The occupation is listed at 346 of 910; the same score covers places 340 through 350. The questions an analyst asks before collecting data shape what they can say at the presentation <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

O*NET lists directing trained survey interviewers, designing or evaluating data collection methods, and joining staff meetings to give management information and proposals about products or services. It also lists preparing written reports and illustrating findings with graphics. The person who asks a customer the question may be someone else. The analyst who wrote it still has to explain what counts as an answer <Cite k="onet31" />.

Consider a fictional assignment. A manager, Inez, wants to know why some pickup orders go uncollected. An analyst, Ravi, asks, “Do you mean people who came to the store and left without the order, or people who never arrived before it expired?” Inez means both. Ravi now has two groups to investigate. He can brief the interviewer about which customers to speak to and tell Inez which question each group can answer. Someone who never reached the store cannot describe what happened at the pickup counter.

O*NET’s work-context figures for this occupation came from 20 occupational experts. They rated telephone conversations at 4.15 out of 5 for frequency and public speaking at 2.40. Those averages describe the broader occupation, which includes marketing specialists. They give a job applicant a reason to ask who conducts customer calls and who presents the findings on this particular team <Cite k="onet31" />.

## What the research says

**What happens when a respondent asks for a definition?** Frederick Conrad and Michael Schober studied telephone interviews with households in the continental United States. Everyone initially heard standardized questions about housing and purchases. On a second call, 118 people again received standardized interviews and 109 received conversational interviews in which the interviewer could explain what a question meant. People in the conversational interviews changed their answers more often, and the reasons they gave fit the survey’s official definitions more closely. The conversations also took longer. The questions came from government surveys. A study of a retailer’s pickup service might need different definitions <Cite k="conrad2000householdclarify" />.

**A change of wording can change the result.** In three years of the US General Social Survey, Kenneth Rasinski examined versions of questions about public spending. More people said the country spent too little on “assistance to the poor” than on “welfare.” Those were responses to particular public-policy questions. When two customer surveys disagree, an analyst can start by comparing the words the interviewers used each time <Cite k="rasinski1989spendingwording" />.

**What does a focus-group report get used for?** William McDonald collected data from 248 focus-group moderators in the United States, Germany and Japan. The study distinguished using the research to solve a specific problem from uses such as helping an organization think about an issue. Its analysis linked the research objectives and the moderators to the reporting and use of findings. It recorded moderators’ perspectives. It did not count how many customers outside a group shared an opinion <Cite k="mcdonald1994moderatoruse" />.

**What managers do with the findings.** Rohit Deshpande and Gerald Zaltman examined how marketing managers used research on a particular project. Among the factors connected with use were the technical quality of the work, whether managers could act on it and the interaction between researchers and managers. A later study by Christine Moorman and colleagues examined relationships involving 779 market research users and providers. The perceived quality of their interactions was associated with using the research. Trust worked indirectly through other aspects of the relationship in the authors’ model. Neither study tested a script an analyst could use to win approval for a proposal <Cite k="deshpande1982researchuse,moorman1992researchtrust" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

When briefing an interviewer, give an example close enough to the boundary that the answer takes thought. In Ravi’s fictional project, a customer reached the store but stayed in the parking lot after receiving a cancellation notice. Would the team count that as a visit? Let the interviewer answer using the current instructions. If the two of you reach different answers, the wording needs another look before the next call.

When presenting a finding, say who supplied it. “Two people in Thursday’s discussion said they never saw the pickup sign” gives Inez a place to investigate. “Customers can’t find pickup” sounds like a claim about everyone who orders. The more precise version leaves room for another explanation, including the possibility that other customers saw the sign and found it clear. If Inez wants to know how common the problem is, that is a new question for the research plan.

## How to practice

These conversations use invented details. The studies above did not test the exercises.

**Brief an interviewer.** Ask a colleague to play Ravi. Give them two imaginary cases, one where a customer collected an order at the counter and one where a friend collected it for them. Tell them precisely whose experience you need. Ask how they would handle the friend’s answer and listen for a difference between the question you wrote and the one they would ask.

**Moderate a short disagreement.** One colleague says the pickup instructions were obvious. Another says they were hard to follow. Ask each to describe the last thing they saw before deciding where to go. For your notes, write one sentence you can attribute to each person. Leave open what other customers would say.

**Give Inez the decision back.** Use the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to estimate how long your draft briefing would take. Read it aloud to a colleague. Then ask what they would do with the sign tomorrow and which question still needs evidence. A useful revision might be one extra sentence about who took part in the discussion.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every market research analyst run focus groups?

O*NET describes several routes to evidence, including surveys and existing data. A role that involves directing interviewers or presenting to managers can require substantial speaking even when other people lead the groups <Cite k="onet31" />. The May 2025 federal estimate of 899,580 U.S. jobs and a $78,760 median annual wage covers market research analysts and marketing specialists together <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

### What should I do when a survey answer could fit two categories?

Check the project’s definition before deciding how to record it. If the instructions leave the case open, bring the example to the research lead so the next interviewer can handle the same situation consistently.

### How can I present an interesting finding from a small group?

Report what the participants actually said and identify the group they came from. If management needs a percentage for all customers, explain what broader evidence you would need before putting that number on a slide.
