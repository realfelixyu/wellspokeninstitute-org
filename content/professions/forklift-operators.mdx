A forklift operator needs to be able to tell a supervisor which truck has a defect, check a load that doesn't match the order and coordinate with people on foot. Public speaking is a small part of the job. O*NET® rates speaking importance at 2.75 out of 5 for Industrial Truck and Tractor Operators, the occupation that includes forklift operators. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score for that occupation is 40.8 out of 100, a score shared by occupations listed 882nd through 884th of 910. Face-to-face discussions score 4.42 out of 5 for frequency, well above public speaking at 1.61 <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list includes checking product loads for accuracy, moving materials between parts of a facility and recording weights or production data. Those tasks give an operator something specific to ask about. Suppose Mina, a fictional operator, finds a pallet labeled for receiving in a row designated for dispatch. “Where does this go?” makes a coworker guess what she has found. “Pallet 37 says receiving. The dispatch list says 73. Which pallet belongs on that order?” gives the coworker two numbers to check against the record <Cite k="onet31" />.

Other reports concern the truck itself. Under OSHA’s general-industry powered industrial truck standard, paragraph (q)(7), trucks covered by the standard must be examined before being put into service. A condition affecting safe operation keeps a truck out of service. Defects found during the examination must be reported and corrected immediately. Paragraph (q)(1) assigns repairs to authorized personnel. An operator can report exactly what they saw or heard without knowing which part needs repair <Cite k="osha1910forklift" />.

There are also situations where a conversation belongs before anyone enters the area. In its forklift alert, the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health recommends separating forklift traffic from other workers wherever possible. A discussion about where pedestrians can walk belongs alongside the site’s physical separation and traffic controls. A shouted warning leaves very little time to resolve a misunderstanding <Cite k="niosh2001forklifts,oregon2008forkliftclerk" />.

## What warehouse investigations found

In an Oregon warehouse, a forklift operator and a receiving clerk had been breaking up a shipment together. The operator went back for the last pallet. While backing down the aisle, he spotted the clerk walking toward him. He shouted, made eye contact and braked. The forklift still struck the clerk, who died. Investigators recommended that workers on foot communicate their intention to enter an operating area and receive an acknowledgment from the operator. The report illustrates the difference between planning access and trying to warn someone when the truck is already moving <Cite k="oregon2008forkliftclerk" />.

In a California retail warehouse, a spotter went behind a pallet of bottled water while an operator worked on the load. The operator could no longer see the spotter. The investigators, who reviewed security footage, said the two appeared to have lost verbal contact as well. Cases fell on the spotter and killed him. Among several contributing factors, the report identified loss of contact and a gap in the employer's procedure for unstable loads. It recommended enforcing exclusion zones, keeping people in the operator’s line of sight and maintaining communication. Those were recommendations following one investigation. It did not establish that any one phrase would have prevented the death <Cite k="kleinmann2023forkliftspotter" />.

A smaller question came out of interviews at a rail-equipment plant near Pittsburgh. Researchers installed colored warning lights on three forklifts and asked nine employees, including operators and pedestrians, about their experience. All three employees interviewed as pedestrians thought the lights helped warn people that a forklift was in the area. Two of four operators mentioned glare from the front light near metal racks or shrink wrap. One person could reasonably report an earlier warning while someone in the cab reported a new difficulty seeing. The pilot collected workers' impressions and did not establish a reduction in collisions <Cite k="bobick2020forkliftlights" />.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Describe the observation.** In a fictional pre-shift report, “Truck 12's horn made no sound during the check, and the truck is out of service” gives the incoming supervisor a truck number, a symptom and its status. “The horn switch is broken” makes a diagnosis the operator may have no way to verify. Use the reporting procedure for the site <Cite k="osha1910forklift" />.

**Find out what the other person understood.** Before a planned change to the warehouse layout, a floor lead can ask an operator and a receiving clerk separately to point out the pedestrian route on a drawing. If they point to different places, the lead has a specific question to settle before work begins. This paper example gives the crew a reason to talk about the traffic controls recommended by NIOSH <Cite k="niosh2001forklifts" />.

**Pass on what remains open.** For the fictional dispatch discrepancy, Mina’s handoff might be, “Pallet 37 is still in receiving. Jo is checking dispatch order 73. I haven't had an answer yet.” The next shift can tell which part it needs to follow up. If the label and order have since been reconciled, say who confirmed the change and where the updated record is.

## How to practice

Try these untested exercises seated at a table, away from operating equipment. Write two imaginary vehicle numbers on separate cards and give one a reported defect. One partner plays the operator finishing a shift. The other plays a supervisor with several trucks to account for. Give the report aloud, then have the supervisor write down which truck is affected, what was observed and whether it is available for use. Compare the note with the card. If the wrong number was recorded, change how you say it and try again.

For a second round, sketch a door, an aisle and a walkway on paper. Give one partner a note saying the usual walkway will be closed. Have that person explain what has changed and whom the crew must ask about the approved alternative. The listener marks what they can establish from the message and asks about whatever is still unclear. This is practice for discussing a plan. On shift, use the actual traffic plan, designated routes and employer's operating procedures.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a horn the same as an acknowledgment?

No. The OSHA standard requires slowing down and sounding the horn at cross aisles and other places where vision is obstructed. The Oregon investigators separately recommended that a worker intending to enter an operating area receive an acknowledgment from the operator <Cite k="osha1910forklift,oregon2008forkliftclerk" />.

### Can these exercises count as forklift operator training?

The exercises cover spoken reports. For trucks under its general-industry standard, OSHA requires formal instruction, practical training and evaluation of the operator's performance in the workplace <Cite k="osha1910forklift" />.

### Do the employment figures cover only warehouse forklift drivers?

The figures cover Industrial Truck and Tractor Operators, including people moving materials in storage yards and factories as well as warehouses. BLS estimated 774,420 jobs in the United States in May 2025 and a median annual wage of $46,420 for that occupation <Cite k="onet31,blsoews2025" />.
