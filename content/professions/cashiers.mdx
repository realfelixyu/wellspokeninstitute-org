“Hi, I can help you here.” It's a useful cashier greeting when a shopper is standing halfway between two lanes. Once they're unloading their basket, a simple “Hi” will do. If they open with “These peaches rang up wrong last time,” answer that before you scan the bag. “What price did you see? We can check when I weigh them.”

## How do you greet the next customer?

Finish the exchange you're in. If one customer is still waiting for change while another is putting groceries on the belt, “Here's your change” belongs to the first customer. Turn to the second one for your hello. That small distinction tells both people whose turn it is.

In a Swedish study, a researcher audio-recorded 46 checkout conversations with one cashier at a supermarket northwest of Stockholm. The cashier opened 41 of them. The researcher described the greeting as a signal that the next exchange could begin. In another of those conversations, the cashier looked up the code for a variety of potato while the customer commented on its price. Those are two different uses for talk at the same register <Cite k="tykesson2006checkout" />.

## What do you say when a shopper questions a price?

Find out which item they mean. If two similar cartons went over the scanner, “Was it the strawberry carton or the plain one?” gives the shopper something specific to answer. Read back what the register shows for that item. If someone else needs to check the shelf, explain what you're asking them to check.

In fieldwork at six Israeli supermarkets, Anat Rafaeli observed customers asking to check prices or sales slips. Some went to the shelves themselves to check a price or a code. Rafaeli also worked as a cashier and interviewed cashiers and shoppers. Customers sometimes wanted to watch a cashier enter an item on the register <Cite k="rafaeli1989cashiers" />.

## Practice a price check with a coworker

Make two pretend shelf labels. The 12-ounce bag of coffee is $8.50 and the 16-ounce bag is $10. Give your coworker the larger bag. They play a shopper who remembers seeing $8.50. You see $10 on the register. Start at the moment they say, “I think the coffee's on sale.”

Try “This bag rang up at ten dollars. Let me check which size the sale tag covers.” Have a third person play the coworker at the shelf, or turn the labels over yourself. Come back with what you found. “The $8.50 tag is for the 12-ounce bag. The 16-ounce one you have is $10. Which would you like?” Let the shopper decide before you give the total.

Switch roles. Write a new shelf tag that says “16 ounces, $8.50,” and leave the register price at $10. This time the size matches and the two prices differ. Follow your store's price-check procedure. Tell the shopper who can confirm the price and what you'll do while they wait. Listen for the point at which the cashier can explain what the label says and what happens next. The customer should hear whether you're still checking or have an answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do cashiers need to announce every price?

Follow the store's procedure. In a 1953 U.S. Department of Agriculture study, announcing item prices was an established practice at one of the two supermarket organizations studied. Observers tallied whether cashiers did it. That was a rule at those stores. In the coffee exercise, say the amount the shopper asked about, then give them a chance to respond <Cite k="kriesberg1953cashiers" />.

### How much speaking does a cashier's job involve?

O*NET lists greeting customers and answering their questions among cashier tasks. Speaking scores 3.00 out of 5 for importance, and face-to-face discussions score 4.56 out of 5 for frequency. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score for cashiers is 50 out of 100 <Cite k="onet31" />. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 3,089,410 cashier jobs in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $32,880 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. BLS projects cashier employment will decline 6.5% from 2025 to 2035 <Cite k="blsoutlook2035" />.
