Training and development specialists are paid to explain, demonstrate, and lead discussion, so speaking skill is part of the product they deliver. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.38 out of 5 for importance, and the occupation ranks 66th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking frequency is rated 3.83 out of 5: 21.74% of surveyed specialists report it daily and 47.83% weekly. Research on workplace training shows that how a session is delivered and debriefed affects how much trainees learn and use.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The first task on the O\*NET list is to "present information with a variety of instructional techniques or formats, such as role playing, simulations, team exercises, group discussions, videos, or lectures." Specialists also assess training needs through interviews with employees, focus groups, and consultations with managers. They attend meetings to report training status to management, negotiate contracts with clients over training outcomes and fees, and supervise and evaluate other instructors <Cite k="onet31" />.

Telephone use is rated 4.52 out of 5 and face-to-face discussion 4.43. Training a workforce involves a good deal of talk outside the classroom, with managers and subject experts. The O\*NET list also includes evaluating modes of delivery, such as in-person or virtual sessions, which call for different speaking habits.

## What the research says

**Well-designed training works, and method matters.** Arthur, Bennett, Edens, and Bell meta-analyzed studies of organizational training. Average effect sizes were medium to large: 0.60 for trainee reactions, 0.63 for learning, 0.62 for on-the-job behavior, and 0.62 for organizational results. The training method used, the type of skill trained, and the way effectiveness was measured were all related to how well programs worked <Cite k="arthur2003training" />. Training is not automatically effective, but it can be.

**Organization and expressiveness both affect recall.** Towler and Dipboye had 135 participants listen to lectures that varied in how well organized they were and how expressive the trainer was. Recall was highest after a lecture that was both expressive and organized. Problem-solving results were more complicated: participants with a strong mastery orientation did worst after an organized but inexpressive lecture, while those with a low mastery orientation were not affected by either factor <Cite k="towler2001expressiveness" />. For recall, the best results came when structure and delivery were both strong.

**Behavior modeling works best with clear learning points and realistic practice.** Taylor, Russ-Eft, and Chan analyzed 117 studies of behavior modeling training, in which trainees watch a model perform a skill and then practice it. Effects were largest on learning and smaller on job behavior and results. Skill development was greatest when key learning points were spelled out as rules and when training time was longer. Transfer to the job was greatest when trainees saw both positive and negative models, practiced scenarios they generated themselves, set goals, and had supervisors who were also trained <Cite k="taylor2005modeling" />.

**Debriefs improve performance.** Tannenbaum and Cerasoli meta-analyzed 46 samples covering 2,136 participants. On average, debriefs (also called after-action reviews) improved effectiveness by about 25% over control groups, an effect size of 0.67. Results were similar for teams and individuals, in simulations and real settings, and in medical and nonmedical groups. The authors found signs that facilitation and structure helped <Cite k="tannenbaum2013debriefs" />.

Taken together, the evidence supports a trainer who organizes content clearly, delivers it with energy, models skills with explicit learning points, and leads a structured conversation afterward.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Clear structure.** Telling trainees what is coming, signaling transitions, and summarizing each section make a session's organization easy to hear.
- **Expressive delivery.** Trainer expressiveness affected recall in the same experiment, and an organized but flat lecture produced the weakest problem-solving among mastery-oriented trainees.
- **Stating learning points.** When modeling a skill, say the rule out loud ("Acknowledge the concern before giving your answer") so trainees can repeat it.
- **Facilitating role play.** Setting up realistic scenarios, coaching during practice, and keeping feedback specific are core facilitation skills.
- **Leading debriefs.** A good debrief asks what was supposed to happen, what happened, why, and what to do next time, with the facilitator asking more than telling.

## How to practice

1. **Outline out loud.** Before your next session, deliver just the opening two minutes: the purpose, the agenda, and what trainees will be able to do by the end. Record it and listen for whether the structure is clear.
2. **Film one module.** Record yourself teaching a 10-minute segment and watch it with the sound off, then with the picture off. Note where your energy drops.
3. **Write learning points as rules.** For each skill you model, write one or two short rules trainees can say back to you, and check them with the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
4. **Script your debrief questions.** Write four debrief questions for your next exercise and commit to asking each one before giving your own view.
5. **Pace your lecture segments.** Check your delivery speed with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) and the length of each lecture block with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for training and development specialists?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.38 out of 5 for this occupation, and it ranks 66th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score.

### Does lecturing still work in workplace training?

It can. The meta-analysis of organizational training found that results depended on the method and the skill being trained, and the lecture experiment found that organized, expressive lectures produced the best recall.

### What is a debrief?

A debrief is a structured discussion after a task or exercise where participants review what happened and agree on what to change. A meta-analysis found debriefs improved performance by about 25% on average.
