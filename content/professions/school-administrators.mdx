Few jobs depend on speaking as heavily as running a school. Education administrators for kindergarten through grade 12, a group that includes principals and assistant principals, rank 3rd of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.75 out of 5 for importance, oral expression 4.88, and oral comprehension 4.88 <Cite k="onet31" />. The research on school leadership points to one kind of talk in particular: conversations with teachers about their teaching.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list for this occupation is mostly a list of meetings. Administrators confer with parents and staff about policies, behavior, and learning problems, and counsel students on personal, academic, and behavioral issues. They observe teaching and evaluate staff, plan and lead professional development, direct teachers and support staff, and recruit and hire. They meet with federal, state, and local agencies, submit program proposals for school board approval, organize committees, and build partnerships with businesses and community groups <Cite k="onet31" />.

The work context ratings back this up. Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.85 out of 5 for frequency and telephone use 4.44, and dealing with the public is rated 4.07 out of 5 for importance. Formal public speaking is less frequent at 3.35 out of 5, with 15.43% of surveyed administrators reporting it daily and 24.4% weekly. The heaviest load is one-on-one and small-group conversation, often with people who are upset, anxious, or under evaluation.

## What the research says

**Leadership focused on teaching has larger effects.** Robinson, Lloyd, and Rowe analyzed 27 published studies linking leadership to student outcomes. In the 22 studies that allowed a comparison, the average effect of instructional leadership was three to four times that of transformational leadership. Among five sets of leadership practices, the strongest average effect came from leaders promoting and taking part in teacher learning and development <Cite k="robinson2008leadership" />. Much of that work happens through talk: observing, discussing, and planning with teachers.

**How principals spend their time matters.** Grissom, Loeb, and Master used full-day, in-person observations of about 100 urban principals over three school years. Total time on instructional tasks did not predict achievement growth. Time spent coaching teachers, evaluating them, and developing the school's educational program did predict gains. Time on informal classroom walkthroughs predicted lower growth, especially in high schools. Interviews suggested walkthroughs often were not tied to a broader improvement plan <Cite k="grissom2013principals" />.

**Coaching conversations change instruction.** Kraft, Blazar, and Hogan pooled 60 studies of teacher coaching that used causal research designs. Coaching programs improved instruction by 0.49 standard deviations and student achievement by 0.18. Effects in large programs were only a fraction of those in small ones, which the authors describe as the challenge of scaling up coaching while keeping it effective <Cite k="kraft2018coaching" />. Most of the evidence came from literacy coaching with prekindergarten and elementary teachers.

**Feedback conversations need time and training.** Kraft and Gilmour interviewed 24 principals in an urban district that had reformed teacher evaluation. Principals said the new framework gave them a common language that made feedback conversations with teachers easier. They also described pressures that weakened the feedback they gave. The authors concluded that useful feedback depends on evaluators having the time and training to give individualized, actionable advice <Cite k="kraft2016evaluators" />.

These studies use different designs, from meta-analyses to observation and interviews. They agree that the administrator talk most closely tied to student learning is specific, regular conversation with teachers about instruction.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Post-observation feedback.** A useful debrief names one specific strength, one specific change, and a concrete next step, using evidence from the lesson. This is the evaluation and coaching time Grissom's team linked to gains.
- **Facilitating professional learning.** Leading adult learning sessions draws on the same skills as teaching: clear goals, examples, and structured discussion.
- **Difficult conversations with families.** Discipline and placement meetings call for calm, plain explanations of what happened and what comes next.
- **Presenting to boards and agencies.** Program proposals and budget requests need a short, evidence-based case delivered in a few minutes.
- **Running meetings.** Committees and staff meetings go better with a stated purpose, a time limit, and a clear decision at the end.

## How to practice

1. **Script a feedback conversation.** After your next observation, write three sentences: the strength you saw, the one change you recommend, and the evidence for both. Say them aloud before the meeting, then ask the teacher what support would help.
2. **Ask before you tell.** Open debriefs with a question such as "What did you notice about the discussion in the second half?" and listen to the full answer before giving your view.
3. **Tighten board presentations.** Draft your case for a program in under two minutes using the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder), then check the full talk's length with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).
4. **Rehearse messages to families.** Recorded calls and video updates to families go more smoothly read from a script. The [free teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) helps you deliver a prepared message without reading from paper.
5. **Review a walkthrough plan.** If you do classroom walkthroughs, decide in advance what you will discuss with each teacher afterward, so the visit leads to a conversation rather than a checklist.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for school principals?

Extremely important. K-12 education administrators rank 3rd of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score, and O\*NET rates speaking 4.75 out of 5 for importance.

### Which principal activities are linked to student achievement?

In a study of about 100 urban principals, time spent coaching and evaluating teachers and developing the educational program predicted achievement gains, while informal walkthroughs did not.

### Does instructional coaching work?

A meta-analysis of 60 causal studies found that coaching improved both instruction and achievement on average, though large programs produced smaller effects than small ones.
