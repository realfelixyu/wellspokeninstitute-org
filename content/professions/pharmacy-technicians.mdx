When someone asks, “Can I collect it today?”, find out what they need to decide. A caller whose last bus leaves at four needs a different answer from someone wondering whether the pharmacy is open after work. If the pickup time is still uncertain, say what you can check. A guessed time can send someone across town for nothing.

The O*NET telephone-use rating for pharmacy technicians is 5 out of 5. Their Institute Speaking Demand Score is 63.4 out of 100. The job includes answering calls and directing questions to pharmacists when needed <Cite k="onet31" />. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 471,680 U.S. pharmacy technician jobs in May 2025 and projects national employment growth of 6.4% between 2025 and 2035 <Cite k="blsoews2025,blsoutlook2035" />.

## Keep the caller's question intact

Imagine the caller tells you, “The text said to contact the pharmacy, and I have to catch the four o'clock bus.” You might ask, “Are you trying to decide whether to make the trip before four?” That gives the caller a chance to correct your reading. Perhaps they're already outside and want to know which counter to visit.

In six Wisconsin community pharmacies, researchers observed nine technicians and nine pharmacists. Technicians answered calls and routed questions. The researchers counted three to seven interruptions of a pharmacist's work per hour across the sites, from several different sources <Cite k="reddy2019interruptions" />. A request for a pharmacist can be entirely appropriate. What a technician can do is spare the caller from having to explain the bus deadline from the beginning.

Try carrying two details into the conversation with the pharmacist. “The pickup message asked them to call. They need to know whether to leave home before four.” If the caller also has a question about taking the medicine, preserve their wording and route it to the pharmacist according to the pharmacy’s procedure.

## When another person owns the answer

A team in Indiana interviewed 37 people at four independent pharmacies, including 15 technicians and 15 patients. When they discussed referrals to community resources, two recurring concerns were who would handle a referral and how anyone would know what happened afterward <Cite k="miller2019pharmacyreferrals" />. At the counter, the phrase “I'll pass that along” leaves the same practical question open. Who should the caller expect to hear from, and when?

Give only a next step your team can carry out. For example, “I can ask the pharmacist while you're on the line. Would you like to hold?” In another setting you may need to arrange a callback. A U.S. survey of community technicians distinguished tasks respondents already performed from tasks they felt willing to take on. The survey included phone work and communication with patients and prescribers <Cite k="doucette2018technicians" />. Check which role owns that callback in your pharmacy before making a promise.

## Practice the transfer

Work in a group of three with an invented pickup request. The caller chooses one constraint, such as a bus departure, and writes it down. The technician hears the request and passes it to the colleague playing the pharmacist. The pharmacist says aloud what the caller needs to decide. Compare that sentence with the caller's note. If “should I leave now?” became “they're in a hurry,” repeat the transfer and restore the missing question. Use fictional details throughout.

## Frequently asked questions

### What do I say when I don't know whether an order is ready?

Say what you know and ask for time to check the unresolved detail. If the caller must decide whether to leave for the pharmacy, find out their deadline before offering to call back.

### How do I avoid making someone repeat a personal question at the counter?

Ask whether they'd prefer to speak with the pharmacist privately. Pass along only what your colleague needs to connect with the person. Let the caller give sensitive details directly in the setting they choose.
