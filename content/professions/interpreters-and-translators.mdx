For interpreters, speaking is the work product itself: every sentence a client hears in the target language comes out of the interpreter's mouth. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.12 out of 5 for importance and oral expression 4.25 for interpreters and translators, and the occupation ranks 102nd of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. The required level of oral expression is 4.75 on a 7-point scale. Research in hospitals and courtrooms shows that small spoken choices by interpreters, from a dropped word to a change in tone, can have real consequences.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The core O\*NET task is to "translate messages simultaneously or consecutively into specified languages, orally or by using hand signs, maintaining message content, context, and style as much as possible." Interpreters also listen closely to speakers to determine meaning, compile technical terminology for legal and medical settings, and "identify and resolve conflicts related to the meanings of words, concepts, practices, or behaviors." Some educate students, parents, and teachers about the role of educational interpreters, and some discuss requirements and fees with clients <Cite k="onet31" />.

Face-to-face discussion is rated 4.64 out of 5 for frequency, and 33.08% of surveyed workers report public speaking every day. The O\*NET occupation combines spoken interpreters with written translators, so the ratings average two quite different kinds of work. In May 2025, 52,060 people were employed in the occupation <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

**Trained professionals made fewer consequential errors.** Flores and colleagues audiotaped 57 pediatric emergency department visits with Spanish-speaking families who had limited English proficiency. They found 1,884 interpreter errors, and 18% had potential clinical consequences. The share of errors with potential consequences was lower with professional interpreters (12%) than with ad hoc interpreters, such as untrained staff or family members (22%), or no interpreter (20%). Among professionals, hours of training mattered and years of experience did not: interpreters with at least 100 hours of training made fewer errors and a much smaller share of consequential ones (2% vs. 12%) <Cite k="flores2012interpreters" />.

**Professional interpreters improved clinical care.** Karliner and colleagues systematically reviewed 28 studies of interpreter use in health care. Across communication errors and comprehension, use of services, clinical outcomes, and satisfaction, professional interpreters were associated with better care than ad hoc interpreters. With professional interpreters, care for patients with limited English proficiency approached or equaled care for patients without language barriers <Cite k="karliner2007interpreters" />.

**Court interpreters often change the style of testimony.** Sandra Hale analyzed Spanish-English court proceedings and found that interpreters tended to render the content of witnesses' answers accurately but regularly altered their style, sometimes making a witness sound better and sometimes worse. Because research shows that speaking style affects how people are perceived, and witness credibility can decide an adversarial case, these shifts could change outcomes <Cite k="hale2002style" />.

**Quality drops when turns run long.** Moser-Mercer, Künzli, and Korac studied simultaneous interpreters working beyond the standard turn length. Interpreters who worked longer than about 30 minutes, the recommended turn time under standard conditions, risked a decline in output quality, apparently from a combination of psychological and physiological strain <Cite k="mosermercer1998turns" />. This was a small pilot study, but it supports the common practice of working in pairs and switching regularly.

Together, the studies show that accuracy covers more than vocabulary. Omissions, additions, tone, and fatigue all affect what the listener receives.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Completeness.** Rendering everything that was said, including hedges and small qualifiers, matters in settings where a dropped phrase can change a diagnosis or a legal meaning.
- **Preserving register and style.** Matching the speaker's hesitations, politeness, and directness, as the O\*NET task describes, keeps the listener's impression of the speaker accurate.
- **Terminology under pressure.** Fast recall of medical and legal terms comes from preparation before each assignment.
- **Clear, steady delivery.** Interpreters must speak at a pace and volume the listener can follow, often in a noisy room or over a phone line.
- **Managing fatigue.** Recognizing when quality is slipping and handing off to a partner is part of professional practice.

## How to practice

1. **Shadow speech in your own language.** Play a news clip or lecture and repeat it aloud a second or two behind the speaker. Start with 5 minutes and build up, recording yourself to check for omissions.
2. **Practice sight translation.** Read a short medical or legal form in one language and render it aloud in the other without pausing. Then check your version against the original line by line.
3. **Keep a glossary drill.** Before each assignment, list 20 terms you expect to hear and say each one aloud in both languages until retrieval is instant.
4. **Measure your pace.** Record a rendering and check it with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator). Aim for a pace the listener can follow, not the fastest pace you can manage.
5. **Track your stamina.** In practice sessions, record a long simultaneous passage and compare accuracy in the first 10 minutes with the last 10 minutes to see when your quality starts to drop.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for interpreters?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.12 out of 5 for interpreters and translators, and the occupation ranks 102nd of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score.

### Are professional interpreters more accurate than family members?

In one study of pediatric emergency visits, a lower share of professional interpreters' errors had potential clinical consequences (12%) than those of ad hoc interpreters (22%). A systematic review also linked professional interpreters to better clinical care.

### Why do simultaneous interpreters work in pairs?

Interpreting quality can decline after about 30 minutes of continuous simultaneous work. Working in pairs lets interpreters switch before fatigue affects accuracy.
