Nursing is a speaking job that rarely gets described as one. In O*NET® data, speaking has an importance rating of 4 on a 5-point scale for registered nurses, and the occupation ranks 154th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Most of that talk is unscripted and happens under time pressure: reporting to the next shift, calling a physician about a dropping blood pressure, coaching a family through a new insulin routine, and telling an aide what to watch for. With 3,379,720 registered nurses employed in the United States in May 2025 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />, small improvements in how nurses speak touch a very large number of patients.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list for registered nurses reads like a set of conversations. Nurses "monitor, record, and report symptoms or changes in patients' conditions," "consult and coordinate with healthcare team members to assess, plan, implement, or evaluate patient care plans," and "instruct individuals, families, or other groups on topics such as health education, disease prevention, or childbirth" <Cite k="onet31" />. They also direct or supervise less-skilled nursing staff, refer patients to community agencies, and, in some roles, inform the physician of a patient's condition during anesthesia.

The work context ratings show how constant this is. The average frequency rating is 4.94 out of 5 for both face-to-face discussion and telephone use. In separate survey questions, 96.25% of respondents reported daily face-to-face discussions and 95.70% reported daily telephone conversations. The importance of dealing with the public is rated 4.52. Formal public speaking is much less frequent, at 2.32, though 9.75% of nurses report doing it daily, which fits the teaching side of the role: childbirth classes, diabetes education groups, and unit in-services.

Three kinds of talk carry the most risk:

- **Shift report and handoff.** Everything the outgoing nurse knows about a patient has to move to the incoming nurse in a few minutes, usually out loud.
- **Escalation.** A phone call to a physician or rapid response team at 3 a.m. has to be short, ordered, and end with a clear request.
- **Patient and family teaching.** Discharge instructions, new medications, and wound care are spoken to people who are tired, worried, and often hearing the terms for the first time.

## What the research says

**Handoffs are widely discussed but thinly studied.** A systematic review of nursing handoff literature screened 2,649 articles and kept 95. Only 20 of those were research studies, and 17 of the 20 scored 8 or lower on a 16-point quality scale. The authors found "remarkable consistency" in recommended strategies but little evidence behind them <Cite k="riesenberg2010handoffs" />. In practice, that means most handoff advice is sensible but not proven, and nurses should expect local protocols to differ.

**SBAR helps most on the phone.** SBAR (situation, background, assessment, recommendation) is the most common script for nurse-to-physician calls. A 2018 systematic review included 8 before-after studies and 3 controlled trials. Across 26 measured patient outcomes, 8 improved significantly. The authors judged the evidence for safety gains as moderate, "especially when used to structure communication over the phone," and noted a lack of high-quality trials <Cite k="muller2018sbar" />.

**Teach-back improves understanding.** In teach-back, the nurse asks the patient to explain the instructions in their own words, then corrects and repeats. A systematic review of 12 studies in people with chronic disease found improvements in disease-specific knowledge, adherence, self-efficacy, and inhaler technique. Effects on readmissions and self-care trended positive but were inconsistent, and quality of life did not improve in the one study that measured it <Cite k="hadinh2016teachback" />.

Taken together, the evidence supports structure and checking, with honest caveats about study quality.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Ordered, prioritized reporting.** The report task is only useful if the listener can follow it. Lead with what could hurt the patient in the next few hours, then fill in background. A handoff that starts with the admission story from four days ago buries the lead.

**2. Saying the recommendation out loud.** The "R" in SBAR is the part nurses most often soften or skip. "I think you should come and see him" or "I need an order for a fluid bolus" gives the physician something to agree or disagree with. A vague call ("just wanted to let you know") invites a vague answer.

**3. Teaching in short chunks, then checking.** Patient teaching works better in two or three points at a time than in a full sheet read aloud. Ending each chunk with a teach-back question ("So I know I explained it clearly, how will you take this tonight?") turns instruction into a check.

**4. Clear delegation.** When directing aides or less-experienced nurses, name the task, the time, and the threshold that should trigger a call back: "Take her pressure at 2 and 4, and call me if the top number is under 90."

**5. A steady voice with families.** A slower rate, a lower pitch, and plain vocabulary help a family hear that someone is in charge, even when the news is uncertain.

## How to practice

- **Script one SBAR call.** In this fictional case, a patient is ready to go home, but the follow-up instructions give two different appointment times. Write one line each for the situation, background, assessment and recommendation. Read the call to a colleague. Can they tell what changed and who needs to clarify the time? Use the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) to estimate how long your invented script would take.
- **Rehearse a shift handoff.** Use the same fictional case. This time the clinic will call back after the shift changes. Give the report from memory and ask your colleague what they would follow up on. If you want to check for verbal fillers, type your practice wording into the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter).
- **Simplify a teaching script.** Write a sample explanation for an imaginary discharge visit. Use the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to find long sentences, then read a shorter version to your colleague.
- **Practice the pause after key instructions.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps build the habit of stopping after a dose or a warning sign so the listener can absorb it.
- **Run teach-back with a colleague.** Take turns playing a patient who nods along but has misunderstood. The goal is to phrase the check-back question so it does not sound like a test.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is SBAR proven to improve patient safety?

The best available review rates the evidence as moderate, with the clearest benefit for structuring phone calls between nurses and physicians. High-quality trials are still scarce, so SBAR is best treated as a useful structure rather than a guarantee.

### What is the teach-back method?

Teach-back means asking patients to explain instructions in their own words, then clarifying anything they get wrong. Studies in chronic disease link it to better knowledge, adherence, and self-efficacy.

### Do registered nurses need public speaking skills?

Formal public speaking is occasional for most nurses, rated 2.32 out of 5 in O*NET data, but about 1 in 10 nurses report doing it daily. Teaching groups and leading in-services draw on the same skills as a short presentation.
