Running a sales floor takes a lot of talking, split between employees and shoppers. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, first-line supervisors of retail sales workers rate speaking 3.75 out of 5 for importance. Face-to-face discussions score 4.95 out of 5 for frequency, a value only 65 of the 910 occupations exceed, and contact with customers or the public scores 4.55. The Institute's Speaking Demand Score for the occupation is 65.4 out of 100, list position 392 of 910 <Cite k="onet31" />. Teaching is a large part of the job: O*NET rates it 4.09 out of 5 for importance <Cite k="onet31" />. That often means showing a newer employee how to handle a return, a price dispute, or a customer who wants the manager.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET tasks for this occupation mix selling with supervising. Supervisors greet and assist customers and respond to their questions and complaints. They instruct staff on how to handle difficult and complicated sales, assign employees to duties, plan work schedules, and enforce safety, health, and security rules. In some stores they also hire, train, and evaluate staff <Cite k="onet31" />. Phone calls score 4.74 out of 5 for frequency <Cite k="onet31" />.

A common moment: Kayla, three weeks into the job, waves the supervisor over. A shopper wants to return a blender without a receipt, and Kayla doesn't know the rule. The supervisor could take over. Or the supervisor could stand beside her and say, "Let's look it up together. Without a receipt we can do store credit at the lowest recent price. Do you want to explain that, or should I?" Kayla learns the policy and keeps the conversation.

## What the research says

**Complaints often don't reach the people who could fix them.** Harris and Ogbonna interviewed 25 managers, 25 supervisors, and 57 front-line employees of UK general retailers and supermarkets. Hiding customer complaints was common at every level. Front-line staff misled customers, resolved complaints without logging them, or concealed and falsified records. Supervisors and managers resolved complaints without logging them, concealed or didn't record them, and disregarded "minor" ones. Reasons included self-protection, finding the customer unpleasant, avoiding extra work, and lack of time <Cite k="harris2010complaintconcealment" />. A supervisor who wants to hear about complaints may need to make reporting them safe and easy; the study did not test this.

**Asking store managers to focus on their people reduced quits.** In a randomized trial at a large retail chain, the CEO asked managers of treated stores to do what they could to reduce employee quits. The quit rate fell by a fifth to a quarter for about nine months, faded, and reappeared after a reminder. Sales didn't change, and treated managers spent more time on personnel matters and less on customer service <Cite k="friebel2022middlemanagers" />. In the working-paper version we read, the CEO's letter specifically asked managers to talk to their employees and to keep an open ear for problems new hires might have <Cite k="friebel2022middlemanagers" />.

**Store managers' job attitudes are linked to staff and customer outcomes.** Netemeyer, Maxham, and Lichtenstein analyzed data from 306 store managers, 1,615 customer-contact employees, and 57,656 customers of one retail chain. Their path analysis supported several links between store managers' satisfaction and performance, employees' satisfaction and performance, customer satisfaction, and growth in customer spending over two years <Cite k="netemeyer2010storemanagers" />. The design is correlational, from a single chain.

**Team cohesion connected managers to sales.** At a South Korean electronics chain, 1,611 sales employees rated their store managers' emotional intelligence and their store's cohesiveness. Manager emotional intelligence had no direct link to objective store sales. Instead it was related to cohesiveness, which was related to employees' sales-directed behavior, which predicted store performance <Cite k="wilderom2015storecohesion" />.

**Unstable schedules are linked to worse well-being.** Schneider and Harknett surveyed hourly service-sector workers, drawing on 27,792 retail and food service employees at 80 large companies. Routine instability in work schedules was associated with psychological distress, poor sleep, and unhappiness, much more strongly than low wages were <Cite k="schneider2019schedules" />. The study doesn't test how schedule changes are announced, but supervisors are often the ones delivering them.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Coaching beside the employee.** Help with a hard sale or return in a way that leaves the employee in the conversation. This fits the O*NET task of instructing staff on difficult and complicated sales.
- **Asking for complaints.** Make it routine to ask, "Any customer problems today?" and respond without blame when someone reports one.
- **Checking in with new hires.** Ask short, specific questions in the first weeks, such as what has been confusing or which customer situations felt hard.
- **Announcing schedule changes clearly.** Say what changed, for whom, and why, as early as you can.
- **Settling a complaint on the floor.** Listen to the customer, explain what the store can do, and say who will follow up.

## How to practice

These drills haven't been tested in any study. They adapt the findings above to the sales floor.

**Side-by-side coaching.** A partner plays a new employee and a third person plays a customer with a tricky request, such as price-matching a competitor's online sale. Practice joining the conversation without taking it over: one sentence of policy, then hand the conversation back. Afterward, ask the "employee" whether they could handle the same request alone next time.

**The complaint check-in.** At the end of an invented shift, ask a partner playing an employee about customer problems. Your partner decides privately whether to mention a complaint they handled badly. Practice responding to a reported mistake with a question about what happened and what would help next time, rather than a reprimand. Then talk about what would have made it easier to bring up.

**The new-hire question.** Write three check-in questions for an employee's second week, such as "Which register task still feels slow?" Ask them to a partner and listen without fixing anything until they finish.

**The schedule change.** Invent a change: the Saturday closing shift now ends at 10:30 instead of 10 because of a holiday sale. Announce it to a partner in under 30 seconds, including the reason and who to see about conflicts. Ask them to repeat the new end time.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much do retail supervisors talk with customers versus staff?

Both, all shift. O*NET data scores face-to-face discussions at 4.95 out of 5 and contact with customers or the public at 4.55 <Cite k="onet31" />. BLS estimated 1,121,800 jobs in the occupation in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $48,520 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

### Why don't employees tell me about customer complaints?

Interviews in UK retailers found complaint concealment at every level, with motives that included self-protection and avoiding extra work <Cite k="harris2010complaintconcealment" />. Asking regularly and responding without blame may make reporting easier, though that hasn't been tested.

### Can store managers reduce turnover?

In a field experiment at a retail chain, asking store managers to work on reducing quits lowered the quit rate by a fifth to a quarter for about nine months <Cite k="friebel2022middlemanagers" />. Those managers shifted time toward personnel matters and away from customer service. The study did not isolate talking from other personnel work.
