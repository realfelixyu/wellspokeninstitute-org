Civil engineers do not give speeches often, but their projects depend on constant conversation with contractors, agencies, and the public. O\*NET® data rate speaking 3.88 out of 5 for importance, the "quite important" band, and the occupation ranks 324th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Telephone use is rated 4.90 out of 5 and face-to-face discussion 4.76, both close to every day. The required level of oral expression is 5.00 on a 7-point scale, suggesting the job calls for explaining complex technical ideas well. Research on engineering practice shows that coordinating people by talking with them is a large share of the job.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list for civil engineers includes directing engineering activities and managing construction at project sites, inspecting sites to monitor progress, and providing "technical advice to industrial or managerial personnel regarding design, construction, program modifications, or structural repairs." They also "prepare or present public reports on topics such as bid proposals, deeds, environmental impact statements, or property and right-of-way descriptions" <Cite k="onet31" />.

Public speaking is rated 2.33 out of 5 for frequency, between a few times a year and monthly, and 4.76% of surveyed civil engineers report doing it weekly. Dealing with the public is rated 3.57 out of 5 for importance. When civil engineers do face an audience, it may be residents with strong views about a road, a pipeline, or a flood project near their homes. In May 2025, 367,840 people were employed as civil engineers <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

**Engineering practice is an oral culture.** Darling and Dannels collected practicing engineers' descriptions of why oral communication matters at work. The engineers described practice as taking place in "an intensely oral culture." Formal presentations mattered, but daily work was characterized more by interpersonal and small-group communication. The skills they named as vital were translation of technical ideas for others, clarity, negotiation, and listening <Cite k="darling2003engineers" />.

**Engineers coordinate other people's work through talk.** James Trevelyan interviewed and observed engineers in Australia and found that coordinating the technical work of others, by gaining their willing cooperation, was a major part of engineering practice. Coordination usually ran through one-on-one relationships with superiors, clients, peers, subordinates, and outsiders, and it mattered from the start of an engineering career. Technical expertise was still essential to doing it well. Engineering education gave only limited, informal preparation for this work <Cite k="trevelyan2007coordination" />.

**Communication is among the core engineering competencies.** Passow and Passow systematically reviewed research on which competencies matter for engineering practice, combining 27 quantitative studies with 14,429 participants and 25 qualitative studies plus 36,100 job postings. They concluded that engineers' technical work is "inseparably intertwined" with collaboration, defined communicating effectively as one of 16 generic competencies, and named coordinating multiple competencies to accomplish a goal as the most important skill <Cite k="passow2017competencies" />.

**Public meetings are a required, untrained skill.** Maria Blevins noted that since the National Environmental Policy Act of 1970, government agencies have had to run a public scoping process that includes at least one public meeting, and that engineers in agencies and private firms are expected to facilitate them. Yet engineering programs provide little training in leading public meetings. Drawing on the literature, observations of meetings, and interviews with engineers who run them, she identified practices such as being prepared for difficult questions and some hostility, letting people be heard, listening actively, going to the public early, and admitting mistakes <Cite k="blevins2011publicmeetings" />. These were preliminary findings from a conference paper.

None of these studies focuses only on civil engineers, and most are interview-based. They agree that engineers who can explain, listen, and coordinate are doing much of the real work of engineering.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Translating technical ideas.** Explaining a load limit, a drainage plan, or a schedule change to a client, a council member, or a resident without jargon is the "translation" skill engineers named in Darling and Dannels' study.
- **Facilitating public meetings.** Listening actively, letting people be heard, and answering hard questions honestly are among the practices Blevins identified.
- **Coordinating by phone and in person.** Getting contractors, agencies, and colleagues to do their part on time depends on clear requests and follow-up conversations.
- **Giving technical advice.** Advising managers on design changes or repairs calls for a clear recommendation, the reasoning behind it, and the risks of the alternatives.
- **Presenting public reports.** Bid proposals and environmental impact findings need short, organized presentations that a non-specialist audience can follow.

## How to practice

1. **Explain one design to a non-engineer.** Pick a current design decision and explain it in two minutes to a friend outside engineering. Note every term they ask about and replace it with plain words.
2. **Test your plain-language script.** Paste your public meeting talking points into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and shorten sentences that are hard to follow by ear.
3. **Rehearse the hostile question.** List the three toughest questions residents might ask about your project and practice answering each in under a minute with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer). Acknowledge the concern before giving facts.
4. **Structure coordination calls.** Before calling a contractor or agency, write down the one decision or action you need, the deadline, and who owns it. End each call by restating all three.
5. **Size your presentation.** Draft your next public report presentation and check its length with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator), leaving time for questions.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for civil engineers?

Quite important. O\*NET rates speaking 3.88 out of 5 for civil engineers, and phone and face-to-face conversations are rated as near-daily parts of the job.

### Do civil engineers have to speak in public?

Sometimes. Public speaking is rated 2.33 out of 5 for frequency, but public meetings are a required part of environmental review for many projects, and engineers are often asked to present and answer questions.

### Which communication skills do practicing engineers value most?

In interviews, practicing engineers named translation of technical ideas, clarity, negotiation, and listening as vital, and described most daily communication as interpersonal and small-group rather than formal presentations.
