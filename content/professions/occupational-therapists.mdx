“I'd like to write again” could mean signing a receipt, keeping a journal or sending a birthday card. An occupational therapist needs to find out which part of the person's day those words refer to. “What would you write first?” gives the speaker a chance to name the occasion. Before the conversation moves on, the therapist can put the answer back into words the client recognizes.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Imagine a client who wants to send his granddaughter a birthday card. A therapist might hear “I want to write” and picture a whole page of handwriting. He explains that his daughter can write the message. He wants his granddaughter to open the envelope and recognize his signature. That last detail changes what the therapist needs to convey to a colleague. “He wants to sign her card himself” preserves something that “He wants to work on writing” leaves out.

The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking importance for occupational therapists at 4 out of 5 and speaking level at 4 out of 7. Face-to-face discussions receive a frequency rating of 4.91 out of 5, compared with 2.27 for public speaking. On the Institute's Speaking Demand Score, occupational therapists score higher than 80.8% of the 910 rated occupations, with a score of 70 out of 100 <Cite k="onet31" />.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 162,450 occupational therapist jobs and a median annual wage of $100,330 in May 2025 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. In its separate employment projections, BLS expects the occupation to grow 14.8% between 2025 and 2035 <Cite k="blsoutlook2035" />.

## What goal-setting studies found

In one rehabilitation center, researchers observed goal-setting sessions and interviewed four novice and four experienced occupational therapists. Both groups faced decisions about how much guidance to give clients. The researchers found similarities as well as differences in how the therapists communicated, guided clients and stayed flexible, and they said their findings challenge the assumption that work experience alone makes goal setting more collaborative <Cite k="otgoalsetting2020" />.

A study of 19 people living at home after a stroke offers a glimpse of the range of answers a therapist might hear. Participants identified difficulties across self-care, productive activities and leisure. The most frequently mentioned difficulties in those categories were bathing, maintaining the household and walking outdoors, respectively <Cite k="otgoalpriorities2004" />. If someone says that getting outside matters, a therapist still has a question to ask. What would they most like to do once they're out there?

In another study, nine people who completed a program to support independent living spoke to researchers about their experiences. One participant described how much she wanted to cut her own nails again. For her, depending on someone else for such a personal task had been particularly hard. Other participants spoke about wanting to remain at home or get back to activities they enjoyed <Cite k="otreablement2024" />. Those interviews help explain how the same everyday task could carry different meanings for different people.

## Practice keeping the client's words in a handoff

In a group of three, use the fictional birthday card scene. Have the person playing the client choose privately what they want to do with the card. They might want to sign a name, draw a cartoon or take it to the mailbox on their own. The person playing the therapist gets only the opening line, “I'd like to send a card.”

Ask whatever you need to understand what the client has in mind. Then give a one-sentence account to a third person as though they were the colleague meeting the client tomorrow. Let the client hear what you said. Ask what they would keep or correct. If the client wanted to draw a cartoon and your colleague expects someone who wants help writing a message, go back to the point where “send” became “write.”

This exercise borrows the distinction rehabilitation researchers made between a person's stated activity and the reason that activity matters <Cite k="otreablement2024" />. You can also use the [college classroom worksheet](/curriculum/worksheets/college) to practice comparing what a speaker intended with what another person heard.

## Frequently asked questions

### What can I ask after a client says “I want my independence back”?

Try “What is one thing you'd like to do on your own again?” If the answer is “get the children ready,” ask which part of that routine they want to take back. Their answer might be choosing clothes with a child or walking together to the bus stop.

### How can I describe a goal when the client and a relative use different words?

Give each person a turn and keep track of who said what. In the birthday card example, the daughter might want a clearly addressed envelope while her father wants his signature inside the card. Ask him how he'd like you to describe his goal. You can pass along the daughter's offer to address the envelope separately.

### How do I know if I understood what matters?

Read a short account back while the person can still change it. “You want to sign the card yourself so she'll know who it's from. What would you change about that?” If they correct “sign” to “draw,” use the correction in the handoff.
