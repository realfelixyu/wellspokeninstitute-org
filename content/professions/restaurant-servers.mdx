Serving tables does not look like a speaking job in most rankings, but the conversation at the table is a big part of what a server sells. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking rates 3.5 out of 5 in importance for waiters and waitresses, and the level of speaking skill required is relatively low, at 2.62 on a 7-point scale. The occupation ranks 574 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, at 56.2 <Cite k="onet31" />. One number cuts against that ranking: 43.04% of servers in the O*NET survey said they speak to an audience every day, likely reflecting the routine of describing specials and the menu to each new table. For many servers, tips depend partly on how those short performances go.

There were 2,270,910 waiters and waitresses in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $35,230 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Several O*NET task statements for waiters and waitresses are spoken from start to finish <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Present menus to patrons and answer questions about menu items, making recommendations upon request."
- "Inform customers of daily specials."
- "Explain how various menu items are prepared, describing ingredients and cooking methods."
- "Take orders from patrons for food or beverages."
- "Check with customers to ensure that they are enjoying their meals, and take action to correct any problems."
- "Describe and recommend wines to customers."

Face-to-face discussions average 4.52 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale. Public speaking averages 2.83, which is higher than for 736 of the 910 occupations in our dataset, because so many servers report daily table-side presentations. Telephone conversations average 3.61, mostly for reservations and to-go orders.

## What the research says

Tipping offers researchers an unusual opportunity: a direct, immediate measure of how customers respond to a worker's behavior. A body of field experiments has tested what servers say and do at the table.

**Introducing yourself by name was linked to higher tips.** Garrity and Degelman randomly assigned 42 two-person dining parties at a buffet brunch to a server who either introduced herself by name or did not. With a buffet keeping server contact and bill size fairly constant, parties served by the server who gave her name tipped at a significantly higher rate, 23.4% compared with 15.0%. The authors also found that diners who paid by card tipped more than those who paid cash (22.6% versus 15.9%), and they concluded that the initial server-diner interaction matters <Cite k="garrity1990introduction" />.

**Complimenting customers' choices was linked to higher tips.** Seiter had 2 female servers wait on 94 couples at dinner, either complimenting the couples on their dinner selections or not. Servers received significantly higher tips when they complimented their customers <Cite k="seiter2007ingratiation" />. A short, sincere remark about a good choice is a spoken move that takes seconds.

**Getting down to the guest's eye level also helped.** Lynn and Mynier ran two naturalistic experiments in which servers either stood or squatted down next to the table while interacting with customers. Squatting increased servers' tips from those tables <Cite k="lynn1993posture" />. Posture is nonverbal, but it shapes the setting in which every table conversation happens.

These are small studies, often with only a few servers in one restaurant, so the effects should not be treated as guaranteed. They do point in a consistent direction: friendly, personal touches in the opening and middle of the meal are associated with higher tips.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. A personal introduction.** The name experiment suggests that the first words at the table matter. A simple "Hi, I'm Dana, I'll be taking care of you tonight" turns a transaction into a personal exchange.

**2. Describing specials and dishes clearly.** Informing guests of specials and explaining preparation methods are mini presentations repeated dozens of times a shift. Good descriptions use two or three vivid details and a steady pace, not a memorized blur.

**3. Recommendations and genuine compliments.** Making recommendations on request, and remarking on a good choice, are both linked to how guests perceive the server. Compliments only work if they sound sincere.

**4. Checking in without interrupting.** Checking that guests are enjoying their meal, and fixing problems quickly, requires reading the table and choosing the right moment to speak.

**5. Order accuracy under noise.** Taking orders in a loud dining room depends on clear questions, confirming special requests, and repeating back anything unusual.

## How to practice

- **Time your specials.** Write out tonight's specials and read them aloud. Check them with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator); aim to keep the full recitation under a minute.
- **Slow down the recitation.** After the hundredth repetition, specials get rushed. Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) to keep your pace easy to follow in a noisy room.
- **Make descriptions easy to hear.** Run your menu descriptions through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and cut tongue-twisting ingredient lists down to the two details guests care about.
- **Pause for decisions.** After asking "Have you decided?", wait. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) builds comfort with the short silence while guests choose.
- **Cut the fillers.** Record yourself describing a dish and use the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter) to catch habits like "um" and "like" that make you sound unsure of the menu.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do restaurant servers need public speaking skills?

More than most people expect. In O*NET data, 43.04% of waiters and waitresses report speaking to an audience every day, which fits the routine of presenting the menu and specials to each table.

### Does introducing yourself by name really increase tips?

In one field experiment at a buffet brunch, a server who introduced herself by name received tips averaging 23.4% compared with 15.0% when she did not. It was a small study in one restaurant, so results elsewhere may differ.

### What else do servers say that affects tips?

Experiments have linked complimenting guests' menu choices to higher tips, and squatting next to the table rather than standing has shown a similar effect. For another job built on short customer conversations, see [retail salespersons](/professions/retail-salespersons).
