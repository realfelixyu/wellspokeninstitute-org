Medical assistants are often the first and last person a patient talks to in a clinic visit. O*NET® rates speaking importance for the occupation at 3.88 out of 5, and medical assistants rank 311th of 910 occupations on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. With 817,870 medical assistants employed in May 2025 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />, their conversations at check-in, in the exam room, and on the phone add up to a large share of what patients hear from primary care.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list for medical assistants mixes clinical and administrative talk. Medical assistants "interview patients to obtain medical information and measure their vital signs, weight, and height," "explain treatment procedures, medications, diets, or physicians' instructions to patients," and "greet and log in patients arriving at office or clinic" <Cite k="onet31" />. On the administrative side, they answer phones, schedule appointments, "contact medical facilities or departments to schedule patients for tests or admission," and "authorize drug refills and provide prescription information to pharmacies."

Telephone use is rated 4.37 out of 5, a little higher than face-to-face discussion at 4.27. Only 18 of the 103 health care practitioner and support occupations in the data share that pattern. The importance of dealing with the public is rated 4 and communicating with supervisors, peers, or subordinates 4.43. Public speaking is rated 2.09, though 14.41% of medical assistants report doing it weekly.

Medical assistant talk tends to come in short, repeated formats:

- **Rooming.** A two-minute intake: reason for the visit, medication changes, allergies, and vital signs, delivered to someone who may be in pain or anxious.
- **Relaying the physician's plan.** After the visit, the medical assistant often repeats instructions, explains a referral, or schedules follow-up.
- **The phone.** Refill requests, test scheduling, and calls from patients who want to know whether a symptom needs a visit.

## What the research says

**Medical assistants can be effective health coaches.** A randomized trial at two safety-net primary care clinics in San Francisco tested medical assistants trained as in-clinic health coaches. The 441 patients had poorly controlled diabetes, hypertension, or high cholesterol. After 12 months, 46.4% of coached patients reached at least one clinical goal compared with 34.3% in usual care. Almost twice as many coached patients reached the hemoglobin A1c goal (48.6% vs 27.6%). Blood pressure control did not differ between groups <Cite k="willardgrace2015coaching" />. Coaching is largely conversation: helping patients understand their conditions, set goals, and work through barriers.

**Coaching also improved how patients experienced care.** In the same trial, patients' ratings of their chronic illness care were higher at 12 months in the coached group (3.82 vs 3.13 on the Patient Assessment of Chronic Illness Care scale). Coached patients were also more likely to say they would definitely recommend their clinic, 85% compared with 73% <Cite k="thom2015coaching" />. Both reports come from one trial in two clinics serving low-income patients, so the size of the effect may differ in other settings.

**The role is changing, with gaps in patient education and coaching.** A survey of medical assistants and family physicians in North Carolina primary care practices found broad agreement on what medical assistants should do and little resistance to delegation. The authors identified three areas where the gap between potential and actual roles could be narrowed: population health and panel management; patient education, coaching, and counseling; and scribing <Cite k="fraher2021maroles" />. Two of the three involve talking with patients.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. A focused intake interview.** The interview task sets up the whole visit. Ask open questions first ("What brings you in today?"), then closed questions for medications and allergies, and confirm what you heard before leaving the room.

**2. Relaying instructions accurately.** When explaining a physician's instructions, use the physician's plan word for word for doses and timing, then put the reason in plain language. If you are unsure, ask the physician rather than improvising. Ending with "What questions do you have about this?" gives the patient a clear opening before they leave.

**3. Phone triage language.** Follow the practice's protocol, ask the same key questions every time, and close each call by stating the next step and when it will happen.

**4. Coaching conversations.** The coaching trials depended on medical assistants helping patients set small, specific goals. Ask what the patient wants to work on, agree on one change, and check on it at the next contact.

**5. A calm greeting.** The greeting task is short but sets the tone for the visit. Using the patient's name and telling them what will happen next can help a nervous patient settle.

## How to practice

- **Script your rooming questions.** Write your intake questions in the order you ask them and read them aloud. The [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator) can show whether the script fits in the time you have.
- **Practice phone closings.** Write three closing lines for common calls ("Dr. Patel will review this by 4 p.m., and we'll call you either way") and rehearse them until they come out naturally.
- **Simplify instructions.** Paste a common set of post-visit instructions into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and break up the hard sentences.
- **Record a role-played coaching call.** Have a coworker play a patient with diabetes. Transcribe it and run it through the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter) to spot hesitations in the parts you are less sure of.
- **Check your phone pace.** Callers cannot see you, so pace matters. Use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) on a recorded mock call.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can medical assistants do health coaching?

Yes. In a randomized trial with 441 low-income patients, medical assistants trained as health coaches improved A1c and LDL cholesterol control compared with usual care, though not blood pressure.

### How much of a medical assistant's job is talking?

O*NET rates speaking at 3.88 out of 5 in importance. Telephone conversations averaged 4.37 out of 5 for frequency, and face-to-face discussions averaged 4.27.

### What communication skills should medical assistants build first?

A clear intake interview, accurate relay of physician instructions, and consistent phone call structure cover most daily situations. Coaching skills are a growing part of the role.
