Construction managers run projects largely by talking: on the phone with owners and subcontractors, in meetings with designers, and on site with supervisors and crews. O\*NET® data rate speaking 3.88 out of 5 for importance, and the occupation ranks 270th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Telephone use is rated 4.89 out of 5 and face-to-face discussion 4.68, both close to every day. Field research on construction sites shows that one kind of talk, daily conversation about safety between site leaders and workers, is linked to measurably safer work.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list asks construction managers to "direct and supervise construction or related workers," to "confer with supervisory personnel, owners, contractors, or design professionals to discuss and resolve matters, such as work procedures, complaints, or construction problems," and to "interpret and explain plans and contract terms" to owners' representatives, workers, and clients. They also negotiate contract revisions, investigate accidents and delays, and run training programs <Cite k="onet31" />.

Public speaking is rated 3.15 out of 5 for frequency, with 10% of surveyed managers reporting it daily and 40% weekly, a pattern that fits regular site meetings and briefings. Dealing with the public is rated 3.90 out of 5 for importance. In May 2025, 380,360 people were employed as construction managers, with a median annual wage of 114,990 dollars <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## What the research says

**Daily safety talk from site leaders improved safety.** Kines and colleagues studied five construction work gangs over up to 42 weeks. At baseline, foremen talked with their workers several times a day, but workers saw safety as part of only 6% to 16% of those exchanges. Foremen in two intervention groups were coached and given feedback every two weeks on their daily verbal safety communication. Safety communication between foremen and workers increased significantly overall, and measured site safety rose by 7% and 12% in the two intervention groups, while the three control groups showed no significant change <Cite k="kines2010safety" />. The authors recommend coaching and feedback at every organizational level.

**Training foremen to give toolbox talks changed site behavior.** Kaskutas, Dale, Lipscomb, and Evanoff noted that falls account for 64% of residential construction worker fatalities. They piloted an 8-hour training for ten foremen at a residential builder that covered fall prevention and how to deliver toolbox talks and safety messages. After training, daily mentoring and toolbox talks became more frequent, more interactive, and more focused on the hazardous tasks of the day. Worksite audits found more consistent use of fall protection and fewer unsafe behaviors <Cite k="kaskutas2013foremen" />. It was a small pilot that compared sites before and after training.

**Accuracy is the first requirement of project communication.** Thomas, Tucker, and Kelly, working with the Construction Industry Institute, identified and measured critical communication variables on engineering, procurement, and construction projects. They grouped them into six categories, in order of importance: accuracy, procedures, barriers, understanding, timeliness, and completeness <Cite k="thomas1998communications" />. The study looked at project communication in general, and each category applies to a site meeting or a phone call.

The two safety studies are small, but they point the same way: on a construction site, what a leader chooses to talk about each day shapes what workers pay attention to.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Bringing safety into everyday talk.** Mentioning a specific hazard or safe method during routine conversations with crews is the behavior Kines' team coached, and it was linked to safer sites.
- **Interactive toolbox talks.** Short talks that involve the crew and focus on the day's most hazardous tasks are what the trained foremen moved toward.
- **Explaining plans and contract terms.** Turning drawings and contract language into plain instructions for workers, and into clear implications for owners, is a core O\*NET task.
- **Accurate, complete information.** Accuracy ranked first among communication variables. Confirming key details aloud prevents costly mistakes.
- **Resolving problems and complaints.** Conferring with owners, contractors, and designers about problems calls for calm, specific discussion and a clear agreed next step.

## How to practice

1. **Add one safety line a day.** For two weeks, include one specific safety point in each routine conversation with a foreman or crew, tied to the work they are doing that day.
2. **Plan a five-minute toolbox talk.** Choose one hazard for tomorrow's work, write two questions to ask the crew about it, and check the talk's length with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).
3. **Confirm critical instructions.** When you give an instruction that matters for safety or cost, ask the listener to repeat back what they will do, and correct any gaps on the spot.
4. **Translate a clause.** Pick a contract clause or change order and explain it out loud in plain words as if to an owner's representative. Run your explanation through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker).
5. **Structure your phone calls.** Before calling a subcontractor or owner, write the issue, the decision you need, and the deadline. Practice stating all three in under a minute with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer).

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for construction managers?

Quite important. O\*NET rates speaking 3.88 out of 5 for construction managers, and telephone and face-to-face conversations are rated as near-daily parts of the job.

### Do toolbox talks improve safety?

In a pilot study, foremen trained to deliver toolbox talks held them more often and made them more interactive, and their sites showed better fall protection compliance. The study was small and compared sites before and after training.

### What does safety communication mean on a construction site?

It means talking about hazards and safe methods as part of daily work. In one field study, coaching foremen to do this more often raised measured safety levels at their sites.
