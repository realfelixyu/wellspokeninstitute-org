A sales manager's numbers come from other people's conversations, so the manager's own speaking is mostly aimed at the sales team. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 4 out of 5 in importance for sales managers and places the occupation 254th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, at 69 <Cite k="onet31" />. Two ratings tell the more specific story. Telephone conversations average 5.0 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale, the top of the scale. Training and teaching others rates 4.33 out of 5 for importance, higher than in 49 of the 55 management occupations in our dataset. Sales managers are, in large part, coaches who talk all day.

In May 2025, 637,080 people worked as sales managers, with a median annual wage of $148,270 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task list mixes team leadership with customer and channel conversations <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Oversee regional and local sales managers and their staffs."
- "Plan and direct staffing, training, and performance evaluations to develop and control sales and service programs."
- "Resolve customer complaints regarding sales and service."
- "Confer with potential customers regarding equipment needs, and advise customers on types of equipment to purchase."
- "Visit franchised dealers to stimulate interest in establishment or expansion of leasing programs."
- "Represent company at trade association meetings to promote products."

Besides phone calls at 5.0, face-to-face discussions average 4.65. Dealing with external customers or the public rates 4.71 out of 5 for importance. Public speaking averages 2.71: 19.05% of respondents speak to an audience weekly, typically in sales meetings and at dealer or association events, and none reported doing so daily.

## What the research says

**Coaching skill predicts sales results, and frequent low-skill coaching hurts.** Dahling, Taylor, Chau, and Dwight followed 1,246 sales representatives in 136 teams at a pharmaceutical company for a year. Managers' coaching skill was assessed in a training exercise. That skill was directly related to how well the representatives they supervised met their annual sales goals, and part of the effect ran through clearer understanding of roles across the team. The striking result concerned frequency: when a manager's coaching skill was low, coaching more often had a negative effect on goal attainment <Cite k="dahling2016coaching" />. More one-on-ones only help if the conversations are good.

**Positive feedback does double duty, and negative feedback is not as damaging as managers fear.** Jaworski and Kohli classified supervisory feedback to salespeople by whether it addressed outcomes or behaviors, and whether it was positive or negative. Positive feedback served both an informational and a motivational function and had a significant effect on performance and satisfaction. Negative feedback was informational but not motivational and improved performance to a smaller extent. Positive feedback about outcomes had the strongest total effect on performance, while positive feedback about behavior had the strongest effect on satisfaction. Neither type of negative feedback seemed to reduce salespeople's satisfaction with their supervisors <Cite k="jaworski1991feedback" />.

**How leaders talk relates to performance and satisfaction.** Mayfield, Mayfield, and Kopf tested "motivating language theory," which describes three kinds of leader talk: giving direction, expressing empathy and sharing feelings, and explaining the organization's culture and norms. Supervisors' use of motivating language correlated significantly with their subordinates' performance and job satisfaction <Cite k="mayfield1998motivating" />. The study was not limited to sales teams, but these three kinds of talk map closely onto a sales manager's weekly conversations.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Coaching conversations that build skill.** A strong coaching conversation reviews a specific call or deal, asks the salesperson to diagnose it first, models one better approach, and ends with a concrete goal. Given Dahling's findings, investing in coaching quality comes before adding more sessions.

**2. Specific, mostly positive feedback.** Naming exactly what a salesperson did well on a call, and the result it produced, carries both information and motivation. Corrective feedback still works when it is specific, and the research suggests it does not damage the relationship.

**3. Clear direction.** Role clarity was one route through which coaching improved results. Telling the team plainly which accounts, products, and behaviors matter this quarter is direction-giving talk in its simplest form.

**4. Taking over difficult customer conversations.** Resolving escalated complaints and advising major prospects requires a calmer, more senior voice than the rep's. Good managers listen fully before offering a fix.

**5. Rallying a room.** Weekly sales meetings and trade association talks call for short, energetic remarks with one message and one call to action.

## How to practice

- **Record a coaching session.** With permission, record a one-on-one and listen for the ratio of your talk to the rep's. Aim to ask before you tell.
- **Feedback scripts.** Write three examples of positive outcome feedback and three of positive behavioral feedback for real team members. Read them aloud and cut any that sound generic.
- **Sales meeting openers.** Draft a two-minute opening for your next sales meeting and time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator). Rehearse it with the [teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) until you can deliver it without notes.
- **Slow down with upset customers.** Practice an escalation call with a colleague playing the customer. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you leave space for the customer to finish before you respond.
- **Check your pace on the phone.** Since sales managers rate phone calls at the top of the frequency scale, check your phone pace with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator), especially when explaining pricing or policy.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should sales managers coach their reps?

The research points to quality first. In a study of 1,246 pharmaceutical sales representatives, more frequent coaching helped only when the manager's coaching skill was high. With low skill, more coaching was associated with worse goal attainment.

### Does negative feedback hurt a sales manager's relationship with the team?

In Jaworski and Kohli's study, negative feedback about outcomes or behaviors did not appear to lower salespeople's satisfaction with their supervisors, though it had a smaller effect on performance than positive feedback.

### How is a sales manager's speaking different from a sales representative's?

Sales managers spend more of their talk teaching, coaching, and directing the team, while [sales representatives](/professions/sales-representatives) spend most of theirs with customers, asking questions and adapting their pitch.
