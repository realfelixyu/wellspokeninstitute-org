Executive assistants do a great deal of talking, almost none of it in front of an audience. The O*NET® 31.0 Database rates speaking at 3.88 out of 5 in importance for executive secretaries and executive administrative assistants, and the occupation ranks 326 of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score at 67.5 <Cite k="onet31" />. Public speaking is rare, averaging 1.41 on the O*NET 1 to 5 frequency scale. Phone calls, on the other hand, average 4.99. Executive assistants speak on behalf of senior leaders all day: screening callers, briefing visitors, coordinating with other executives' offices, and relaying decisions. They also hold a delicate second role, telling those same leaders things they may not want to hear.

The occupation employed 459,910 people in May 2025, with a median annual wage of $76,590 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O*NET task statements show how much of the role is spoken representation <Cite k="onet31" />:

- "Answer phone calls and direct calls to appropriate parties or take messages."
- "Greet visitors and determine whether they should be given access to specific individuals."
- "Meet with individuals, special interest groups, and others on behalf of executives, committees, and boards of directors."
- "Interpret administrative and operating policies and procedures for employees."
- "Prepare agendas and make arrangements, such as coordinating catering for luncheons, for committee, board, and other meetings."
- "Supervise and train other clerical staff and arrange for employee training by scheduling training or organizing training material."

Face-to-face discussions average 4.65 on the frequency scale. Communicating with people outside the organization rates 4.45 out of 5 for importance and communicating with people inside it rates 4.64. In the O*NET survey, no executive assistants reported presenting to an audience weekly and only 0.2% reported doing so daily.

## What the research says

Research focused specifically on executive assistants' speech is limited, so the studies below cover the occupation's working conditions and two parts of the job with strong research bases: speaking up to senior people and the meetings assistants organize.

**The role is changing for some, but not for most.** Truss, Alfes, Shantz, and Rosewarne surveyed 1,011 secretaries. Compared with the past, secretaries were better qualified and generally well paid, and a minority were taking on complex managerial tasks. Most, however, still performed traditional tasks, and career prospects remained poor. The authors concluded that secretarial work remains largely a "ghetto" occupation shaped by entrenched gender-typing <Cite k="truss2013secretarial" />. For assistants who want the broader role, speaking up about their own contributions is part of making that shift.

**People hold unspoken rules that keep them quiet with senior leaders.** Detert and Edmondson studied "implicit voice theories," taken-for-granted beliefs about when and why speaking up at work is risky or inappropriate. Interviews in a large corporation suggested that such beliefs underlie reluctance to raise even pro-organizational suggestions. Across four studies, they found these theories were widely held and added to the explanation of workplace silence <Cite k="detert2011implicit" />. Executive assistants work closely with senior leaders, so they often see problems early and may feel the same pressure to stay quiet.

**Meeting quality matters to everyone who attends.** Rogelberg, Leach, Warr, and Burnfield surveyed full-time employees about the meetings they attended. The relationship between meeting time demands and employees' job attitudes and well-being depended on how interdependent their work was, the quality of their meeting experience, and their drive to accomplish things. Perceived meeting effectiveness had a strong, direct relationship with job attitudes and well-being <Cite k="rogelberg2006meetings" />. Assistants who prepare agendas and arrange meetings influence whether those meetings feel worthwhile.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Gatekeeping with tact.** Determining "whether they should be given access" means telling callers and visitors no, or not now, without damaging the relationship with the executive. Offering a specific alternative ("She can call you Thursday at 2") softens the refusal.

**2. Representing the executive accurately.** Meeting with individuals and groups "on behalf of executives" requires saying exactly what the executive authorized, no more and no less, and stating clearly what you will take back for a decision.

**3. Speaking up to the executive.** The research on implicit voice theories suggests that many people stay silent because of untested assumptions about risk. Raising a scheduling conflict, a relationship problem, or a mistake early, briefly, and with a suggested fix gives the executive time to act.

**4. Shaping better meetings.** Preparing agendas is partly a spoken skill: clarifying with the executive what each meeting is for, and confirming with attendees what they need to bring.

**5. Explaining procedures to staff.** Interpreting policies for employees, and training clerical staff, calls for a clear, patient explanation style different from the shorthand used with the executive.

## How to practice

- **Gatekeeping phrases.** Write five ways to decline or redirect a request for the executive's time, each with a concrete alternative. Practice them aloud until they sound warm rather than scripted.
- **Upward briefing in 60 seconds.** Choose a real concern you have not raised. Script it as a one-minute briefing (issue, impact, suggestion) and time it with the [speech length calculator](/tools/speech-length-calculator).
- **Phone clarity.** Check your phone pace with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator), especially when relaying times, names, and dial-in details.
- **Plain-language policy explanations.** Paste your explanation of an office procedure into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and simplify anything a new employee would struggle to follow.
- **Self-introductions.** When representing the executive to new contacts, a short, confident introduction helps. Use the [elevator pitch builder](/tools/elevator-pitch-builder) to prepare one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do executive assistants need public speaking skills?

Rarely in the formal sense. O*NET data shows public speaking at 1.41 out of 5 for frequency, with no respondents presenting weekly and only 0.2% daily. Phone and face-to-face conversation skills matter much more.

### How can an executive assistant raise concerns with a senior leader?

Research on implicit voice theories shows that many people avoid speaking up because of assumptions about risk that they never test. A short, private briefing that names the issue, its impact, and a suggested fix is a practical way to start.

### How does this role compare with a receptionist's?

Both are phone-heavy, but executive assistants communicate on behalf of specific leaders and handle more confidential and outside-facing conversations. See the page on receptionists for front desk research.
