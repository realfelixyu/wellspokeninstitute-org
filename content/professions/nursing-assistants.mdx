A nursing assistant needs to hear what a person is asking for and carry that meaning into the next conversation. A resident can tell an assistant exactly why she rang the call bell. The nurse who follows up may hear only the assistant's account. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking has an importance rating of 3.12 out of 5 for nursing assistants. The occupation ranks 594th of 910 on the Wellspoken Institute Speaking Demand Score, at 54.9 out of 100 <Cite k="onet31" />.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Answering a call light starts with finding out what the person wants. O*NET lists that task alongside talking with patients about how they feel, identifying a need for emotional support, and reporting observations or complaints to nurses. Face-to-face discussion has a frequency rating of 3.85 out of 5. Formal public speaking is less frequent, at 2.23 <Cite k="onet31" />. The audience for much of this work is one person.

Imagine a resident has rung for help before lunch. His coat is on his lap. The assistant could assume he wants to leave the table. She asks, “What would you like help with?” He explains that his daughter usually visits before lunch and he wants his phone from the coat pocket. Once he has it, he asks to stay at the table. His first gesture alone would have made a poor handoff. The question gave him a chance to say what mattered.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated 1,448,910 nursing assistant jobs in May 2025 <Cite k="blsoews2025" />. In its separate national projections, BLS expects employment in this occupation to grow 2.6% from 2025 to 2035 <Cite k="blsoutlook2035" />. Those figures cover the full occupation. The studies below looked specifically at communication in long-term care.

## What the research says

Maria Carpiac-Claver and Lené Levy-Storms examined videos of nurse aides helping residents at mealtimes in two skilled nursing facilities and one assisted living facility. Alongside reminders to eat or drink, the researchers saw aides making personal conversation, acknowledging individual residents, asking how they were, and offering encouragement <Cite k="carpiacclaver2007manner" />. A meal gives an assistant something practical to do and someone to spend time with. The observations show what those conversations sounded like. They leave open which approach a particular resident will welcome.

Louis Burgio and colleagues studied 64 certified nursing assistants (CNAs) and 67 residents. Some units received communication training, memory books for use in conversation, and staff support to keep using the new skills. Compared with aides on units without the program, trained aides spoke more and made positive remarks more often. The researchers found no increase in total time spent giving care. They could still detect changes in aides' behavior two months after the researchers left. Most measures of residents' own communication showed no change <Cite k="burgio2001cometalk" />. The results apply to the program as a whole. The contribution of the memory books on their own remains uncertain.

Christen Page and Graham Rowles asked 23 CNAs in focus groups about talking with residents and colleagues. The assistants described learning residents' ways of communicating through regular contact. They also described a divide between the staff who knew a resident's small habits and people higher in the organization who could act on their concerns <Cite k="page2016gettoknow" />. This was the assistants' account of working relationships. It raises a practical question for the listener at handoff, too. If an aide says, “That was unusual for her,” asking what was different gives the aide room to explain.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**Ask a question the person can correct.** Suppose a resident starts folding a sweater while the assistant is gathering laundry. “Would you like this put away?” leaves room for “I'm setting it out for my granddaughter.” Wait long enough to hear the answer before reaching for the sweater. If the person uses gestures, say what you think the gesture means and give them a chance to correct you.

**Keep the resident's words in the report.** A fictional resident asks to take his newspaper to breakfast because he wants to finish the crossword with a friend. “He kept asking for things at breakfast” strips away what the next assistant could use. “He asked for his newspaper so he could do the crossword with Mr. Lee” preserves both the request and the reason. For a concern that belongs with a nurse, use the reporting process in your workplace and identify what you saw or heard yourself.

**Check what the listener understood.** A useful follow-up can be as short as “Did I get that right?” when an assistant has repeated a resident's request. At a shift change, leave time for the incoming assistant to ask about a detail. In the crossword example, the friend is waiting at breakfast. Passing along only the newspaper request makes it easy to miss the timing.

## How to practice

Work in pairs using an invented resident and an ordinary household object. One person plays a resident who wants something done with the object and privately chooses a reason. The other plays the assistant. Start with a gesture or a few words. The assistant can ask a question, listen, and repeat the request in their own words. The resident can amend the account until both agree on what was meant.

Now add a third person as the incoming assistant. Give that person a one-sentence report. Ask them to tell the group what they think the resident wants and when. Compare their answer with the resident's original request. If the meaning changed, listen for the word or detail that disappeared. Try the handoff again with that detail restored. To rehearse leaving room after a question, use the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) with the same fictional scene.

## Frequently asked questions

### What can a CNA say when a resident's request is unclear?

Describe the part you understood and ask about the missing piece. “You'd like the chair moved. Where would you like it?” lets the resident supply the direction without having to start over.

### How should a nursing assistant practice a shift report?

Use an invented example and separate what the person said from what the assistant observed. Have a partner repeat the report back. Differences between the two versions show which details need clearer wording.

### Is public speaking a major part of a nursing assistant's job?

The O*NET work-context ratings put face-to-face discussions ahead of speeches or presentations <Cite k="onet31" />. A practice partner playing a resident or an incoming colleague gives a nursing assistant a realistic audience.
