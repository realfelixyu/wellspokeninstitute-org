Family medicine is one of the most speech-heavy jobs in the U.S. economy. The occupation ranks 20th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score, and O*NET® rates oral expression at 4.88 and oral comprehension at 4.88 on a 5-point importance scale, both near the ceiling <Cite k="onet31" />. Almost every part of the visit, from the history to the treatment plan, is carried by talk, and the patient may be a newborn's parent in one room and an 85-year-old with five conditions in the next.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

Several O*NET task statements for family medicine physicians are spoken by definition. Physicians "explain procedures and discuss test results or prescribed treatments with patients," "advise patients and community members concerning diet, activity, hygiene, and disease prevention," and "direct and coordinate activities of nurses, students, assistants, specialists, therapists, and other medical staff" <Cite k="onet31" />. They also coordinate with social workers, pharmacists, psychologists, and rehabilitation therapists, and refer patients to specialists.

The work context ratings confirm the pattern. Face-to-face discussion is rated 5 out of 5, telephone use 4.66, and the importance of dealing with the public 4.6. Formal public speaking is rare at 1.26, so the speaking demand comes almost entirely from one-on-one and small-group conversation rather than presentations.

The family physician's talk also spans a wide range of registers in one day:

- Gathering a history from a patient who has saved up several concerns for one visit.
- Explaining a new diagnosis such as type 2 diabetes in terms that support years of self-management.
- Discussing prevention, from vaccines to alcohol use, with patients who did not come in for that.
- Coordinating with a nurse, a pharmacist, and a specialist, often by phone.

## What the research says

**Physician empathy is linked to better diabetes control.** A study at a university-affiliated practice followed 891 patients with diabetes treated by 29 family physicians. Physicians completed a validated empathy scale. Patients of high-empathy physicians were more likely to have good hemoglobin A1c control (56% vs 40%) and good LDL cholesterol control (59% vs 44%) than patients of low-empathy physicians, and the association held after adjusting for physician and patient age and gender and insurance type <Cite k="hojat2011empathy" />. The design was correlational, so it shows an association, not proof that empathy caused the better results.

**How the visit sounds is associated with malpractice claims.** Researchers audiotaped 10 routine visits each from 59 primary care physicians (general internists and family practitioners) and 65 surgeons, then compared physicians with and without prior malpractice claims. In primary care, physicians without claims used more orienting statements (telling patients what to expect and how the visit would flow), laughed and used humor more, and tended to do more facilitation, such as soliciting opinions, checking understanding, and encouraging patients to talk. Their routine visits were also longer, 18.3 minutes compared with 15.0. The same differences did not appear among surgeons <Cite k="levinson1997malpractice" />.

**Communication affects adherence, and training helps.** A meta-analysis of 106 correlational studies and 21 intervention studies found a 19% higher risk of non-adherence among patients whose physician communicates poorly. When physicians received communication training, the odds of patient adherence were 1.62 times higher than without training <Cite k="zolnierek2009adherence" />. Family physicians manage many long-term conditions, where adherence has to hold for years.

## Speaking skills that matter most

**1. Hearing the whole agenda first.** Family physicians often see patients who arrive with several concerns. Asking "What else?" until the patient says "That's it," before diving into the first problem, lets the physician set priorities with the patient instead of discovering the most important concern at the door.

**2. Orienting statements.** The malpractice study points to a cheap habit: telling the patient what is about to happen. "First I'll examine your knee, then we'll talk about options" reduces uncertainty and gives the patient a map of the visit.

**3. Expressed empathy.** Empathy only helps if the patient can hear it. Naming the emotion ("That sounds exhausting") and pausing before moving to the plan turns private concern into something the patient can perceive.

**4. Explaining a plan in steps.** The explain-and-discuss task covers results, procedures, and treatments. Break the plan into two or three steps, state the reason for each, and check understanding before adding more.

**5. Prevention advice that fits the visit.** Advising on diet, activity, and disease prevention works better as a short, specific suggestion tied to what the patient came in for than as a general lecture.

## How to practice

- **Write your standard openers.** Draft the first two sentences you say when entering the room, including an agenda-setting question, and read them aloud until they sound natural.
- **Check a diagnosis explanation for clarity.** Paste your usual explanation of a common new diagnosis into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and shorten any sentence it flags.
- **Measure your pace on results.** Record yourself explaining a lab result, then use the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) to check whether you speed up when explaining numbers.
- **Practice pausing after empathy.** The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you build a two-second gap after an empathic statement so it does not run straight into the next instruction.
- **Review a recorded role-play.** With a colleague playing a patient, record a visit and count orienting statements and checks of understanding. Both are easy to add once you notice their absence.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a family physician's communication affect health outcomes?

Studies link physician empathy to better diabetes control and poor communication to lower adherence, and training in communication raised adherence in intervention studies. Most of the outcome evidence is correlational, so the size of the causal effect is uncertain.

### Are communication habits related to malpractice claims?

In one study of primary care physicians, those without malpractice claims oriented patients more, used more humor, checked understanding more, and had longer visits. The same pattern did not appear among surgeons.

### Do family physicians give presentations?

Rarely. O*NET rates public speaking at 1.26 out of 5 for the occupation, while face-to-face discussion is rated 5, the maximum.
