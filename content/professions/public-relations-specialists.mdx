Public relations specialists speak on behalf of organizations, and much of their value shows when something goes wrong. O\*NET® data rate speaking 4.12 out of 5 for importance and rate communicating inside the organization 4.77 and outside it 4.76 for importance. The occupation ranks 96th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score <Cite k="onet31" />. Experimental research on crisis communication shows that when a spokesperson speaks, and how their voice sounds, both affect how the public judges the organization.

## Where speaking shows up in the job

The O\*NET task list begins with responding "to requests for information from the media" or designating "an appropriate spokesperson or information source." Specialists also coach client representatives "in effective communication with the public or with employees," prepare or deliver speeches, arrange public appearances and lectures for clients, confer with managers about trends and concerns, and build relationships with community, consumer, and employee groups <Cite k="onet31" />.

Face-to-face discussion and telephone use are each rated 4.82 out of 5 for frequency, close to every day. Formal public speaking is rated 2.95: no surveyed specialists reported it daily, and 22.73% reported it weekly. The typical day is phone calls with reporters and meetings with executives, with occasional moments in front of a camera or a crowd.

## What the research says

**Disclosing a crisis first protects reputation, and emotion helps when you do.** Claeys, Cauberghe, and Leysen ran an experiment with 168 participants comparing two timing strategies: the organization disclosing a crisis itself ("stealing thunder") or responding after the news broke elsewhere. They crossed timing with message framing, either rational or expressing sadness. Self-disclosure restored reputation better than responding later. When the organization disclosed first, a spokesperson who expressed sadness produced a better reputation than a rational message, because people saw the spokesperson as more sincere. Framing made no difference when the organization responded after others broke the news <Cite k="claeys2013thunder" />.

**A lower voice signals control during a crisis.** Claeys and Cauberghe examined nonverbal signs of power in organizational spokespersons. In the first study, a spokesperson speaking with a lowered voice pitch appeared more competent than one with a raised pitch. In the second, powerful nonverbal behavior during an active crisis reduced reputational damage by making the spokesperson seem more competent. In the aftermath, however, less powerful nonverbal behavior helped the organization's reputation by making the spokesperson seem more sincere <Cite k="claeys2014control" />.

**A sad voice builds empathy and costs some authority.** De Waele, Schoofs, and Claeys tested how sadness expressed through a CEO's voice affected the public during a crisis. A sad voice increased empathy toward the CEO, which improved attitudes toward the organization. The same voice also made the CEO seem less powerful, which hurt attitudes. In a second experiment, the loss of perceived power appeared only during the trigger event of the crisis, while the empathy benefit appeared at every stage. The authors conclude that spokespersons should not be afraid to let genuine emotion show in their voices <Cite k="dewaele2020empathy" />.

These are controlled experiments with fictional or simulated crises, so real-world effects may be smaller or larger. They point to timing and vocal delivery as levers a spokesperson can control. They also suggest the right delivery depends on the stage of the crisis: a voice that projects control helped most while events were unfolding, and a softer, more emotional tone carried less cost once the acute phase had passed.

## Speaking skills that matter most

- **Speaking first.** Getting the organization's own account out before others do is the strategy best supported in this research. That requires holding statements prepared in advance.
- **Vocal control under pressure.** A steady, lower-pitched delivery projected competence during the acute phase of a crisis in these experiments.
- **Calibrated emotion.** Genuine sadness or concern, expressed in the voice, increased perceived sincerity and empathy. The timing matters: early in a crisis it also cost some perceived power.
- **Coaching others.** When executives speak for the organization, the O\*NET task of coaching representatives includes teaching delivery as well as drafting messages.
- **Media phone work.** Fast, accurate answers to reporters' calls, including a clear "I'll find out and call you back by 3 p.m." when you do not know, maintain trust.

## How to practice

1. **Write a 30-second holding statement.** Draft what your organization would say in the first hour of a likely crisis: what happened, what you are doing, and when you will update. Time it with the [interview answer timer](/tools/interview-answer-timer).
2. **Record two deliveries.** Read the statement once in your normal voice and once slower and slightly lower. Listen back and note which sounds more in control.
3. **Practice a sincere tone.** Read a statement of regret aloud, focusing on pace and pauses rather than adjectives. Compare recordings with a colleague and agree on which sounds genuine.
4. **Prepare a spokesperson.** Run an executive through a mock press conference with tough questions, and use the [free teleprompter](/tools/teleprompter) for the opening statement so they can keep eye contact.
5. **Check statements for the ear.** Paste your draft into the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) and simplify any sentence that is hard to say in one breath.

## Frequently asked questions

### How important is speaking for public relations specialists?

Very important. O\*NET rates speaking 4.12 out of 5 for this occupation, and it ranks 96th of 910 on our Speaking Demand Score.

### Should an organization announce bad news before the media does?

Experiments on "stealing thunder" found that organizations that disclosed a crisis themselves kept a better reputation than those that responded after the news broke elsewhere.

### Should a spokesperson show emotion in a crisis?

Experimental evidence suggests genuine sadness can increase empathy and perceived sincerity. Early in a crisis it may also make a spokesperson seem less powerful, so timing matters.
