It depends on what you mean by persuasive. A speaker who looks toward the audience can earn higher ratings for persuasiveness. But when students watched videos arguing mostly against views they already held, those asked to watch the speaker’s eyes changed their minds less than those asked to watch the mouth. The speaker faced the camera in both groups. The experiment changed where the listener looked <Cite k="yokoyama2012persuasivegaze,chen2013persuasiongaze" />.

## What happened when listeners already had an opinion?

Frances Chen and colleagues first tracked the eyes of 20 students watching videos about controversial issues. The students chose where to look. Those who spent more time looking at the speaker’s eyes tended to move less toward the speaker’s position. Because the viewers chose for themselves, that finding leaves open whether their views influenced where they looked <Cite k="chen2013persuasiongaze" />.

The researchers then assigned 42 other students at random to focus on the speakers’ eyes or mouths. Each saw four videos on issues such as university tuition and nuclear energy. The researchers chose arguments opposing each viewer’s stated position. For the 16% of cases in which the viewer had expressed no opinion, they showed the majority view <Cite k="chen2013persuasiongaze" />.

The mouth group shifted an average of 0.60 points toward the speakers’ positions on a seven-point scale. The eyes group shifted 0.14 points. By our calculation, that is a 0.46-point difference between the reported averages. Both averages moved toward the speakers. Looking at the eyes reduced the size of the shift in this experiment <Cite k="chen2013persuasiongaze" />.

## Can someone look convincing without changing my mind?

Suppose you think a presenter made a convincing case and still plan to vote against the proposal. Two experiments illustrate why those judgments can come apart. In one, viewers rated a woman as more persuasive when she directed more gaze toward them. In another, viewers decided whether to believe unfamiliar trivia statements delivered by one speaker. They accepted more statements overall when she looked toward them. Only one of the two comparisons with a sideways glance was statistically significant after the researchers adjusted for multiple comparisons <Cite k="yokoyama2012persuasivegaze,kreysa2016gaze" />.

## What does a listener’s gaze tell me in a real conversation?

In face-to-face discussions of political issues, participants talked with someone working with the researchers who either agreed or disagreed with them. Participants looked toward that person more while listening than while speaking. Compared with a conversation on a neutral topic, the gap widened during disagreement, especially because listeners looked toward their partner more. Someone looking right at you may still have an objection <Cite k="tyler2021conversationgaze" />.

## How could I practice checking what landed?

Imagine you and a colleague disagree about moving a weekly meeting to Friday. Give your reason in a sentence or two, then ask which part of the proposal would matter most to them. Later, compare what each of you remembers with what each of you would choose. You can recall an argument accurately and still want the meeting to stay where it is. This is an untested practice exercise.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did anyone repeat the eyes-versus-mouth experiment?

The same team followed its observation with a randomized experiment and again found less movement toward the speaker among viewers looking at the eyes. We could not verify an independent study that repeated the eyes-versus-mouth instructions and measured opinion change. The face-to-face study measured where people looked in conversation <Cite k="chen2013persuasiongaze,tyler2021conversationgaze" />.

### Does looking into a camera help people remember a presentation?

In a video study about two fictional soap products, viewers recalled more information when the presenter looked into the camera for part of the presentation. A second group heard the same recordings with the picture turned off, and their recall did not differ significantly between conditions. The study measured memory for details, so it cannot tell us which soap people would buy <Cite k="fullwood2006videorecall" />.

### How long should I hold eye contact to make a point?

These studies give no time target for changing someone’s mind. The political-video experiment assigned listeners a part of the face to watch. It never assigned speakers a number of seconds to look at someone <Cite k="chen2013persuasiongaze" />.
