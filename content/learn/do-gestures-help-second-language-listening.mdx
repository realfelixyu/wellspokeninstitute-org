Often, yes, especially for listeners who are still building their English. Learners in two studies understood more when they could see a speaker's face and hands than when they only heard the voice. How much the gestures add depends on how well the listener already knows the language, what the gestures show and how clear the sound is. If you are learning English, sit where you can see the speaker, and turn on video for calls when you can.

## What happened when learners watched a lecture?

In one experiment, 42 learners of English as a second language watched a videotaped lecture by a native speaker. They were split across 3 viewing conditions, an average of 14 learners per condition: video showing the speaker's gestures and face, video showing the face without gestures, or audio only. Learners at both a low-intermediate and an advanced level scored better on a comprehension test when they could see the speaker. The advanced learners scored highest when they saw the face alone. The lower-level learners scored highest when they could also see the gestures <Cite k="sueyoshi2005gestures" />.

A second study worked with seventh and eighth graders in the United States and Norway. Some were listening in their first language and some were learning English as a foreign language. A speaker described a cartoon the students couldn't see, and each student drew what they heard. Seeing the speaker's gestures made little difference to the students listening in their first language. For the foreign-language learners, gestures made the difference: with them, their drawings fell within the native listeners' range, and without them, comprehension and recall dropped <Cite k="dahl2014gestures" />.

## Which gestures help most?

A meta-analysis combined 63 samples that compared speech alone with speech plus gestures. Overall, gestures gave listeners a moderate benefit. Gestures showing physical actions helped more than gestures about abstract topics. They also helped more when they added information the words left out, and children gained more than adults <Cite k="hostetter2011gestures" />.

Listeners seem to combine hand movements and words without trying. In one set of experiments, people were slower and less accurate when a gesture conflicted with the spoken word, for example hearing "chop" while seeing a twisting motion. A follow-up experiment suggested that listeners take in the gesture whether or not they mean to <Cite k="kelly2010integrated" />.

## Does seeing the speaker help when the sound is bad?

It can. In a lab study, 20 listeners watched an actress say action verbs while the audio was distorted to different degrees. Their answers were more accurate when they could see her lips and a gesture together than when they saw only one or neither. The extra help was larger when the audio was moderately distorted than when it was severely distorted <Cite k="drijvers2017visual" />.

The same researchers then tested highly proficient non-native listeners. Compared with native listeners, they were less likely to benefit from seeing lips and gestures together, especially when the audio was too poor for the lip movements to help much <Cite k="drijvers2020nonnative" />. For a second-language listener, video helps, but it does not make up for bad sound. If a call is hard to hear, ask the speaker to move closer to the microphone, say it again or type the key detail. ## Practice listening with and without the picture

Find a short video of someone explaining a task with their hands, such as a recipe or a simple repair. Cover the screen, listen to the first minute and write down the steps. Then watch the same minute with the picture on and correct your list. Which steps did the speaker's hands make clearer?

With a partner, try a drawing exercise. Your partner describes a simple picture you can't see while you draw it. Do it once with your partner out of sight and once facing you, using a different picture each time. Compare how close each drawing came to the original.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I turn my camera on for calls with English learners?

When you can, yes. In the studies above, learners understood more when they could see the speaker than with audio alone. Keep your hands in the frame when you explain a process, a direction or a size.

### Do gestures help people listening in their first language?

Somewhat. A meta-analysis found a moderate benefit across listeners. In one study of seventh and eighth graders, though, gestures made little difference for students listening in their first language and a clear difference for foreign-language learners.

### Can gestures make a message harder to follow?

Yes, when they don't match the words. In one experiment, listeners were slower and made more errors when a gesture conflicted with the spoken word.
