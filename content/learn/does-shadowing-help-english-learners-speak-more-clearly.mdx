It may, but the evidence is limited. Shadowing means repeating a recording aloud while you listen to it, a moment behind the speaker. A 2025 review of 44 studies found that most studies measuring how easy learners were to understand, or how fluent they sounded, reported gains after shadowing, while results for individual sounds were inconclusive. One study with a comparison group found gains in unscripted speech after two weeks. Still, most studies were short, fewer than half compared shadowing learners with a control or comparison group, and most tested reading aloud or repeating a recording rather than conversation. For listening, small studies found gains in catching words, and in one study comprehension improved only among lower-proficiency learners.

## What is shadowing?

Shadowing began as a training exercise for simultaneous interpreters. In 1992 it was brought into English teaching in Japan as a way to practice listening. The learner hears a recording without a written script and repeats it as close to the speaker as possible. In listen-and-repeat practice, by contrast, the learner waits and repeats one chunk or sentence at a time. Because shadowing leaves almost no gap between hearing and speaking, researchers argue that it keeps learners' attention on the sounds they hear rather than on meaning. One variation has learners shadow while reading a script <Cite k="hamada2019shadowing" />. A later review notes that 16 variations have been documented, all built on speaking at nearly the same time as listening <Cite k="whitworth2025shadowing" />.

## Does shadowing help learners hear English better?

Most research on shadowing has been about listening, and most of it came from Japan <Cite k="hamada2019shadowing" />. In one study, 43 Japanese university students shadowed during nine lessons and were split into lower- and intermediate-proficiency groups by a listening pretest. Both groups improved on a 20-item dictation test, which the author used to measure how well learners perceive sounds. Only the lower-proficiency group improved on high-school-level listening comprehension questions <Cite k="hamada2016shadowing" />.

A later study by the same author used a 112-item test of basic listening skills with 36 university students who shadowed during eight lessons over a month. Their scores rose on recognizing words and on picking out the most prominent words in speech, but not on telling similar sounds apart. A revised procedure that added attention to the learner's own speech, corrective feedback and explicit instruction was then used with 12 students for three months, and their scores on telling sounds apart improved <Cite k="hamada2020procedure" />. In both studies every participant shadowed, so the results show change over time rather than an advantage over other listening practice. Some of the gain could come from regular classes or from taking a test a second time.

## Does shadowing make speech easier to understand?

The largest review we found included 44 studies. The authors set aside 8 that had learners repeat isolated words after a delay rather than shadow, and 2 that were earlier versions of other studies, leaving 34 that they described. These were published between 2008 and 2023, and 27 involved learners of English. Most studies that measured how easy listeners found learners' speech, how much of it listeners understood, or how accented it sounded reported gains after shadowing. All 8 studies that measured fluency reported improvements. Results for individual sounds were mixed, and the authors called that evidence inconclusive <Cite k="whitworth2025shadowing" />.

Of 31 studies that tested pronunciation, 20 used only controlled tasks, such as reading aloud or repeating a recording. Only one retested learners after a delay, so it is unknown how long the gains last. Fifteen studies had a control or comparison group <Cite k="whitworth2025shadowing" />. Reviews of pronunciation teaching in general have pooled dozens of studies into a single estimate, as our guide to [whether pronunciation teaching helps adults be understood](/learn/does-pronunciation-teaching-help-adults-be-understood) describes. The shadowing review instead summarized results by outcome in a narrative synthesis, without combining them into one estimate, partly because much of the evidence mixed test scores with surveys and interviews <Cite k="whitworth2025shadowing" />.

## What did the most careful studies find?

The review rated two studies especially highly <Cite k="whitworth2025shadowing" />. In the first, 47 third-year high school students in China joined as an extracurricular activity. Thirty-seven shadowed for 12 half-hour sessions over two weeks, using tablets to watch short videos with scripts and record themselves sentence by sentence. Unlike standard shadowing, they could read along and re-record each sentence. The other 10 spent a similar amount of time on vocabulary, grammar and writing, with no speaking practice. Five advanced second-language users of English rated the recordings on 9-point scales <Cite k="shao2023choral" />.

When students told a picture story without a script, the shadowing group's average rating for how easy they were to understand rose from 3.75 to 4.58. By our calculation, that is a gain of 0.83 points on the 9-point scale. The comparison group moved from 3.78 to 3.96, a change that was not statistically significant. The study did not directly test whether the two groups' gains differed, and with only 10 students, the comparison group's change is hard to judge. The shadowing group was also rated somewhat less accented. The comparison group was small, and the authors call for a larger study with equal groups. The read-aloud sentences on the tests came from the training videos, which the authors note may have helped the shadowing group <Cite k="shao2023choral" />.

In the second study, 16 learners shadowed short dialogues on mobile devices at least four times a week, for at least 10 minutes each time, over eight weeks. Twenty-two listeners rated an unscripted speaking task. The learners were rated easier to understand and more fluent, but not less accented <Cite k="foote2017shadowing" />. All 16 shadowed, so the study shows change over time rather than an advantage over other practice. Comprehensibility, meaning how easy listeners find speech to follow, can improve while accent stays the same, as our guide to [accent and intelligibility](/learn/accent-vs-intelligibility) explains.

Not every study found gains. Hamada describes a month-long comparison in which 29 intermediate university students shadowed and 29 practiced listen-and-repeat. Six native English speakers rated read sentences before and after and found little improvement in either group, in how easy they were to understand or in accent. He notes that the learners in the eight-week study above were highly proficient and suggests that shadowing for pronunciation requires fairly high proficiency <Cite k="hamada2019shadowing" />.

## Why is the evidence still thin?

Most listening studies of shadowing lasted about a month, and the best amount of practice has not been identified. Hamada also notes that speaking research on shadowing often lacks a robust design and relies heavily on students' own reports <Cite k="hamada2019shadowing" />. In the 2025 review, 10 of the 34 studies came from Japan, and the review included only papers written in English, which left out research published in Japanese. Learners in many studies said they enjoyed shadowing and found it useful, but most of that evidence came from questionnaires, probably collected in many cases by their own teachers, which may make views look more positive than they are <Cite k="whitworth2025shadowing" />.

## How to try shadowing

The routine below combines details from the studies above. The studies did not test this exact routine.

Pick a recording of 30 seconds to a minute in which you know most of the words, ideally a video where you can see the speaker's face. In a brain-imaging study of 42 Japanese learners of English, participants correctly repeated about 2 percentage points more of the selected content words when the speaker's face was visible than when it was hidden behind a mosaic (91% versus 88%, rounded). That was measured during the experiment, not after a period of training, and the authors did not test long-term retention <Cite k="jeong2026face" />.

Listen with a transcript until you understand the content. In one study Hamada describes, students who shadowed after studying a passage's content improved on a listening test, while those who shadowed before studying it did not. Then shadow without the transcript several times and record your last attempt. Compare the recording with the transcript and mark the words you dropped or blurred. In another study he describes, students who recorded their shadowing and checked it against the script reproduced the recording more accurately than students who checked with a partner or by recalling their attempt <Cite k="hamada2019shadowing" />.

Every week or two, describe a picture or answer an everyday question without notes, and record it. Ask a listener how easy it was to follow. Most shadowing studies tested read or repeated speech, so this check shows whether the practice carries over to unscripted talk.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is shadowing the same as listen-and-repeat?

No. In shadowing you speak along with the recording, a moment behind it, while still listening. In listen-and-repeat you wait and repeat a chunk at a time. In one month-long comparison on read sentences, neither group improved much in how easy they were to understand or in accent.

### How long does shadowing take to help?

No one knows the best amount. Most listening studies lasted about a month. In the Chinese high school study, 12 half-hour sessions over two weeks were followed by gains, and in another study learners practiced at least four times a week for eight weeks.

### Should beginners try shadowing?

They can start with listening. Hamada recommends that beginners use shadowing to practice listening and move to shadowing for speaking at upper-intermediate or advanced levels. In one study of university students, only those in the lower-proficiency group, set by a listening pretest, improved in comprehension, though people who score lowest on a first test tend to score higher the second time.
