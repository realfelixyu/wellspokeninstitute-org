Yes for fluency on the repeated task, with less certainty beyond it. In controlled studies, English learners who told the same story or gave the same talk again spoke faster and paused less, with most of the gain coming in the first few repetitions. Effects on accuracy and grammatical complexity were mixed: some studies found small gains, and others found none, especially when learners repeated under time pressure and reused the same phrases. Whether fluency carries over to new topics also varies. One small experiment found gains on some measures a month later, while a larger classroom study found that differences between repetition schedules had faded within a week. Each study we reviewed had fewer than 80 learners.

## What counts as task repetition?

In language teaching, a task is a speaking activity with a real goal, such as explaining how to solve a puzzle, telling a story from pictures or giving an opinion. Task repetition means doing the same task again, sometimes immediately, sometimes days or weeks later. Researchers distinguish repeating exactly the same task from repeating the same type of task with different content, and some designs change the listener each time <Cite k="lambert2017taskrepetition" />. One common classroom version is the 4/3/2 activity, in which a learner gives the same talk three times, in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes <Cite k="dejong2011fluency" />.

Studies usually measure three dimensions of speech: complexity, accuracy and fluency <Cite k="bui2019spacing" />. Fluency covers speed and smoothness, accuracy covers errors, and complexity covers how varied and elaborate the grammar and vocabulary are.

## How much faster do learners get?

In one study, 32 Japanese university students at three proficiency levels each performed three monologue tasks six times, with a different partner as listener each time, all within about 90 minutes. Speech rate rose significantly through the fifth performance. The largest gains came over the first three, and there was no further gain from the fifth to the sixth. The overall effect did not differ significantly by proficiency level or task type <Cite k="lambert2017taskrepetition" />.

Different parts of fluency changed at different times. Filled pauses such as "er" and "uhm" at the ends of clauses decreased only between the first and second performances. Filled pauses in the middle of clauses decreased gradually through about the fourth. Self-corrections decreased only in the fifth and sixth performances. The authors suggest that early repetitions help learners plan what to say, and later ones free attention for finding words and monitoring their speech <Cite k="lambert2017taskrepetition" />.

On the picture-story task, the group's average speech rate rose from 1.66 syllables per second on the first telling to 2.29 on the sixth. By our calculation, that is an increase of 0.63 syllables per second. The time allowed was cut from 120 to 90 seconds after the second telling, though the authors report that most learners finished before time ran out. The study measured only filled pauses, because classroom noise made silent pauses impossible to analyze, and it could not separate the effect of repetition from that of hearing partners perform the same task. The authors say the results apply to short-term gains within a single session <Cite k="lambert2017taskrepetition" />.

## Do the gains last and carry over to new topics?

The evidence is mixed. In an experiment at a U.S. English language institute, students in two class sections were randomly assigned to do the 4/3/2 activity either on one topic repeated three times or on three different topics, three times over two weeks. A third section did the repeated version later in the semester. Fluency improved in both conditions during training. On two-minute speeches about new topics, about one and four weeks after training, only students who repeated kept gains on some measures, such as shorter pauses, though the repeating group had just 10 students <Cite k="dejong2011fluency" />. The study is small: 47 students enrolled, but because of absences only 24 completed every session, leaving groups of 10, 9 and 5.

A larger classroom study in Japan compared three schedules for telling the same cartoon story six times: all in a row in one class, three times at the start and three at the end of a class, or three times in each of two classes a week apart. A fourth class served as a control. On an immediate test with a new cartoon, the group that repeated all six times in a row paused least, but it also spoke more slowly than the other three groups, and the authors report that its students repeated words more often as they spoke, a sign of disfluency. On a test with another new cartoon one week later, the authors found no meaningful differences among the groups <Cite k="suzuki2022massed" />. The study included 79 students in four intact classes rather than randomly assigned groups, and the control group had only 15 students.

## Does repetition improve accuracy and complexity?

Less consistently. In a study of 20 Vietnamese learners who gave the same talk three times, those whose time shrank each round improved most in fluency but showed no significant change in complexity or accuracy. Those who had the same amount of time each round improved less in fluency but made modest gains in both. Learners, especially those under time pressure, reused large amounts of their earlier wording word for word <Cite k="thai2016monologue" />. An earlier study with 10 adult learners found the same pattern of verbatim reuse and concluded that shrinking time limits may undercut the chance of gains in accuracy <Cite k="boers2014reappraisal" />.

A study of 60 Iranian intermediate learners compared four conditions that crossed task repetition, or no repetition, with careful or pressured planning during speech. Learners who could take their time while speaking and also repeated the task improved significantly in accuracy, complexity and fluency <Cite k="ahmadian2011planning" />. We read only the summary of this study, not the full paper.

## Does the gap between repetitions matter?

In a study in Hong Kong, 71 college students were randomly assigned to repeat a picture description task either immediately or after one day, three days, one week or two weeks, with 14 or 15 students per group. They were not told the task would come back. Across groups, repetition led learners to use more subordinate clauses and longer units of speech, but it did not change the variety of their vocabulary or the share of their clauses that were error-free. The number of errors fell slightly. Speech rate rose most with immediate repetition. The authors caution that groups this small have limited power to detect differences, so some negative results may be misses <Cite k="bui2019spacing" />.

Together with the cartoon study above, this suggests that repeating right away gives the quickest boost in speed, while spacing repetitions may change other aspects of speech. Neither study tested repetition over months.

## How to practice with repetition

The routine below draws on the studies above. None of them tested this exact routine.

Pick a short story you can tell from pictures, or a personal story such as how you chose your job. Tell it to a listener or into a recorder three or four times in one sitting, since the studies found most of the speed gain in the first few repetitions. Keep the same amount of time for each telling rather than shrinking it, and between tellings note one or two words or sentences you would like to say better, so you are not only repeating yourself word for word. A week later, tell a different story of the same kind and listen for whether it comes out more smoothly. If you also want to work on pronunciation, our guide to [shadowing](/learn/does-shadowing-help-english-learners-speak-more-clearly) covers a different kind of repetition practice.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many times should I repeat a speaking task?

In one study, speech rate kept rising through the fifth performance, with the biggest gains over the first three. Many participants felt that three or four repetitions were enough, though some found all six useful.

### Is it better to repeat a task right away or later?

It depends on the goal. In one study, immediate repetition raised speech rate the most. In a study of six repetitions, doing them all in a row reduced pauses most but slowed speech, and a week later the schedules no longer differed on a new story.

### Will repeating a task make my English more accurate?

Not reliably. Some studies found small gains in accuracy or grammatical complexity, especially without time pressure. Others found none, partly because learners reused the same wording each time.
