Yes. If you know an English word on paper but keep missing it when someone says it, try watching a short clip with English captions. Look for the word you missed, then play that line again and listen for it. To see whether the practice carried over, try a fresh line from the same speaker with the captions hidden.

One experiment tested this idea with Dutch listeners who knew English. They watched a video in an unfamiliar Scottish or Australian accent. Listeners who saw English captions went on to repeat more words correctly in previously unheard phrases than listeners who had watched without subtitles. The test measured what they could recognize immediately afterward <Cite k="mitterer2009subtitles" />.

## Which subtitles should I choose?

Choose the language according to what you want from this viewing. In an experiment with 60 intermediate learners of English in Spain, participants watched an episode of a British drama. Viewers who saw Spanish subtitles did best on questions about the plot. Viewers who saw English captions gained the most on a listening test using dialogue from a different episode, spoken by characters they hadn't heard in the first one <Cite k="birules2016films" />.

The test asked listeners to fill in 24 missing words. The English-caption group improved by an average of 4.05 answers from its first test to its second. The group watching without text improved by 1.70. In the Institute’s calculation, the English-caption group gained **2.35 more correct answers on average** than the group that watched without text. That figure describes one episode and one immediate listening test <Cite k="birules2016films" />.

You can keep captions on when your goal is to follow a story. In a separate study, 250 university students with a wide range of English proficiency watched nine TV episodes. Across the group, having captions improved the odds of answering comprehension questions correctly. The researchers could no longer detect a significant added benefit among students at the highest proficiency level <Cite k="pujadas2024captions" />.

## Try it with one missed detail

Imagine you hear a colleague say, “The shipment came in on Thursday.” You catch the day, but the words before it run together. With the caption visible, listen for where *came in* falls between *shipment* and *on Thursday*. If the caption paraphrases the speaker's words, pick another line where the wording matches.

Now try a new line from the clip. Before revealing the text, write down the time, place or action you heard. Compare your note with what the speaker said. “Come **in** before lunch” and “come **back** before lunch” could send someone to different places. Finding the missing word gives you something specific to replay. This exercise borrows a distinction from speech research: listeners can check the words they recovered separately from how easy a voice felt to understand <Cite k="munroderwing1995" />.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do translated subtitles make it harder to learn English?

Translated subtitles can help you follow the story. In the Dutch experiment, they also helped listeners repeat previously heard phrases. English captions worked better for recognizing words in new phrases from the accent the listeners had practiced <Cite k="mitterer2009subtitles" />.

### Should I always practice with the same voice?

Start with one voice if you're trying to follow a particular person. If your goal is to understand several people, include several voices in your practice. In one study, native English listeners who practiced with multiple speakers of Chinese-accented English could carry what they learned to a new speaker of that accent <Cite k="bradlow2008adaptation" />.

### What if I understand a clip only after reading the captions?

Save a sentence you haven't watched yet for the next check. Being able to repeat a sentence you just read and recovering the words in an unfamiliar one are different tasks. You can use [the Institute's guide to accent and intelligibility](/learn/accent-vs-intelligibility) to compare your impression of a voice with the words you actually heard.
