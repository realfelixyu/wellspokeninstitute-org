Probably somewhat, though the research tests a narrower kind of memory than giving a talk requires. In laboratory studies, people remember words and passages better when they read them aloud than when they read them silently, a pattern called the production effect. The benefit is clearest when people later have to recognize what they studied, and it has held for sentences, essays and a one-week delay. It is weaker or absent when people must recall material without prompts, which is closer to speaking from memory, and reading aloud did not reliably improve deeper understanding of passages. Studies of speech students link out-loud rehearsal to better speeches, but they only show a correlation.

## What is the production effect?

The production effect is the memory advantage for material said aloud over material read silently. In a set of eight recognition experiments, researchers found the advantage for words spoken aloud, for words only mouthed without sound and for made-up but pronounceable words. It was not explained by participants reading the silent words carelessly. In their first three experiments, the advantage appeared only when people read some words aloud and others silently in the same list. The authors argued that saying a word aloud makes its memory record distinctive, which helps people pick it out later from items they did not produce <Cite k="macleod2010production" />.

## Does it help if you say everything aloud?

That early finding suggested the benefit might depend on contrasting spoken items with silent ones. If so, rehearsing an entire talk aloud would not help. A 2013 meta-analysis tested this by pooling studies in which one group read every item aloud and another read every item silently. It found a moderate benefit for recognition memory in those comparisons, with a standardized difference of 0.37 <Cite k="fawcett2013production" />.

An experiment published the same year tested University of Calgary undergraduates in five groups of 48, including one that read everything aloud and one that read everything silently. Each person studied 50 nouns shown for 2 seconds each and then took a recognition test mixing those words with 50 new ones. Compared with the all-silent group, the all-aloud group recognized studied words at a similar rate (76% versus 72%). By our calculation, that is a difference of 4 percentage points. But the aloud group mistook new words for studied ones half as often (9% versus 18%). Overall, that group's ability to tell studied from new words was better <Cite k="bodner2014production" />.

The same study found a cost to mixing. When people read some words aloud and others silently in one list, their memory for the silent words suffered, and their overall accuracy across both kinds of words was nearly identical to the all-silent group's. When aloud and silent words were studied in separate blocks, the cost to silent words disappeared. The authors suggest that if the goal is overall accuracy, reading everything aloud may be the better strategy <Cite k="bodner2014production" />.

## Does it help you recall material, not just recognize it?

Giving a talk is closer to recall than recognition: no one hands you a list to choose from. An updated 2023 meta-analysis of studies comparing all-aloud and all-silent groups confirmed the recognition benefit but found no overall benefit for recalling the studied words. Within recall, the analysis found a benefit at late serial positions but not early ones. Reading aloud also reduced intrusions, meaning words people recalled that had never been on the list <Cite k="fawcett2023production" />. For a speaker, fewer intrusions might mean fewer wrong details slipping in, though no study tested that in talks.

## Does it work for longer material?

Most production studies have used word lists tested within minutes. One paper tested whether the benefit lasts and carries over to more complex material. In one experiment, the benefit was still present after a one-week delay. In another, it appeared for word pairs and for sentences. In a third, people read essays and later answered fill-in-the-blank questions, and they did better on questions about parts they had read aloud <Cite k="ozubko2012production" />.

A 2023 set of four experiments, with 47 to 151 participants each, had people read passages from reading comprehension tests, some aloud and some silently, and then answer multiple-choice questions. Reading aloud consistently improved answers to questions that asked about facts stated in the text. For questions that required deeper understanding, such as inferences or the main theme, a small benefit appeared in the first experiment but did not hold up in the next three <Cite k="roberts2023readaloud" />. Reading aloud reliably helped people retain stated facts but not understand passages more deeply.

## Is there evidence from people rehearsing actual talks?

Some, though it is correlational. A study of 119 public speaking students recorded their speeches and asked about their preparation. Researchers rated the speeches for content and delivery. Better speeches went with more total preparation time, more rehearsals in front of an audience, more time rehearsing out loud, more out-loud rehearsals and also more time rehearsing silently. When the researchers looked for the factors that best predicted speech quality together, the significant ones were grade point average, total preparation time, rehearsals in front of an audience and anxiety about the speech, not out-loud rehearsal on its own <Cite k="menzel1994preparation" />.

A later study had public speaking students keep journals of their preparation over a semester. Students who spent more time preparing overall, and more time on delivery and practice, earned higher speech grades <Cite k="pearson2006preparation" />. In both studies, students chose how to prepare and reported it themselves, so motivated or more skilled students may have both practiced more and spoken better. Neither study tested whether out-loud rehearsal helped them remember what they planned to say.

## Rehearse aloud, then check recall without notes

The studies above did not test this routine. It combines the memory research on speaking material aloud with the classroom studies linking practice to better speeches. Write your talk's main points and the exact wording of anything that must be precise, such as a number, a name or a quotation. Say the whole talk aloud from start to finish rather than switching between saying some parts and reading others silently. Then put your notes away, say the talk again from only a list of section headings and record it. Compare the recording with your notes and mark any point you skipped or detail you got wrong, then rehearse those sections aloud again. If you can, run one rehearsal in front of a listener. Rehearsing for an audience was one of four factors linked to better speeches in a correlational study. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is reading aloud better than reading silently for memorizing?

For recognizing words and facts later, studies consistently say yes. For recalling material without prompts, the benefit is weaker, and an updated meta-analysis found no overall effect on recall.

### Does mouthing the words count?

In early experiments, mouthing words without sound also improved later recognition compared with reading silently. Whether mouthing helps as much as full speech in rehearsing a talk has not been tested.

### Should I memorize my talk word for word?

The research does not answer that. Reading aloud helped people remember stated facts but not understand passages better, so it may suit details that must be exact more than the overall flow of an argument.
