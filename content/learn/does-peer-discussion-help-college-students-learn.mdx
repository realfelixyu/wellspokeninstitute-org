Yes, at least in the college science courses where it has been tested most. In a genetics course, students who discussed a question with classmates did better on a new, similar question they answered alone, apparently even when no one in their group had known the answer at first. College science and math classes that replace part of the lecture with group problem solving and other active learning have higher exam scores and fewer failing grades on average. In one experiment, students in active classes learned more but felt they had learned less. Discussion works best when a question splits the room and the instructor explains the answer afterward.

## Does discussion help, or do students just copy?

In many college classes, the instructor poses a conceptual question, students answer individually with a clicker or a card, discuss the question with their neighbors and then vote again. The share of correct answers usually rises. That could mean students understood more, or simply that they copied a classmate who already knew. To tell the two apart, researchers in an undergraduate genetics course followed the second vote with a new, similar question that students answered on their own. They concluded that discussion improved understanding even when none of the students in a discussion group had originally known the correct answer <Cite k="smith2009peer" />. A later review notes that this conclusion rests on a statistical analysis suggesting that some students' groups had no one who knew the answer. It also points out that gains after discussion could partly reflect extra time to think <Cite k="vickrey2015peer" />.

A physics method called Peer Instruction is built around this routine. Two physicists who taught with it reported ten years of data from introductory calculus-based and algebra-based physics courses for students who were not physics majors. After the courses adopted the method, students showed greater mastery of both conceptual reasoning and quantitative problem solving <Cite k="crouch2001peer" />. The data come from courses at the authors' university over time, compared with years taught traditionally, not from randomly assigned classes.

## The instructor's explanation still matters

A follow-up study in two genetics courses compared ways of handling a question. After answering individually, students in the majors' course discussed it with peers only, heard an explanation from the instructor only, or discussed it and then heard the explanation. The nonmajors' course compared only the explanation alone with the combination. In both courses, students did best on a second, similar question after the combination, and students at every ability level benefited most from it. The strongest students were not helped by the instructor's explanation on its own, which suggests that discussion matters even for students who already do well. In the course for nonmajors, the weakest students gained only slightly more from the combination than from the explanation alone <Cite k="smith2011combining" />.

## Active learning raises scores and lowers failure rates

A meta-analysis combined 225 studies comparing undergraduate science, technology, engineering and math courses taught by traditional lecturing with courses that used active learning. Exam and concept test scores were 0.47 standard deviations higher with active learning. In the 67 studies that reported failure rates, meaning the share of students who got a D or F or withdrew, the average was 21.8% with active learning and 33.8% with traditional lecturing. By our calculation, that is a gap of 12 percentage points. None of the studies with failure-rate data randomly assigned students to the two kinds of class <Cite k="freeman2014active" />. Active learning in this review was broad. It included vaguely defined group activities in class, worksheets, clickers, problem-based learning and studio classrooms, taking anywhere from 10% to 100% of class time. The result describes active teaching in general rather than peer discussion alone.

A later analysis asked who benefits most. It compared students from underrepresented racial and ethnic groups and low-income students with their classmates, using exam data from 15 studies with 9,238 students and failure data from 26 studies with 44,606 students. On average, active learning reduced the gaps by 33% for exam scores and by 45% for passing rates. The authors report that only classes using active learning for a high share of class time narrowed the gaps <Cite k="theobald2020gaps" />.

## Students may feel they learned less

In a randomized comparison in large introductory physics courses, 149 students were taught the same content with the same handouts in two ways. In the active version, they worked through problems in small groups while the instructor moved around the room asking questions, then the instructor gave the full solution. In the passive version, an experienced and highly rated instructor lectured. Students in the active classes scored 0.46 standard deviations higher on a test of the material but rated their feeling of learning 0.56 standard deviations lower. The authors warn that judging teaching by how much students feel they learned could favor lecturing. The experiment covered two class meetings, so it shows a short-term effect. In a separate physics course, the instructor gave a 20-minute presentation near the start of the semester about active learning and the evidence for it. At the end of the semester, 75% of students said it had helped them feel more favorably toward active learning <Cite k="deslauriers2019feeling" />.

## Running a discussion question well

The Peer Instruction study tracked how answers changed after discussion. The share of correct answers rose substantially when 35% to 70% of students were right at first, and the improvement was largest when about half were right <Cite k="crouch2001peer" />. A review of research on Peer Instruction recommends skipping straight to the explanation when more than 70% answer correctly. It also notes that instructors often change a method when they adopt it, which can unknowingly reduce its effectiveness <Cite k="vickrey2015peer" />.

Try one question in your next class. Choose a conceptual question you expect about half the room to get right. Ask students to commit to an answer on their own, then give them two or three minutes to explain their reasoning to a neighbor before voting again. Explain the answer, then ask a similar question that students answer individually, and compare the two results. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is Peer Instruction?

It is a teaching routine from college physics. The instructor poses a conceptual question, students answer individually, discuss their answers with classmates and answer again before the instructor explains.

### Does discussing problems help strong students too?

Yes. In one genetics study, the strongest students were not helped by an instructor's explanation alone but gained when discussion came first.

### Why do some students prefer lectures?

In a randomized comparison, students in active classes learned more but felt they had learned less. A clear, polished lecture can feel more productive than working through a problem with classmates.
