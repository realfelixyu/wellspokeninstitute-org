For most behavioral interview questions, the common advice is to answer in about 1 to 2 minutes. That is the range given by university career centers at Duke, Ohio State, and the University of Maryland, Baltimore County <Cite k="dukecareerstar,osucareerinterview,umbcstar" />. At a conversational pace, which averages close to 200 words per minute, that works out to a few hundred words <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />. It is worth being clear about the evidence, though. We could not find a peer-reviewed study that tests what answer length works best, so the 1 to 2 minute range reflects the experience of career advisers, not experimental results. What research does show is that structure and delivery affect how interviewers judge your answers.

At the 196-word-per-minute rate measured in recorded conversations, two minutes would contain 392 words. That rate counts words from both callers. Use your own rate to estimate the length of an interview answer. <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />.

## What career advisers recommend

The guidance is consistent across the career centers we checked. Duke's career center says a strong answer using the STAR method (situation, task, action, result) "will last one to two minutes" and advises being brief in the setup <Cite k="dukecareerstar" />. Ohio State's arts and sciences career office says most STAR responses should be about 1 to 2 minutes long and warns that students often spend too much time describing the situation <Cite k="osucareerinterview" />. UMBC's career center says answers can take 1 to 2 minutes and should be descriptive but concise, and it tells students to expect the interviewer to question and probe <Cite k="umbcstar" />.

These recommendations are for behavioral questions, the "tell me about a time when" kind. Short factual questions, such as when you can start, need only a sentence or two. A broad opener like "walk me through your background" can reasonably run a bit longer, as long as it stays organized.

## What the research does and does not tell us

Employment interview research is large, but it focuses on whether interviews predict job performance and how interviewers form judgments. It does not, as far as we found, test the effect of answer length directly. Here is what it does show.

Structure matters for accuracy. A meta-analysis of 245 validity coefficients from 86,311 people found that structured interviews, which use planned questions and consistent scoring, predict job performance better than unstructured conversations <Cite k="mcdaniel1994interviews" />. A later review of structured interview research identified probing, follow-up questions, and prompting as one of the main topics in the field <Cite k="levashina2014structured" />. In practice, that means many interviewers expect to ask follow-up questions. You do not need to include every detail in your first answer.

Self-presentation affects ratings, especially in unstructured interviews. A meta-analysis of how appearance, impression management, and verbal and nonverbal behavior relate to interview ratings concluded that "what you see in the interview may not be what you get on the job," and that unstructured interviews are especially affected by these tactics <Cite k="barrick2009selfpresentation" />. In one study, self-promotion, meaning describing your own accomplishments, was positively related to end-of-interview scores (r = .20), while tactics the authors call image creation were negatively related <Cite k="swider2011initial" />.

Delivery is part of the answer. In videotaped interviews with managers, a combined measure of vocal cues, including pitch variability, speech rate, pauses, and variation in loudness, correlated with interviewers' judgments (r = .20) and with supervisors' job performance ratings in two samples (r = .18 and r = .20) <Cite k="degroot1999vocal" />. The researchers found that these links ran through personal reactions such as liking, trust, and perceived credibility. These are modest correlations, but they suggest that how you sound while answering carries weight along with what you say.

## Why a time range still helps

Even without a tested optimum, a target range is useful because both extremes create problems that interviewers can see.

An answer much shorter than a minute often leaves out the part interviewers are scoring: what you did and what happened as a result. That forces the interviewer to drag the story out of you with follow-ups, which uses up the time they have for other questions.

An answer much longer than 2 minutes usually means too much setup. Ohio State's advice to keep the background brief and spend most of the answer on actions and results targets exactly this problem <Cite k="osucareerinterview" />. A long answer also leaves less room for the follow-up questions that structured interviews often include.

The range is not a hard rule. A complex technical question may need more time, and some interviewers will tell you how long they want. If they do, follow their instruction over any general guideline.

## How to build a 1 to 2 minute answer

A simple way to stay in range is to plan the proportions of a STAR answer before you plan the words:

- Situation and task: one or two sentences. Enough for the story to make sense.
- Action: most of the answer. What you specifically did, in the order you did it.
- Result: one to three sentences, with a number or concrete outcome if you have one.

Then say it out loud and time it. A stopwatch is more reliable than your sense of how long you talked. If you consistently run long, cut the setup first.

Finally, practice the delivery, not only the content. Because vocal cues such as rate and pausing relate to how interviewers judge candidates, it helps to rehearse at a steady pace with clear pauses between the parts of the story <Cite k="degroot1999vocal" />. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is a 3-minute interview answer too long?

For a typical behavioral question, it is longer than the 1 to 2 minutes most career centers recommend <Cite k="dukecareerstar,osucareerinterview" />. It may be fine for a complex technical question or a broad opener, especially if the interviewer asked for detail.

### How many words is a 2-minute answer?

At an average conversational rate of about 196 words per minute, 2 minutes is a little under 400 words <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />. Your own pace may differ, so time yourself rather than counting words.

### Do interviewers prefer short answers?

There is no research that settles this directly. Structured interviews often include follow-up questions, so a focused answer that leaves room for probing is usually safer than one that tries to cover everything <Cite k="levashina2014structured" />.
