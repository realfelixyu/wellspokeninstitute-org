There is no single correct speaking rate, but there are useful reference points. Everyday English conversation runs at close to 200 words per minute, and adults reading aloud average about 183 <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate,brysbaert2019reading" />. For presentations, recorded messages, and broadcasts, most writers on radio recommend 160 to 180 words per minute <Cite k="rodero2012speechrate" />. Slow down from there when your listeners are working in a second language or the content is new and dense. Speed up only when the material is familiar and your audience can keep pace.

## How fast people actually talk

The best data on natural speaking rates come from large collections of recorded speech. One research team analyzed large collections of recorded telephone conversations in English and Chinese. In the English Switchboard collection, speakers averaged 196 words per minute over the full length of each conversation, and individual conversations ranged from 111 to 291 words per minute <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />.

The difference between the fastest and slowest recorded conversations was 180 words per minute. Averages hide that spread. Each rate counts words from both callers over the elapsed conversation. <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />.

That average includes the silences between words and turns. When the researchers removed silences and noises and measured only the time spent producing words, the average rose to 236 words per minute <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />. So when you time yourself, remember that pauses count. A speaker who talks quickly but pauses often can still land at a comfortable overall rate.

The same study found that rate depends on context. People spoke more slowly and used longer turns when talking with strangers or discussing certain topics, older speakers tended to be slower, and men spoke slightly faster than women on average <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />.

Reading aloud is slower than conversation. A 2019 meta-analysis of reading rate found that adults reading English aloud averaged 183 words per minute across 77 studies and 5,965 participants <Cite k="brysbaert2019reading" />. Silent reading was faster, about 238 words per minute for nonfiction. The author noted that reading rates are in line with the maximum speed at which people can listen with understanding.

Situation matters as well. A study of British English speech found that the standard range of speech rates teachers had relied on, which came from radio news announcers, did not fit conversations, lectures, or interviews, and proposed a different range for those situations <Cite k="tauroza1990speechrates" />. In practice, a scripted news read, a lecture, and a chat with a colleague will not move at the same pace, and they should not.

## What broadcasters aim for

Radio is a useful benchmark because listeners cannot reread anything. A comparison of 40 news bulletins from public broadcasters in the United Kingdom, France, Italy, and Spain noted that most authors who write about radio recommend 160 to 180 words per minute. Only the BBC bulletins fell inside that range; the Italian and Spanish bulletins were faster and used fewer pauses <Cite k="rodero2012speechrate" />. The same study found that listeners' subjective assessments of the bulletins varied with the speech rate.

If you are presenting to a room or recording a voice message, 160 to 180 words per minute is a sensible default. It is a little slower than relaxed conversation, which gives listeners time to follow a planned message they have not heard before.

## When slower is better

Speed has a real cost for listeners who are working in a second language. Research on English learners has found that comprehension declines once speech passes a certain rate. One study reported that rates faster than 200 words per minute impaired comprehension for lower-intermediate learners <Cite k="griffiths1992speechrate" />.

A later study took a different approach and gave learners control of the playback speed. When students could adjust the rate themselves, their comprehension improved, and they improved it by slowing the speech down <Cite k="zhao1997ratecontrol" />. Listeners differ, and a single fixed pace will not suit everyone. In a live talk, you cannot give each listener a speed dial, so watch faces and slow down when attention drops.

Slow down for numbers, names, instructions, and anything your audience must act on. Those are the moments where a missed word costs the most.

## What faster speech does to persuasion

Faster speech is not always worse. In two field experiments in the 1970s, people who heard a faster speaker were more persuaded, and the researchers concluded that speech rate worked as a cue that made the speaker seem more credible <Cite k="miller1976speed" />.

Later work found a more complicated picture. In an experiment comparing a moderate rate of 180 words per minute with a fast rate of 220, fast speech made listeners less able to tell strong arguments from weak ones. Fast speech increased persuasion only for listeners with moderate personal involvement in the topic, and that effect worked through how credible the speaker seemed <Cite k="smithshaffer1995speed" />.

The practical lesson is that a brisk, confident pace can help a speaker seem competent, but it also makes it harder for listeners to judge the substance. If your argument is strong, give people enough time to notice.

## Different languages, similar information rates

If you work across languages, you may notice that some languages sound faster than others. That impression is accurate at the level of syllables, but a study of 17 languages found that faster languages tend to pack less information into each syllable. The result was that languages carried information at a similar rate, about 39 bits per second, despite large differences in syllable speed <Cite k="coupe2019information" />. A speaker of a "fast" language is not necessarily delivering more content per minute.

## How to measure and adjust your pace

Time yourself on a real passage rather than guessing. Read or speak a section of your talk for one minute, count the words, and compare the result with the reference points above. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good speaking rate for a presentation?

A common target is 160 to 180 words per minute, the range most writers on radio recommend for listeners hearing new information <Cite k="rodero2012speechrate" />. Go slower for audiences listening in a second language.

### Is 200 words per minute too fast?

For conversation it is typical, since recorded phone calls average about 196 words per minute <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />. For a presentation to people learning English, rates above 200 words per minute have been found to reduce comprehension <Cite k="griffiths1992speechrate" />.

### How many words is a 5-minute speech?

At 160 to 180 words per minute, a 5-minute speech is roughly 800 to 900 words. Pauses, audience reactions, and slides can lower that number, so time a rehearsal to check.
