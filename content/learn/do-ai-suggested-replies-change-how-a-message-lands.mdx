Yes. In a text chat between strangers, people rated a partner as more cooperative and felt closer to them when the partner used more suggested replies. Yet people who *thought* their partner relied heavily on suggestions gave them lower ratings. The guesses had only a weak relationship with how often the partner actually tapped a reply <Cite k="hohenstein2023smartreplies" />.

Imagine a teammate writes, “I've tried the new form twice and it keeps deleting my notes.” Your phone offers “Thanks for letting me know!” You might mean every word. Before sending it, consider what your teammate still needs to know. If you'll bring the problem up at the afternoon meeting, say so. If you're unsure whether they recovered their notes, ask.

## What could people tell in the chat?

Researchers paired online workers to discuss how their platform should handle work rejected unfairly. Everyone could type a message. By random assignment, some screens also offered suggested replies. Partners couldn't see each other's options. The researchers logged which replies people sent and used the random assignment to estimate how sending them affected the other person. They recruited 438 people and used 361 post-chat surveys to measure impressions <Cite k="hohenstein2023smartreplies" />.

People guessed how much help their partner had used *after* the discussion. A frustrating exchange could have made someone suspect the partner was tapping suggestions. The study also excluded six pairs who sent suggestions for more than three-quarters of their messages. It tells us little about a whole discussion conducted that way <Cite k="hohenstein2023smartreplies" />.

## What happens when a reply carries an AI label?

In one experiment, people described a personal difficulty and later read a written response. The researchers assigned the author and the label on the reply separately. Among people who received an AI-written response, those told it came from another person averaged 6.12 out of 7 for feeling heard. Those told it came from AI averaged 5.41. That **0.71-point difference** describes how much more heard the first group felt, even though AI wrote the replies for both groups. The experiment labeled the author of a whole response. It didn't test telling someone you used a tool to edit one sentence <Cite k="yin2024feelheard" />.

In an experiment with a scripted partner, participants trusted the partner more when chatting through a messaging app that offered suggested replies than through one that didn't. Other differences between the two apps could have contributed. In two later experiments, people read a message from a stranger asking them to send points worth money. Allowing writers to use predictive text made little difference to how many points recipients entrusted to them, even when recipients were told whether the writer had access to the tool <Cite k="hohenstein2020trust,purcell2025writingtrust" />.

## Try the reply aloud before a meeting

Hearing someone speak can itself change how an exchange feels. In a separate experiment, strangers who talked by voice reported a stronger connection than strangers who chatted by text. The researchers compared ways of having the conversation. They didn't test having AI choose the spoken words <Cite k="kumar2021voice" />.

Try the missing-notes example with a colleague. Have them read the complaint aloud as if they'd just raised it in a meeting. First answer with the suggested thank-you. Then try, “Did you get the notes back? I can show the team where the form failed this afternoon.” Ask what they expect you to do in each version. If they heard a promise you can't keep, change the words and say it again.
