A few filler words will not ruin a talk, and they may even help listeners in small ways. Experiments show that um and uh tell listeners a delay is coming, and that words after a hesitation can be easier to recognize and remember <Cite k="foxtree2001umuh,corley2007hesitations" />. The cost is mostly social. Listeners tend to read frequent ums as a sign of nerves or poor preparation, and in one experiment a talk with the pauses removed was rated best of all <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />. The goal is not zero fillers. It is fewer long hesitations, especially at moments where you want to sound sure.

## Why people say um and uh

Fillers are not random noise. In an analysis of several large collections of spontaneous speech, psychologists Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree argued that speakers use uh to announce a short delay and um to announce a longer one. Speakers monitor their own speech plans, notice an upcoming delay, and choose which filler to use and where to put it. In this view, uh and um are ordinary English words with a job to do <Cite k="clarkfoxtree2002uhum" />.

That job tends to come up when speaking is harder. A study of task-based conversations found higher disfluency rates when speakers were giving directions rather than following them and when they described abstract shapes rather than photographs, which points to planning difficulty as a cause <Cite k="bortfeld2001disfluency" />. Older speakers had only slightly higher rates than younger ones. Fillers such as uh were also distributed differently from repeated words and restarts, which suggests they play a role in coordinating with the listener as well as in planning <Cite k="bortfeld2001disfluency" />.

So when you hear yourself saying um, it usually means you are deciding what to say next. The useful question is why you needed to decide in that spot.

## What fillers do for listeners

Several experiments show that listeners pick up on fillers and use them.

In studies of English and Dutch speech, hearing an uh helped listeners recognize the word that came next. Hearing an um had neither a helpful nor a harmful effect on word recognition <Cite k="foxtree2001umuh" />.

Fillers also shape what listeners expect to hear. In an eye-tracking study, listeners heard spoken references to objects in a display, such as "the candle." When the article was spoken fluently, listeners looked toward an object that had already been mentioned. When it was spoken with a hesitation, as in "thee, uh, candle," they looked toward an object that had not been mentioned yet <Cite k="arnold2004disfluency" />. Listeners treat a hesitation as a hint that something new or hard to name is coming.

Hesitations can even help memory. In a brain-recording study, a hesitation before an unpredictable word reduced the brain-wave difference normally seen when a word is unexpected, and in a later memory test, words that had followed an "er" were more likely to be remembered <Cite k="corley2007hesitations" />.

None of this means more fillers are better. These studies looked at single, natural hesitations in short utterances. They show that a filler is information, not just noise.

## How fillers affect what listeners think of you

The more important question for most speakers is how fillers shape impressions. The best direct test is an older experiment by psychologist Nicholas Christenfeld.

He first asked people what ums mean. The average listener assumed that ums come from anxiety or lack of preparation, even though ums do not seem to be a product of either <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />.

He then took a recorded talk and edited it into versions: the original with its ums, a version where each um was replaced by silence, and a version where the ums were cut out entirely. Listeners rated the speaker. The version with no pauses at all was rated best. But the ums version was rated better than the version where the same gaps were left silent <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />.

Two other findings from that study are worth knowing. Listeners told to focus on the content did not notice how many ums there were, while listeners told to focus on style noticed most. And the ums affected ratings even when listeners could not say how many they had heard <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />.

Research on second-language speech points in a similar direction. When untrained listeners rated how fluent speakers of Dutch as a second language sounded, measures of pausing and speed best predicted their ratings, and repairs such as restarts and corrections mattered very little <Cite k="bosker2013fluent" />. Listeners judge fluency largely by the flow of speech, and long or frequent pauses interrupt that flow.

## Um and uh are different from "like" and "you know"

Not all filler words behave the same way. A study of everyday conversations, recorded by a device that sampled participants' speech over several days, found that the five common fillers fell into two groups. Um and uh behaved as filled pauses and were used at similar rates by men and women and across age groups. "I mean," "you know," and "like" behaved as discourse markers and were more common among women, younger people, and more conscientious people <Cite k="laserna2014filler" />.

That distinction is useful in practice. Um and uh mark a pause in planning. "Like" and "you know" do social work in conversation and are tied to age and personal style. Cutting them from casual speech is mostly a matter of taste. In a formal talk or interview, a heavy use of either kind can distract.

## When fillers are worth working on

Most speakers do not need to eliminate fillers. They are worth working on when:

- They cluster at the start of most sentences, which suggests you are planning each sentence from scratch.
- They appear at moments where you need to sound certain, such as a recommendation, a price, or the answer to a direct question.
- Listeners or reviewers have mentioned them.

Start by measuring. Record yourself answering a question or giving a short talk, transcribe it, and paste the transcript into our [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter), which reports your rate per 100 words. Some automatic transcription tools remove fillers, so check that yours keeps them.

Then reduce the need for long hesitations. Rehearse the opening and key transitions out loud so you are not planning them live. Practice short, deliberate pauses at the ends of sentences; the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) can help. Since Christenfeld found that silent gaps were judged more harshly than ums, the aim is shorter planning gaps, not swapping every um for an equally long silence <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do filler words make you sound less confident?

They can. In Christenfeld's experiments, listeners tended to read ums as signs of anxiety or lack of preparation, and a talk with its pauses removed was rated best <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />. An occasional um has little effect.

### Is it better to pause silently than to say um?

Not always. In the same study, replacing ums with silence of the same length made ratings worse, not better <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />. Short, deliberate pauses at natural breaks work better than long silences in the middle of a sentence.

### Why do I say um so much?

Fillers usually signal that you are planning what to say next, and they increase when a speaking task is harder <Cite k="clarkfoxtree2002uhum,bortfeld2001disfluency" />. Rehearsing your structure out loud usually reduces them more than trying to suppress the word itself.
