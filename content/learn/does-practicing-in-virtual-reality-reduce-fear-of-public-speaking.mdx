In the trials so far, yes, at least compared with waiting for treatment. People with social anxiety who faced feared situations in virtual reality, such as giving a talk to a virtual audience, ended treatment less anxious than people on a waiting list <Cite k="chesham2018vret" />. Whether virtual practice works as well as practice in front of real people is unsettled, and the meta-analyses that asked disagree <Cite k="chesham2018vret,wechsler2019vrinvivo" />. Most trials were small, measured mainly how anxious people said they felt, and tested therapist-guided treatment for people with diagnosed or high anxiety rather than casual practice at home.

## How much does virtual practice help?

A 2018 meta-analysis pooled six studies with 233 participants that compared virtual reality exposure therapy for social anxiety with a waiting list. The virtual reality groups improved more, with a pooled effect size of g = 0.82, a standardized difference where 0 means no difference. The authors found signs of publication bias, meaning studies with less favorable results may be missing from the record. Adjusting for that lowered the estimate to 0.71. Limiting the analysis to the four studies that randomly assigned their 161 participants gave g = 0.92 <Cite k="chesham2018vret" />.

One trial focused on public speaking. It enrolled 97 adults from the community who had social anxiety disorder and named public speaking as their main fear. Their average age was 39, and 62% were women. They were randomly assigned to eight sessions of virtual reality exposure therapy, eight sessions of exposure therapy in a group, or a waiting list. Compared with the waiting list, each treatment improved all but one measure. The virtual reality group did not improve significantly more than the waiting list on self-reported fear of negative evaluation, and the group therapy arm did not on the length of a standardized speech <Cite k="anderson2013vret" />.

## Is it as good as practicing in front of real people?

In that public speaking trial, the two treatments did not differ on any outcome at any time, or in how many people reached partial or full remission. The authors wrote that a larger sample was needed to test differences between the treatments <Cite k="anderson2013vret" />.

Meta-analyses that pooled such comparisons disagree. The 2018 meta-analysis compared virtual exposure with real-life or imagined exposure in seven studies with 340 participants and found essentially no difference (g = -0.01). Its authors cautioned that a nonsignificant difference does not by itself prove the treatments are equal <Cite k="chesham2018vret" />. A 2019 meta-analysis used stricter rules. It included only randomized trials that gave the same amount of exposure in both conditions, and it left out comparisons with imagined exposure. Across three social anxiety trials with 148 participants, real-life exposure did better (g = -0.50). For specific phobias and agoraphobia, the difference was not significant <Cite k="wechsler2019vrinvivo" />.

Recalculating the public speaking trial's self-report data, the 2019 authors found a large difference favoring the group condition, and they pointed to differences in how the trials were run. In that trial, people in the group condition also listened to other members' speeches and received positive feedback from the whole group on videos of their own speeches. The virtual reality group received feedback from the therapist only <Cite k="wechsler2019vrinvivo" />.

Single trials have pointed both ways. In a trial of 60 adults with social anxiety disorder in Amsterdam, exposure to real situations reduced social anxiety symptoms more than virtual exposure did, both at the end of treatment and at follow-up. Both treatments used exposure alone, without cognitive techniques <Cite k="kampmann2016vret" />. In a trial of 59 adults run by researchers in Quebec, cognitive behavioral therapy with virtual exposure was more effective than the same therapy with real-life exposure on the main outcome measure at the end of treatment. On a scale therapists completed after each exposure session, covering cost, time and planning, the virtual sessions were rated as more practical. Two of the authors own equity in a company that develops virtual environments, although that company did not create the ones used in the study <Cite k="bouchard2017vrinvivo" />.

## Do the gains last?

In the public speaking trial, participants still showed significant improvement from where they started on every self-report measure 12 months after treatment <Cite k="anderson2013vret" />. In the Quebec trial, improvements were maintained at six months <Cite k="bouchard2017vrinvivo" />. The Amsterdam trial found less staying power. Three months after virtual treatment, the only change from the start that remained significant was in perceived stress, while every improvement after real-life exposure remained significant <Cite k="kampmann2016vret" />.

A 2020 meta-analysis reported on 22 studies with 703 participants. Symptoms were lower than before treatment, with reported effect sizes of -0.86 after treatment, -1.14 at six months and -0.74 at 12 months. These compare people with their own starting point rather than with a group that received no treatment. It concluded that virtual exposure was similar to real-life exposure at the end of treatment but became inferior at later follow-ups <Cite k="horigome2020vret" />. In a published letter, other researchers, including three authors of the Amsterdam trial described above, challenged that analysis. They wrote that it counted two trials twice, so the 22 publications described 20 studies. They also argued that two included studies were not mainly virtual reality treatment and that six had fewer than 10 people receiving it. Their reanalysis kept 12 studies, six of them randomized, and found no significant difference between virtual and real-life exposure at the end of treatment or at follow-up. They cautioned that it rested on a limited number of randomized trials <Cite k="morina2021vretreanalysis" />. The 2020 authors replied that they should have described 22 publications reporting 20 studies, but that no data were counted twice in the analysis, and they defended their other choices <Cite k="horigome2021reply" />.

## What happens in a virtual reality session?

In the Amsterdam trial, treatment was ten 90-minute sessions held twice a week. By our calculation, that is 900 minutes, or 15 hours, of scheduled sessions, of which up to 7 hours were exposure. The first two sessions explained the treatment and ranked the virtual situations by how much anxiety each person expected, and participants spent up to 5 minutes in a neutral virtual world to get used to the equipment. Sessions 3 through 9 each held two 30-minute blocks of exposure. A therapist watched through a one-way mirror, talked with the participant over an intercom and controlled the virtual people, changing whether they were friendly or unfriendly, how many were present and how personal the conversation became. Scenarios included giving a talk followed by questions from the audience and attending a job interview. Participants rated their anxiety at the start, at the highest point and at the end of each exercise <Cite k="kampmann2016vret" />.

Other trials spent less time inside the headset. In the Quebec trial, exposure took about 20 to 30 minutes per session in the 7th through 14th weekly sessions. That limit was set to reduce the risk of cybersickness, the unwanted side effects that immersion in virtual reality can cause. In no session were scores on a simulator sickness questionnaire significantly higher after immersion than before <Cite k="bouchard2017vrinvivo" />. Across the nine trials in the 2019 meta-analysis, which also covered specific phobias and agoraphobia, people had between three and eight exposure sessions lasting 20 to 90 minutes each. In the public speaking trial, the virtual audience could be set to react with boredom, hostility or distraction and could ask standardized questions <Cite k="wechsler2019vrinvivo" />.

## Who has been studied, and who benefits?

Most trials enrolled adults with diagnosed social anxiety disorder or high public speaking anxiety. Participants in the Amsterdam trial averaged 36.9 years of age, and 63.3% were women. In that trial, 25% dropped out of virtual treatment and 15% out of real-life exposure, a difference that was not statistically significant <Cite k="kampmann2016vret" />. The 2020 meta-analysis also found no significant difference in dropout between virtual and real-life exposure <Cite k="horigome2020vret" />.

The trials say little about which people benefit most. The 2018 meta-analysis lists greater acceptability as a potential advantage of virtual exposure and suggests studies that compare how willing socially anxious people are to accept it versus other exposure treatments, since few ever seek treatment <Cite k="chesham2018vret" />. ## What are the limits of this evidence?

The studies are small. The nine trials in the 2019 meta-analysis averaged 41.22 participants each, and its authors called the small number of studies their main limitation. They could include only self-report measures because too few studies used comparable behavioral tests. They also noted that they and the authors of the original studies worked in the virtual reality field, and one of them holds a stake in a company that develops virtual environment research systems. The included trials were published between 2000 and 2016, and the authors saw no pattern of newer headsets making virtual exposure compare better with real-life exposure <Cite k="wechsler2019vrinvivo" />.

Results for how people actually spoke were mixed. In the Amsterdam trial, both treatments lengthened speeches compared with the waiting list. Independent judges who did not know each person's condition also rated videotaped speeches, and only real-life exposure improved those ratings compared with the waiting list, although the two treatments did not differ from each other on that measure. As one possible reason virtual treatment did less well, the authors noted that theirs was the first attempt to develop and apply diverse and complex virtual social interactions to a range of social fears <Cite k="kampmann2016vret" />.

## How can you use the same structure without a headset?

The studies above tested therapist-guided exposure for people with diagnosed or high anxiety. They did not test the exercise below. It borrows their structure: a ranked list of situations, repeated practice, anxiety ratings and a planned move toward real listeners.

Write down five speaking situations from least to most frightening, such as reading a paragraph aloud alone, giving a two-minute talk to one friend and answering questions from three people. Start with the easiest. Rate your anxiety from 0 to 10 at the start, at the hardest moment and at the end of each attempt. Repeat a step until it feels manageable, then move up one step. Each week, include at least one attempt in front of a real listener. If fear of speaking keeps you from work, school or relationships, a licensed mental health professional can offer the kind of exposure therapy tested in these trials.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is virtual reality as good as practicing in front of real people?

The evidence is mixed. One meta-analysis found essentially no difference. Another found an advantage for real-life exposure in social anxiety, and single trials have favored each approach.

### How long did virtual reality treatment take in the studies?

Usually several weeks. The social anxiety trials described here ran 8 to 14 sessions, with exposure taking only part of each session.

### Can virtual reality make people feel sick?

It can. Immersion sometimes causes cybersickness, so one trial limited exposure to about 20 to 30 minutes per session. In that trial, sickness scores were not significantly higher after immersion than before in any session.
