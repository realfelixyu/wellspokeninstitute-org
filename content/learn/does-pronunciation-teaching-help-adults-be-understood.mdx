It clearly helps learners produce the sounds and speech patterns they practice. Whether it makes their everyday speech easier for listeners to understand is less certain. After training, listeners tend to rate learners' speech as somewhat easier to follow, and that change is larger than any change in accent. But studies that compared taught learners with untaught ones have not clearly confirmed a benefit for listeners' overall judgments, and most tests used controlled tasks such as reading aloud rather than conversation. If you teach adult learners or are learning English yourself, aim practice at being understood and check progress in real conversation, not only in drills.

## What the large reviews found

A meta-analysis combined 86 study reports that tested pronunciation instruction in a second language. Taught groups outscored untaught groups by an average of d = 0.80. In plain terms, the average taught learner scored higher than about 79% of untaught learners on the study's test. The authors called the effect large, and a later review described the same figure as medium. Longer programs, programs that gave learners feedback, and more controlled tests produced larger effects <Cite k="lee2015pronunciation" />.

A second meta-analysis looked more closely at how gains were measured. It covered 77 studies published between 1982 and 2017, 47 of them also in the earlier review. Instruction was most effective when it targeted learners' careful production of specific sounds or of stress, rhythm and intonation. When listeners instead judged overall pronunciation, for example by rating how easy speech was to understand, the difference between taught and untaught groups did not reach statistical significance <Cite k="saitoplonsky2019" />. The benefit for everyday speech may be real, but the studies so far cannot show it clearly.

A narrative review of 75 studies found a mismatch between goals and research. Many researchers now argue that intelligibility and comprehensibility, meaning how much listeners understand and how easy they find it, should be the target. Yet most of the studies it surveyed aimed at native-like pronunciation. Reading aloud was the most common test, and very few studies measured spontaneous speech <Cite k="thomson2015narrative" />.

## Easier to follow and less accented are different goals

Listeners can rate a speaker as strongly accented and still understand every word. Our guide to [accent and intelligibility](/learn/accent-vs-intelligibility) explains why the two are separate judgments.

A 2021 meta-analysis of 17 training studies with 290 English learners looked at both judgments before and after instruction. From before to after training, listeners rated learners easier to understand (d = 0.61) and slightly less accented (d = 0.28). The change in comprehensibility was statistically larger, though the author classes both changes as small. Training on individual sounds and training on prosody, the stress, rhythm and intonation of speech, each improved comprehensibility without a clear change in accent. The author recommends that teachers of English learners prioritize prosody <Cite k="saito2021comprehensible" />. Only 5 of the 17 studies had a comparison group that received no instruction, so these are before-and-after changes, and part of them may come from simply taking the test a second time.

## Adults who have lived with English for years can still change

A workplace training program worked with second-language speakers who had lived in an English-speaking environment for an average of 19 years. After the training, tests showed significant improvement in how well they perceived English sounds, in how easy listeners found their speech to understand and in how much of it listeners understood. Their fluency did not change, and in one posttest listeners rated their accent as stronger <Cite k="derwing2014workplace" />. This was one program. It suggests that long-standing speech habits can still shift with training.

## Listening practice can carry over to speaking

Some training never asks learners to speak. In perception training, learners practice hearing the difference between second-language sounds that their first language may not separate. A meta-analytic review of 25 years of this research found that tightly controlled perception training led to medium-sized improvements in perception (d = 0.92) and smaller improvements in production (d = 0.54) <Cite k="sakai2018perception" />. The authors conclude that hearing and producing sounds are connected, although the link between the size of perception gains and production gains was not statistically significant.

## How much of the gain comes from the teaching?

Some of it may come from the test. The 77-study meta-analysis included 2,573 learners. By our calculation, that is an average of about 33 learners per study, small enough that any single study's result is uncertain. In 29 of the studies, 612 learners were in comparison groups that received no pronunciation teaching. Those learners also improved between the first and second test, by d = 0.31, which reflects practice with the test, time and other exposure. Patterns in the data also suggested that some small or negative results may be missing from the published record, so the authors say the overall estimate may be slightly inflated <Cite k="saitoplonsky2019" />. Taught learners still improved more than untaught ones on the specific features they practiced.

## Check practice against real conversation

Pick a question you are likely to be asked, such as how you got to work today or what you do in your job. Answer it out loud for one minute without notes and record yourself. Play the recording to a partner and ask them to write down your main points and to tell you which part was hardest to follow.

Choose one feature to work on, such as which syllable gets the stress in words you use often, or where your voice rises and falls at the end of a sentence. Practice it in short sentences for a few weeks. Then answer a new everyday question in the same way and compare your partner's notes. The studies above did not test this exercise. It checks whether practice on careful speech shows up when you talk without a script.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can adults still improve their pronunciation?

Yes, at least on the sounds and patterns they practice. Meta-analyses of second-language learners found clear gains on those features, and a workplace program improved the speech of people who had lived in an English-speaking environment for an average of 19 years.

### Should I try to lose my accent?

Researchers increasingly recommend being understood as the goal. In the 2021 meta-analysis, listeners' ratings of how easy speech was to understand changed more after training than their ratings of accent.

### How long does pronunciation training take to work?

There is no single answer. In one meta-analysis, longer programs and programs that gave learners feedback produced larger effects. A review of 75 studies found that programs focusing on a single feature were generally shorter than those addressing several.
