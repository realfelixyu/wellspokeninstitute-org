Yes, a spoken exchange with a chatbot can give English learners practice that carries over to a speaking test. Whether practice makes talking to another person feel easier is a separate question <Cite k="hou2025dialogue,ballidag2025anxiety" />.

## What improved on a speaking test?

In one ten-week classroom study, 13 learners practiced with a voice assistant and 15 were in the comparison group. The first group made larger gains on a speaking test. On a listening test, the groups’ gains did not differ significantly. It was a small, quasi-experimental study. If your goal is to follow a fast conversation, its speaking result gives you little guidance about how to practice listening <Cite k="dizon2020ipa" />.

Zhuohan Hou and Shangchao Min looked across 16 studies of dialogue systems and second-language speaking. They found a positive average effect. The fluency estimate favored dialogue practice. The grammatical-accuracy estimate was too uncertain to distinguish from zero, and the review found no statistically significant difference across the five speaking outcomes. The review included pre/post designs as well as comparisons with other groups. Most systems followed preset rules, so these studies cannot tell us how much a particular generative chatbot will help an individual learner <Cite k="hou2025dialogue" />.

By our calculation, the review’s 89 effect sizes across 16 studies amount to an average of 5.6 results per study. A group of students could contribute results for several outcomes, such as fluency and pronunciation. Counting all 89 as independent trials would give a misleading impression of how many groups have been studied <Cite k="hou2025dialogue" />.

## Will I feel more comfortable speaking to someone?

Researchers measure willingness to start a conversation separately from how well a learner speaks. One trial assigned 33 English learners to an AI practice group and 32 to a face-to-face group and measured both speaking skills and willingness to communicate. The chatbot group improved more on both measures than the students doing conventional peer speaking activities. A broader review of chatbot studies pooled outcomes across language skills. Its overall estimate includes learning other than speaking, so it cannot predict how readily someone will speak up in a meeting <Cite k="fathi2024speaking,lyu2024chatbots" />.

A study at a Turkish university examined anxiety directly. Among 22 students doing chatbot speaking tasks, there was no statistically significant overall change in anxiety. The students doing peer activities reported a decline. The two groups’ final anxiety scores did not differ significantly. Also, the peer group practiced in class and the chatbot group practiced outside class, so the setting differed along with the speaking partner <Cite k="ballidag2025anxiety" />.

## How could I practice asking for clarification?

Imagine you have agreed to meet someone at the library, but there are two entrances. Ask a voice chatbot which entrance it means. Then try the same situation with a classmate who chooses an entrance without telling you. Can you both point to the agreed meeting place? If your classmate has to guess, try a more specific follow-up question. This is a practice idea. The studies above did not test this exercise.

## Frequently asked questions

### What kind of chatbot did the studies test?

Most dialogue systems in the speaking review followed preset rules. The review offers limited guidance on what to expect from a newer generative system <Cite k="hou2025dialogue" />.

### Does speaking practice improve listening too?

In the ten-week voice-assistant study, learners made larger gains than their comparison group on the speaking test. The same comparison showed no significant difference in listening gains <Cite k="dizon2020ipa" />.

### If I’m willing to speak, does that mean I’m less anxious?

A learner can decide to speak while still feeling nervous. One trial asked about willingness to communicate and found an advantage for chatbot practice. Another measured anxiety and found no significant difference in the two groups’ final scores <Cite k="fathi2024speaking,ballidag2025anxiety" />.
