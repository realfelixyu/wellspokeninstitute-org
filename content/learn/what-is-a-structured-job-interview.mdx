In a structured job interview, candidates answer a common set of job-related questions, and interviewers use agreed criteria to score their answers. Some employers write examples of what an answer would have to demonstrate at each score. These examples are called *rating anchors* <Cite k="levashina2014structured,campion1988structured" />.

Suppose an interviewer asks, “Tell me about a time you handed unfinished work to a colleague.” You might remember the shift when an order went out late. Which detail would tell the interviewer how you handled the handoff? “We stayed on top of it” leaves your part unclear. “I called the evening supervisor, told her which order was still open, and asked her to read back the new delivery time” gives the interviewer something specific to judge.

## Listen for what the question asks you to do

“Tell me about a time” asks for an event that happened. Say what was yours to do and what you actually did. If a teammate made the decision and you relayed it, say so. “What would you do if your shift was ending and the order was still open?” gives you a hypothetical problem. You can explain which fact you would check first and what you would do if the evening supervisor was unavailable.

That change in wording can matter even when both questions assess the same skill. A review combined 29 comparisons covering 8,148 participants. The authors examined scores on past-experience and hypothetical questions written to assess matching job requirements. The average observed correlation was 0.40. Someone who scored well on one format tended to score well on the other, but the scores were far from interchangeable <Cite k="culbertson2017twoformats" />.

## What the scoring guide adds

Imagine the interviewer is listening for how you make ownership clear. One example on a practice scale might describe an answer that names the colleague who needs the update. Another might describe an answer that also explains how the speaker checks who will take over. These are examples for the handoff exercise. An employer could score a different aspect of the very same situation.

In one experiment, 112 people rated recordings of five scripted interviews for a residence-hall assistant job. Raters who used examples of behavior agreed more closely with one another and came closer to the researchers’ benchmark scores than those who marked a conventional rating scale. The researchers wrote the scripts using those behavioral examples, so they could check how closely listeners recovered the intended score. The experiment measured how people scored recordings <Cite k="vance1978interviewratings" />.

A study of entry-level production jobs could ask a different question. Three-person panels interviewed 243 applicants. Of those, 149 started the job and received a performance appraisal after six months, leaving 94 applicants outside that comparison. Among the hires, interview scores and later appraisals had an observed correlation of 0.34. The employer used several procedures together, including common questions and scoring examples, so the study cannot isolate the contribution of the scoring guide <Cite k="campion1988structured" />.

A larger review also found that interviewers’ ratings of different skills overlapped. Ratings intended to assess teamwork and other ways of supporting colleagues did predict corresponding behavior on the job to some degree. An interviewer can listen for evidence of a particular skill and still have an imperfect measure of it <Cite k="wingate2025interviewconstructs" />.

## Try the handoff twice

Ask a partner to read the past-experience question aloud. Use a real handoff from work, school or volunteering. After you finish, ask, “Who did you think took over, and which part of my answer told you that?” If your partner has to guess, try again using the detail you left out. If you never learned whether the other person received the message, say what you do know.

Then have your partner ask the hypothetical version and change one fact. Perhaps the replacement hasn’t arrived, or the customer has already called back. Explain how that affects your next step. The exercise gives you a way to check whether someone can follow your reasoning. The studies above did not test this drill or estimate a candidate’s chances of receiving an offer.
