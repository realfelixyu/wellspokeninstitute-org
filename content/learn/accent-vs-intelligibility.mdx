A strong accent and being hard to understand are not the same thing. In a well-known 1995 study, applied linguists Murray Munro and Tracey Derwing found that the speech of Mandarin speakers of English was usually highly intelligible to native listeners, even though many of the same speech samples were rated as heavily accented <Cite k="munroderwing1995" />. Their conclusion was that a strong foreign accent does not necessarily reduce how well listeners understand. For people speaking English as a second language, that shifts the practical goal from sounding native to being easy to follow.

## Three things listeners judge

Munro and Derwing's work rests on separating three ideas that are often blurred together.

Accentedness is how different someone's speech sounds from the listener's own variety. Listeners rate it on a scale from no accent to a very strong accent.

Comprehensibility is how easy or hard the speech feels to understand. This is also a rating, and it captures listener effort.

Intelligibility is how much of the message the listener actually gets. Researchers measure it directly, for example by asking listeners to write down exactly what they heard and counting the words they got right.

In the 1995 study, 18 native English listeners heard short extemporaneous speech samples from 10 Mandarin speakers and 2 native English speakers. They transcribed each sample and rated it for accent and comprehensibility on 9-point scales <Cite k="munroderwing1995" />.

## What the research found

Most utterances were highly intelligible and rated as easy to understand, but accent ratings varied widely, with a sizable share at the heavily accented end <Cite k="munroderwing1995" />. When the researchers looked at each listener's ratings, most listeners' accent scores tracked pronunciation and grammar errors. Fewer listeners showed a link between accent and comprehensibility, and fewer still showed a link between accent and intelligibility <Cite k="munroderwing1995" />.

A follow-up study tested whether the pattern held for other groups. Twenty-six native English listeners rated intermediate students whose first languages were Cantonese, Japanese, Polish, and Spanish. The relationships among accent, comprehensibility, and intelligibility were again only partly connected, regardless of the speakers' proficiency level <Cite k="derwingmunro1997accent" />. The study also found that listeners who were more familiar with an accent were better at identifying the speaker's first language.

The researchers describe the relationship as "quasi-independent." Accent, comprehensibility, and intelligibility tend to move together, but one can be strong while another is fine.

## Accent can cost listeners a little time

Understanding is not the only thing that matters. Effort matters too. In a separate experiment, Munro and Derwing asked 20 native English listeners to judge whether simple statements were true or false. Statements spoken by Mandarin speakers took longer to evaluate than the same kinds of statements from native speakers, and utterances rated low in comprehensibility took longer still <Cite k="munroderwing1995processing" />.

The key detail is that the strength of the accent itself was not related to processing time <Cite k="munroderwing1995processing" />. What slowed listeners down was speech they found hard to understand, not speech that simply sounded accented. That is another reason to focus practice on comprehensibility rather than on erasing an accent.

## Why adult accents tend to persist

Accent is closely tied to when a person started learning the language. In a study of 240 native Italian speakers who had lived in Canada for an average of 32 years, the age at which they began learning English explained an average of 59% of the variation in listeners' accent ratings. Very few who started after age 15 were rated within the native English range <Cite k="flege1995accent" />.

For adults, then, a goal of sounding exactly like a native speaker is unrealistic for most and unnecessary for nearly all. The research on intelligibility suggests that a noticeable accent is compatible with being clearly understood.

## The listener's role

Listeners are not neutral instruments. Research on what is called reverse linguistic stereotyping shows that when listeners believe a speaker belongs to a particular group, that belief can distort how they evaluate the speaker's speech <Cite k="kangrubin2009rls" />. In that line of work, listeners with more multicultural experience, such as more friends who are nonnative speakers, gave different evaluations.

Earlier research on university teaching assistants who were nonnative speakers of English examined how instructor ethnicity affected undergraduates' reactions even when the recorded language was completely standard. The same research found that a short coaching program did not change undergraduates' attitudes, and concluded that skills training for speakers needs to be paired with real intercultural preparation for listeners <Cite k="rubin1992nnsta" />.

The practical lesson is that some of the difficulty attributed to accents sits with listeners. Familiarity helps, as the 1997 study found for identifying accents <Cite k="derwingmunro1997accent" />. Exposure to a range of accents is one way workplaces and schools can improve communication from the listener's side.

## What to work on if you want to be easier to understand

If your goal is clearer communication, the evidence points toward the features that affect comprehensibility rather than toward accent reduction for its own sake.

A 12-week study compared three groups of English learners: one taught individual sounds, one taught general speaking habits and prosody (rhythm, stress, and intonation), and one with no pronunciation instruction. Both instructed groups improved on read sentences. Only the prosody group improved in comprehensibility and fluency when speaking spontaneously <Cite k="derwing1998broad" />.

In a later review, Derwing and Munro argued for a pronunciation framework in which mutual intelligibility is the main consideration, while still recognizing the social effects of accent <Cite k="derwingmunro2005accent" />. They also noted that pronunciation teaching has often been left to teachers' intuitions for lack of research.

In practice, that suggests focusing on:

- Pacing and pauses, so listeners can separate your ideas into chunks. The [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice) helps you rehearse pausing at natural breaks.
- Word and sentence stress, which shape the rhythm of English.
- Specific sounds that cause confusion between common words, rather than every difference from a native accent.

Recording yourself and asking a listener to write down what you said is a simple version of the intelligibility test researchers use.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a foreign accent make you less understandable?

Not necessarily. In Munro and Derwing's research, speech rated as heavily accented was often still highly intelligible, and accent strength was only partly related to how well listeners understood <Cite k="munroderwing1995" />.

### What is the difference between intelligibility and comprehensibility?

Intelligibility is how much a listener actually understands, usually measured by having them write down what they heard. Comprehensibility is how easy the listener feels the speech is to understand <Cite k="munroderwing1995" />.

### Can adults lose their accent?

Most adults keep some accent; in one large study, very few people who began learning English after age 15 were rated within the native range <Cite k="flege1995accent" />. Adults can still become easier to understand, especially by working on rhythm, stress, and intonation <Cite k="derwing1998broad" />.
