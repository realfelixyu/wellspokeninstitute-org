Sometimes, and usually by a small amount. Older studies, many of them real interviews held mostly by phone, found lower ratings for interviews held through technology than for face-to-face ones. Recent mock video experiments found gaps of a fraction of a point on a 5-point scale. A study of a real hiring process that switched to video during the COVID-19 pandemic found only small differences that were not statistically significant. The research base is small. Candidates also tend to rate live video and recorded one-way interviews less favorably, though in two recent experiments their fairness ratings after the interview did not differ significantly by format. For recorded one-way interviews, results on ratings are mixed. Most of these studies used mock interviews, often with university students, so treat the numbers as rough estimates for real hiring.

## How large is the gap in ratings?

A meta-analysis combined 12 studies, with 13 samples and 1,557 people, that compared face-to-face interviews with interviews held by telephone, video, computer or automated voice systems. Across 11 samples, interviewer ratings were lower in the technology-mediated interviews by a standardized difference of 0.41. In plain terms, the average rating sat about four-tenths of a standard deviation below the in-person average. The size of the gap varied widely from study to study. It was 0.59 in field studies and 0.22 in laboratory studies <Cite k="blacksmith2016technology" />.

The evidence on video in particular was thin. Only three of the rating comparisons involved video, covering 109 people, and the range of plausible values for that estimate included no difference at all. Most of the studies were published between 2001 and 2004, and the authors noted that the most recent one was 7 years old by the time of their review <Cite k="blacksmith2016technology" />.

## Do the gaps still appear with modern video calls?

The meta-analysis also found that more recent studies tended to show lower interviewer ratings <Cite k="blacksmith2016technology" />. Because video-calling technology has improved considerably since those studies, later experiments tested the question again with up-to-date software and found a similar pattern. In one, 88 final-year students and recent graduates at a Swiss university were randomly assigned to a face-to-face, telephone or video interview. The interviewer asked the same 11 questions in the same order with no follow-ups, and two raters scored the answers on a 1 to 5 scale. After adjusting for differences in prior interview experience, answers averaged 4.04 in person, 3.83 over video and 3.65 by phone. The telephone and video groups did not differ significantly from each other. Scores for a short self-introduction, which participants had four minutes to prepare, showed only small differences that were not significant. All data were collected before the COVID-19 pandemic <Cite k="melchers2021technology" />.

Another experiment randomly assigned 114 students at a German university to a face-to-face or a video interview with 13 questions and no follow-up questions. Interviewers' live ratings averaged 3.96 out of 5 in person and 3.78 over video. By our calculation, that is a gap of 0.18 points on the 5-point scale. When a second rater scored the same interviews from recordings, the gap was smaller and just missed statistical significance <Cite k="basch2021videoconference" />.

These experiments used mock interviews. A study inside a large European organization compared real applicants who were interviewed face to face before the COVID-19 pandemic with those interviewed by video afterward. It found only small mean differences in performance ratings that were not statistically significant, and no sign that the interviews predicted differently. The authors suggest the practical consequences of the format may be smaller than earlier research implied <Cite k="langer2024quasi" />. Because applicants were not randomly assigned and the switch coincided with the pandemic, this comparison has its own limits.

## Why might video ratings be lower?

The German study also asked candidates about their interview afterward. Those interviewed over video reported poorer eye contact (2.96 versus 3.97 on a 5-point scale), rated the conversation as less warm and personal (a measure of social presence) and reported less ability to present their skills in an attractive way. Interviewers also reported poorer eye contact with candidates on video. The researchers' statistical analysis linked part of the ratings gap to these differences, particularly to how well candidates felt they could present themselves <Cite k="basch2021videoconference" />. Because eye contact and self-presentation were measured rather than assigned, these links suggest explanations rather than prove them.

The Swiss study points away from one explanation. Self-reported strain, interview anxiety and a physiological measure of strain did not differ significantly across face-to-face, telephone and video interviews, and the authors concluded that strain or anxiety was unlikely to explain the lower ratings. Across all three formats combined, more anxious candidates did tend to receive lower ratings <Cite k="melchers2021technology" />. ## Are recorded one-way interviews rated differently?

In a one-way interview, candidates see questions on a screen and record their answers, which the employer reviews later. The evidence on how these answers are rated is mixed. In one experiment, 113 participants were randomly assigned to a live video interview or a recorded one. Those in the recorded interview received better ratings, but they found the format creepier and less personal and reported more privacy concerns. The two groups did not differ in how attractive they found the organization <Cite k="langer2017digital" />.

Another experiment randomly assigned 180 members of a nonprofit human resources organization in China to one of three groups, including a live video interview and a recorded interview scored by human raters. The sponsoring organization had agreed to refer candidates to employers based on their performance. Format made no significant difference to interview scores once raters' first impressions of the candidates and judgments of their appearance were taken into account. Raters' first impressions were linked to scores in both formats, more strongly in the live interviews. Candidates viewed the live format more favorably than the recorded one, but their fairness ratings did not differ <Cite k="suen2019synchrony" />.

## How do candidates feel about video interviews?

In the meta-analysis, candidates' reactions, such as perceived fairness and satisfaction with their own performance, were less favorable to technology-mediated interviews by a standardized difference of 0.36 across five samples <Cite k="blacksmith2016technology" />. One experiment suggests that some of this skepticism fades with experience. In the Swiss study, participants rated the expected fairness of each format on a 6-point scale before their interview, giving 4.92 to face-to-face interviews and 3.86 to video interviews. After the interview, fairness ratings in the face-to-face and video groups (5.26 and 4.99) no longer differed significantly, because ratings rose among people who had done a telephone or video interview <Cite k="melchers2021technology" />. In the German study, fairness ratings did not differ significantly between formats before or after the interview. Candidates saw the video interview as more flexible but reported more privacy concerns about it <Cite k="basch2021videoconference" />.

## What can candidates do?

The German researchers originally suggested that candidates in live video interviews look into the camera rather than at the screen to give the interviewer a sense of eye contact, though their study did not test that advice <Cite k="basch2021videoconference" />. Two of the same researchers later tested gaze directly in two experiments in which participants rated recorded interview answers. When a candidate's gaze was above or below the camera, as when a webcam sits on top of the screen, ratings of interview performance, warmth and competence were not lower. When the camera was off to the side, candidates were rated as less hirable. Deviations in eye contact also lowered the candidate's perceived social presence, which was linked to lower performance ratings. The researchers concluded that restrictions in eye contact are not an immediate driver of lower ratings <Cite k="basch2025eyecontact" />.

Recorded interviews sometimes let candidates prepare before answering or record an answer again. In a mock recorded interview with 175 participants, researchers varied whether preparation time was unlimited or limited and whether re-recording was allowed. Offering these options did not change outcomes by itself. Participants who used more re-recording attempts performed better and engaged in less deceptive impression management. Those who used more preparation time also performed better, though they engaged in slightly less honest impression management <Cite k="roulin2023preparation" />. Participants chose how much to use each option, so these are associations: people who take more time may differ in other ways.

## Rehearse one answer on camera

Put your camera directly above the screen rather than off to one side, and place the window with the other person close to it. Record yourself answering one question about a past experience, such as a time you caught a mistake before it reached a customer. Watch the recording once with the sound off and notice where your eyes go while you think. Then answer the same question on a live video call with a friend, and ask which part of your answer was easiest to follow. ## Frequently asked questions

### Are video interviews harder than in-person interviews?

Older studies, many of them real phone interviews, found lower ratings than face to face, and recent mock video experiments found smaller gaps. A recent study of a real hiring process found only small differences that were not statistically significant. In one experiment, candidates' strain and anxiety did not differ by format.

### Should I look at the camera or the screen?

It may matter less than once thought. In experiments with recorded answers, looking below a webcam that sits on top of the screen did not lower performance ratings. With a camera off to the side, candidates were rated as less hirable. Keeping the camera above the screen avoids that.

### Are recorded one-way interviews scored more harshly than live ones?

Not consistently. One experiment found higher ratings for recorded interviews than for live video interviews, and another found no significant difference once first impressions were accounted for. Candidates in both studies rated the recorded format less favorably on at least some measures.
