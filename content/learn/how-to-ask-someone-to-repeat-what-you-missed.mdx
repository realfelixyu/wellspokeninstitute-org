If you missed most of what someone said, “Could you say that again?” is enough. If you heard most of it, ask for the part you need. A friend says where to meet, but a passing bus drowns out the time. You can say, “I heard ‘outside the library.’ What time should I be there?”

Asking again is an ordinary part of conversation. In recordings of spoken and signed conversations across 12 languages, researchers counted 2,053 times when someone signaled that they had missed or misunderstood something in an earlier turn. Across the 48.5 hours they sampled, that works out to about 42 times an hour. That average counts everyone in the conversations <Cite k="dingemanse2015repair" />.

## What if you think you heard the answer?

Suppose you heard something like “a quarter to six.” You can check both the sound and the meaning by asking, “Did you say five forty-five?” If you heard correctly, your friend can simply say yes. If the meeting is actually at six fifteen, they can correct the time. When you have no guess, “What time did you say?” leaves the answer open. In the recordings, people used both kinds of questions. Measured against what the speaker originally said, replies were shortest on average when the listener offered a possible answer <Cite k="dingemanse2015repair" />.

Sometimes you heard every word and still lost track of the conversation. Paul Drew found that listeners on phone calls sometimes asked for a repeat just after the other person changed the subject unexpectedly <Cite k="drew1997openrepair" />. If your friend suddenly mentions meeting outside the station, you might ask, “Are we still talking about tonight?” Hearing “the station” a second time would leave that question open.

## What if they repeat the same words?

Say a neighbor offers to “give you a lift.” You hear *lift* clearly the second time, but you still don’t know what the neighbor is offering. “What does ‘give me a lift’ mean?” gives them a chance to explain that they can drive you. In one recorded conversation among English speakers, a listener asked several times what someone meant by an expression. When the speaker finally used a different word, the listener could answer the original question <Cite k="kendrick2015englishrepair" />.

People who use English with speakers of other first languages do this too. In 15 hours of recorded conversation, Jagdish Kaur documented people asking for clarification, checking a possible meaning and putting something into different words <Cite k="kaur2010mutualunderstanding" />. A separate study gave 16 learners of English directions for a task. Some heard directions simplified in advance. Others could talk with the person giving the directions. The group who could ask questions placed the pictures more accurately. Those exchanges included repetitions and explanations <Cite k="pica1987interaction" />.

## Practice making a plan

Ask a partner to write down a meeting place and a time, then tell you the plan without showing you the note. Write down what you heard. Leave a blank if you missed something. Ask for the missing detail. If you caught everything, pick one detail to confirm aloud. Then compare notes.

Switch roles. This time, pick a place with a name your partner might not know. If repeating the name doesn’t help, try spelling it or describing what’s next door. Can your partner tell you where to meet?
