Oracy is the ability to express yourself well in speech and to listen and respond to others. Advocates in England argue it deserves the same status as reading, writing, and arithmetic. England's 2025 national Curriculum and Assessment Review describes oracy as incorporating speaking, listening, and communication, including nonverbal forms such as sign language <Cite k="dfe2025curriculumreview" />. Oracy education usually has two parts: teaching students to talk well, for example to explain, discuss, and present, and using structured talk to help them learn other subjects. The evidence that classroom talk improves learning is promising, though most of it measures reading and reasoning rather than speaking skill itself.

## Where the word comes from

The term is not new. Andrew Wilkinson published a paper titled "The Concept of Oracy" in 1965 <Cite k="wilkinson1965oracy" />. In England, oracy has recently become part of national debates about what schools should teach.

In October 2024, the Commission on the Future of Oracy Education in England published its final report, *We Need to Talk*, calling for oracy to be recognized as a "fourth R" alongside reading, writing, and arithmetic <Cite k="rcslt2024oracy" />. The report also argued that a definition of oracy should include the other ways children communicate, so that pupils who do not use spoken language are not excluded <Cite k="rcslt2024oracy" />.

A year later, the independent Curriculum and Assessment Review for England recommended that the government introduce an oracy framework to support classroom practice and complement the existing frameworks for reading and writing <Cite k="dfe2025curriculumreview" />.

## What oracy education looks like

In practice, oracy teaching covers a wide range of classroom activity. The Education Endowment Foundation, which reviews education research for schools in England, groups much of it under "oral language interventions": approaches that emphasize spoken language and verbal interaction in the classroom <Cite k="eeforallanguage" />. The EEF's examples include:

- Reading aloud and discussing books with young children.
- Explicitly extending pupils' spoken vocabulary.
- Structured questioning to develop reading comprehension.
- Purposeful, curriculum-focused dialogue and interaction, with adults modeling and scaffolding high-quality speaking and listening.

These approaches use talk as a tool for learning, and many do not target speaking skill as their main outcome <Cite k="eeforallanguage" />. Formal presentations are only one part of what oracy teaching can include.

## What the evidence shows

The best summary for schools is the Education Endowment Foundation's Teaching and Learning Toolkit. It rates oral language interventions as "high impact for very low cost based on extensive evidence," with pupils making an average of 6 months' additional progress <Cite k="eeforallanguage" />. The review behind that rating includes 188 studies. Its key findings are worth knowing:

- The average effect is based mainly on reading comprehension scores, because that is the outcome the Toolkit uses when available.
- The evidence is strongest for interventions integrated with the existing curriculum.
- The higher the share of disadvantaged pupils in a study, the more effective the intervention tended to be.
- Most studies were in early years and primary schools; fewer tested secondary schools.

The EEF is also clear about what this evidence does not cover. Formal public speaking and presentational talk are not the focus of the research it reviews, and the impact on science and math achievement, tested in a small number of studies, is not clear <Cite k="eeforallanguage" />.

Three individual studies show the kind of evidence behind these summaries.

The first is about talking to reason. In an early experiment by Neil Mercer and colleagues, 60 British children aged 9 to 10 took part in a program that made them more aware of how they used language and taught "ground rules" for talking together. Compared with matched control classes, the children changed how they talked in groups, and their individual scores on a nonverbal reasoning test also improved <Cite k="mercer1999talk" />.

The second is a large randomized trial. A dialogic teaching program developed by Robin Alexander was tested in a randomized controlled trial funded by the Education Endowment Foundation, with nearly 5,000 Year 5 students (4th grade in the U.S.) and 208 teachers. After 20 weeks, students in the program were about 2 months ahead of their peers in English, mathematics, and science tests <Cite k="alexander2018dialogic" />. Video recordings showed clear changes in how both teachers and students talked. Alexander noted that the trial design itself explains little about why the program worked <Cite k="alexander2018dialogic" />.

The third looks at everyday classroom dialogue. A study that video-recorded lessons in 72 classrooms of 10- to 11-year-olds measured features of teacher-student dialogue and related them to student outcomes. When students participated widely, lessons where students elaborated on and questioned each other's contributions were associated with better mastery of the curriculum <Cite k="howe2019dialogue" />. This was an observational study, so the result is a correlation, even though the researchers accounted for prior attainment and other factors.

## Why oracy matters beyond school

Spoken communication is central to most adult work. In the O*NET® 31.0 Database, speaking is rated important or higher in 89.1% of the 910 occupations rated, according to the Wellspoken Institute's analysis <Cite k="onet31" />. The [speaking demand lookup](/tools/speaking-demand-lookup) shows how much a particular job depends on it. This does not prove that school oracy programs improve career outcomes, which has not been tested directly, but it explains why educators and employers have taken an interest in how speaking is taught.

## Open questions

The case for oracy education is strong on plausibility and promising on evidence, but there are gaps. Measuring spoken language reliably is difficult, as the EEF notes, since it involves both speaking and listening <Cite k="eeforallanguage" />. Most rigorous trials measure reading, reasoning, or subject knowledge rather than speaking skill itself. And there is less research in secondary schools than in primary schools.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does oracy mean?

Oracy means the ability to communicate effectively through speaking and listening. England's Curriculum and Assessment Review defines it as incorporating speaking, listening, and communication, including nonverbal forms <Cite k="dfe2025curriculumreview" />.

### Is oracy the same as public speaking?

No. Public speaking is one part of oracy, but most oracy teaching focuses on discussion, explanation, and learning through talk. The EEF notes that formal public speaking is not the focus of the research on oral language interventions <Cite k="eeforallanguage" />.

### Does oracy education improve results?

On average, oral language interventions are linked to about 6 months' additional progress, mostly in reading comprehension <Cite k="eeforallanguage" />. A large randomized trial of dialogic teaching found gains of about 2 months in English, mathematics, and science <Cite k="alexander2018dialogic" />.
