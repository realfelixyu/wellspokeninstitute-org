Public speaking can be learned, and the evidence comes from three directions. Randomized trials show that fear of public speaking drops substantially with structured treatment <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />. Studies of college speech courses find that students leave less anxious and more confident than they arrived <Cite k="broeckelmanpost2017hybrid,hunter2014speechcourse" />. And training programs for doctors and other professionals change how they actually communicate, as rated by observers <Cite k="barth2011oncology" />. The weakest part of the evidence is on measured speaking performance, which fewer studies test directly. Still, none of the studies we reviewed suggest that speaking ability is fixed.

## Fear of public speaking responds well to treatment

The most rigorous evidence concerns anxiety. A 2019 meta-analysis combined 30 randomized controlled trials with 1,355 participants. Across 62 interventions, psychological treatment reduced fear of public speaking with an average effect of g = 0.74, a moderate to large effect. At follow-up, the effect was larger still (g = 1.11) <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />.

Most of the trials tested cognitive or behavioral approaches. Treatments delivered online or with other technology were as effective as face-to-face treatment. The interventions also had a smaller effect on broader social anxiety (g = 0.35) <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />.

Two caveats matter. Effects measured by self-report were larger than effects on physiological and behavioral measures, so people's own sense of fear tends to change more than the outward signs of it. And the authors found some signs of publication bias, which means the true average may be somewhat smaller <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />.

A comprehensive review of the public speaking anxiety literature describes a large body of research on its causes and on techniques for reducing it <Cite k="bodie2010psa" />.

## Speech courses reduce anxiety and build confidence

Many universities require a speech or communication course, which has produced a steady stream of before-and-after studies.

At one university with a large, standardized public speaking course, students' scores on a standard measure of public speaking anxiety dropped significantly over the course, with an especially strong effect for women <Cite k="hunter2014speechcourse" />.

A study of 908 students compared a traditional public speaking course with a hybrid introductory communication skills course. Both reduced public speaking anxiety and raised students' ratings of their own communication competence by similar amounts <Cite k="broeckelmanpost2017hybrid" />.

Another study tracked students through a basic communication course and found that self-rated competence rose and communication apprehension fell over the semester. Students who started out most apprehensive and least confident changed the most <Cite k="rubin1997instruction" />. That is useful news for anyone who assumes they are too nervous to improve.

The content of the course may matter less than the practice it provides. A comparison of a voice and diction course with a general public speaking course found that students in both reported less apprehension and more confidence and competence afterward, and the size of the gains did not differ between the two <Cite k="hancock2010curriculum" />.

## Skills improve faster with good feedback

Feeling less anxious is not the same as speaking better, so it helps to look at studies that rate actual performance. A quasi-experiment with 144 first-year undergraduates compared feedback on presentations from teachers, from peers, and from the students themselves. Students' knowledge about presenting and their attitudes toward it improved whatever the feedback source. But teacher feedback did the most to improve their presentation behavior, and self-assessment alone had the weakest effect on behavior and attitude <Cite k="vanginkel2015feedback" />.

The practical takeaway is that practice works better with an outside listener. Watching your own recording helps, but a knowledgeable person who tells you what to change helps more.

## Professionals can learn to communicate differently

Some of the clearest evidence on behavior change comes from medicine, where researchers can observe and score real or simulated conversations.

A meta-analysis of 13 trials of communication skills training for cancer care staff found a moderate effect on observed communication behavior (effect size 0.54). Longer or more extensive training added a small further benefit. Effects on patient outcomes were small and not yet firmly established <Cite k="barth2011oncology" />.

Training can also reach the people on the other side of the conversation. A meta-analysis of 21 intervention studies found that when physicians received communication training, the odds that their patients followed treatment were 1.62 times higher than when physicians had no training <Cite k="zolnierek2009adherence" />.

These studies matter for the broader question because the trainees were working adults with established habits. Their communication still changed with structured practice and feedback.

## What the evidence does not show

The research has real gaps. Many course studies compare the same students before and after a semester without a control group, so some of the improvement may reflect maturity or other classes. Much of the evidence relies on self-reported anxiety and confidence, which, as the meta-analysis above shows, tends to move more than behavior <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />. And there are fewer rigorous studies of skill outcomes, such as whether audiences understand or are persuaded by trained speakers, than of anxiety outcomes.

None of these gaps point toward speaking ability being fixed. They point toward a need for better measurement.

## How to put this into practice

The studies above share a pattern: repeated speaking, gradually larger or higher-stakes audiences, and specific feedback. You can build the same structure on your own.

- Speak out loud, not just in your head, and record it.
- Measure one thing at a time, such as pace with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) or fillers with the [filler word counter](/tools/filler-word-counter).
- Get feedback from someone who knows what good speaking looks like, since outside feedback outperformed self-assessment in the study above <Cite k="vanginkel2015feedback" />.
- If anxiety is the main barrier, start small and build up. Our [public speaking anxiety self-check](/tools/public-speaking-anxiety-self-check) can help you notice where nerves show up. If fear is limiting your work or life, a licensed mental health professional can offer treatment of the kind tested in the trials above.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is public speaking a talent or a skill?

The evidence treats it as a skill. Anxiety drops with treatment, students gain confidence over a single course, and adult professionals change their observed communication after training <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops,hancock2010curriculum,barth2011oncology" />.

### How long does it take to get better at public speaking?

Most course studies measure change over one semester, and they find significant reductions in anxiety in that time <Cite k="hunter2014speechcourse,rubin1997instruction" />. Larger skill changes usually require continued practice with feedback.

### Does taking a public speaking class help with anxiety?

Yes, on average. Both traditional public speaking courses and broader communication courses reduced public speaking anxiety in a study of 908 students <Cite k="broeckelmanpost2017hybrid" />.
