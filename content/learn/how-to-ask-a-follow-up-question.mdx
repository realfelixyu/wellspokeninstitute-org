Pick something the speaker just said that made you curious. Suppose a classmate tells you, “I thought the interview was over, and then they took me out to the workshop.” You might ask, **“What happened when you got to the workshop?”** Your classmate can hear which part of the story caught your attention and choose what to tell you about it.

A useful test is whether you could have prepared the question before hearing the answer. “Where else have you applied?” would make sense at almost any point. Save it for later if you still want to hear about the workshop.

## Give the speaker room to correct you

Sometimes you caught the words and missed their meaning. If a coworker says, “The handover was better this time,” you could ask, “What went differently?” Asking “Did the new checklist help?” introduces your own explanation. That question makes sense if you need to evaluate the checklist. If you're trying to learn what happened, let your coworker tell you what changed.

You can also say what you think you heard. “So you knew who would take over the shift this time. Have I got that right?” A correction gives you something to work with. If the speaker says they'd rather leave it there, let them change the subject.

## How researchers measured feeling heard

In a study of 115 undergraduates, trained conversation partners tried different ways of responding. In one condition they restated what they heard and sometimes invited the speaker to elaborate. In another they acknowledged what the speaker said with brief words or nods. Afterward, participants rated how understood they felt. The average scores were 17.27 for active listening and 11.79 for brief acknowledgments, a difference of 5.48 points on a scale running from minus 32 to plus 32. The experiment tested a combination of responses, so it can't tell us how much any one question contributed <Cite k="weger2014active" />.

In a separate experiment, strangers got acquainted by instant message. People whose partners had been assigned to ask more questions liked those partners more on average. A subsequent journal correction addressed reporting errors and said the conclusions of the hypothesis tests stood. The experiment assigned the number of questions people should ask. It didn't assign particular follow-up wording <Cite k="huang2017asking,asking2025correction" />.

Spoken conversations give a more mixed picture. In another study, participants talked about personal prompts with trained study partners they had just met. When participants asked more follow-up questions, the study partners later recalled feeling more connected during the conversation. A separate small-talk exercise with an experimenter produced less consistent results for individual listening behaviors <Cite k="west2025listening" />. Researchers have also disagreed about what follow-up questions predicted in a speed-dating dataset. A decision to meet again can depend on plenty besides how well someone listened <Cite k="kluger2019dyadic,yeomans2019followup" />.

## Try it in a conversation

Ask a practice partner to tell you about the last time a plan changed. Listen for one detail you'd like them to explain. Ask about it, then give them the floor. Afterward, ask which words in your question helped them tell you the next part of the story. Swap roles and see which detail your partner picks up in yours. For a classroom version, the [college lesson](/curriculum#college) gives each speaker a short report and each listener a chance to ask what the speaker meant.
