Pausing helps listeners in two ways. It gives them time to process what they just heard, and it can make the words that follow more noticeable and memorable. In one experiment, listeners were more likely to recognize words they had heard right after a silent pause than the same words heard in fluent speech <Cite k="macgregor2010silence" />. Pauses also lower your overall speaking rate without making each word slower. The catch is length and frequency: long or frequent silent pauses make speech sound less fluent to listeners <Cite k="bosker2013fluent" />.

## Pauses are a normal part of speech

Silence is built into ordinary speaking. A study of about 6,000 pauses in 5.5 hours of read and spontaneous speech in five European languages found three clusters of pause length: brief pauses under 200 milliseconds, medium pauses of 200 milliseconds to 1 second, and long pauses over 1 second. The long pauses appeared only in spontaneous speech, not in reading <Cite k="campione2002pause" />.

Pauses also take up a meaningful share of speaking time. In a large collection of recorded U.S. phone conversations, speakers averaged 196 words per minute when measured over the whole conversation. With silences and noises removed, the rate rose to 236 words per minute <Cite k="yuan2006speakingrate" />. Much of what feels like a speaker's pace is the rhythm of their pauses.

## A pause can make the next words stick

The most direct evidence that pauses help listeners comes from brain-recording experiments by psychologists Martin Corley, Lucy MacGregor, and David Donaldson.

In the first, listeners heard sentences that ended in either a predictable or an unpredictable word, sometimes with an "er" just before it. Normally the brain shows a distinct response when a word is unexpected. After the hesitation, that difference shrank, and in a surprise memory test afterward, listeners were more likely to remember words that had followed the hesitation <Cite k="corley2007hesitations" />.

A follow-up asked whether a plain silent pause would do the same thing. It did. The brain's response to unexpected words was again reduced after a silence, and listeners were more likely to recognize words they had encountered after silent pauses <Cite k="macgregor2010silence" />. The researchers argued that what matters most from a listener's point of view is the time delay a pause adds to the speech signal.

These experiments used single sentences in a lab, so they do not tell you exactly how to pause in a 20-minute talk. They do show a mechanism: a short delay changes how listeners attend to and store what comes next.

## Pauses give listeners time to keep up

Processing speech takes effort, especially for listeners working in a second language or hearing unfamiliar material. When English learners in one study were given control over playback speed, their comprehension improved, and they improved it by slowing the speech down <Cite k="zhao1997ratecontrol" />. Pausing is the speaker's way of offering the same relief without dragging out every word.

Broadcasting offers a real-world comparison. In a study of 40 radio news bulletins from four European public broadcasters, the bulletins that ran faster than the commonly recommended 160 to 180 words per minute also used fewer pauses <Cite k="rodero2012speechrate" />. A speaker who wants to slow down can often get there by adding pauses at sentence boundaries.

## In conversation, gaps are short

Conversation runs on much tighter timing than a speech. A study of 10 languages, from major world languages to languages of small indigenous communities, found the same basic pattern in all of them: people avoid talking over each other and minimize the silence between turns. Average gaps between turns differed across languages, but only within about 250 milliseconds of the overall mean <Cite k="stivers2009turntaking" />.

Those gaps are short, on the order of 200 milliseconds, even though planning a spoken response takes more than 600 milliseconds. From that mismatch, researchers infer that listeners must predict when a turn will end and start preparing their reply while the speaker is still talking <Cite k="levinson2015timing" />. In a conversation or interview, your listener is already working while you talk, and a pause at the end of a point gives them a natural place to respond.

## Where pauses hurt

Pausing is not automatically good. Listeners judge how fluent a speaker is largely by pauses and speed. In a study where untrained listeners rated the fluency of people speaking Dutch as a second language, measures of pausing and speaking speed best predicted fluency ratings, while self-corrections and restarts contributed very little <Cite k="bosker2013fluent" />.

How a pause sounds to listeners also depends on context. In an older experiment that edited a recorded talk, a version where the speaker's ums were replaced by silence of the same length was rated less favorably than the original with ums, and a version with the pauses removed was rated best <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />. A silence that sounds like searching for words is not the same as a pause that marks the end of an idea.

The practical rule that follows from this research is about placement. Pause at the boundaries of ideas: after a sentence, before a key number, after a question to the audience, between sections. Avoid long gaps inside a phrase, where they signal that you lost your place.

## How to practice pausing

Pausing on purpose can feel unnatural at first. Some ways to build the habit:

- Mark pauses in your script with a slash at the end of each idea, and say the script aloud with those breaks.
- Use the [pause practice trainer](/tools/pause-practice), which reads your script back in phrases with timed gaps so you can rehearse the rhythm.
- Run your script through the [speakability checker](/tools/speakability-checker) to find long sentences that leave no natural place to pause.
- Time a rehearsal with the [speaking rate calculator](/tools/speaking-rate-calculator) before and after adding pauses to see how much they lower your overall rate.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a pause be in a speech?

Research has not settled on an ideal length. In natural speech, medium pauses run from about 200 milliseconds to 1 second, and pauses over 1 second appear mainly in spontaneous speaking <Cite k="campione2002pause" />. As a practical starting point, try a pause of about a second at the end of an important point and adjust after listening to a recording.

### Do pauses make you sound more confident?

In moderation. Long or frequent silent pauses make speech sound less fluent to listeners <Cite k="bosker2013fluent" />, so aim for brief, deliberate pauses after key points rather than long searches for words.

### Is it better to pause than to say um?

Not always. In one experiment, replacing ums with silence of the same length made listeners rate the speaker lower <Cite k="christenfeld1995um" />. A short pause at a natural break is a reasonable habit, but a long silent search for words is not an improvement over a brief filler.
