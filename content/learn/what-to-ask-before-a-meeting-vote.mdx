Before asking for votes, ask **“What do you know about these options that the rest of us may have missed?”** If the answer is silence, name the person closest to one part of the decision and ask about their experience. Give people a chance to hear the answer before anyone, including the chair, announces a preference.

Consider a team choosing between Tuesday and Friday for a stockroom move. Everybody has seen the quote showing Friday costs less. Only the evening supervisor has heard that the loading dock closes early on Fridays. A show of hands can tell you which day people want. Asking when the delivery crew can actually get inside might change the choice.

## Why an early vote can mislead

In a study by Garold Stasser and William Titus, students met to choose among hypothetical candidates for student body president. The full set of descriptions favored one candidate. Some groups received the complete picture. In others, details that favored that candidate were scattered across the people at the table. Together they had the evidence to find the candidate, even though none of them had read the whole case alone <Cite k="stasser1985unshared" />.

When everyone started with the full picture, 83% of 18 groups chose the candidate favored by the evidence. When clues were divided, 18% of the 38 groups that reached a decision chose that candidate. Subtracting the authors' rounded percentages gives a 65 percentage point gap. That figure describes how the information was distributed in this simulation. The researchers did not test the opening question suggested here <Cite k="stasser1985unshared" />.

A later set of experiments examined the timing of preferences. When participants learned what their peers favored before discussing the evidence, they paid less attention to the information exchanged and were less likely to identify the best-supported option. The researchers also observed the effect in face-to-face groups of three <Cite k="mojzisch2010preferences" />. If the chair already favors Friday, the team can still hear that view after the evening supervisor has described the dock hours.

## Ask the person who would know

“What have we missed?” can be hard to answer when nobody knows which details their colleagues already have. A narrower question can give the right person a way in. In the stockroom example, try asking, “You close up on Fridays. What usually happens when a delivery runs late?”

In a separate experiment, groups had to solve a fictional crime. Telling everyone which member held extra clues about each suspect helped groups mention more clues that only one person held. More groups found the correct suspect <Cite k="stasser1995expertroles" />. In a real meeting, the chair can do something similar by identifying who spoke to the supplier or who worked the last closing shift.

A direct invitation also tells a junior colleague why the chair wants their input. In a survey of staff on improvement teams in 23 neonatal intensive care units, people who described their leaders as more inclusive also reported feeling safer speaking up. The study concerned teams in a particular workplace and could not isolate the effect of a particular phrase <Cite k="nembhard2006inclusiveness" />.

## Turn the answer into a decision

Suppose the supervisor says, “Last Friday they locked the dock at four.” Check the scope of that observation. Was it a one-time closure or the usual policy? Someone who has the current schedule can answer. If nobody does, the chair can make the decision conditional. “Book Friday if Facilities confirms we can unload at five. If the dock closes at four, book Tuesday.” Give one person the job of confirming the hours before the deposit is due.

In an observational study of workplace meetings, teams whose discussions included more constructive exchanges, such as solving problems and planning action, reported greater satisfaction with their meetings <Cite k="kauffeld2012meetings" />. Satisfaction says something about the meeting experience. Whether Friday's move works still depends on the hours Facilities confirms.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if the vote is about a preference?

For a choice such as which snack to order, a preference poll may answer the question perfectly well. Ask first about facts that could rule out an option, such as an allergy, if they matter to the decision.

### What can I say if the chair has already called for votes?

Try, “Before we count, can I add something from Friday's shift?” State what you personally saw, then say what still needs checking. A detail others can verify gives the chair a reason to reopen the discussion.

### How can a team practice this?

Give three colleagues the same imaginary supplier quote and give one of them a note about the loading dock. Let a fourth colleague chair a short meeting without seeing the note. Afterward, ask which question brought the dock hours into the discussion, or what someone could have asked if the issue never came up.
