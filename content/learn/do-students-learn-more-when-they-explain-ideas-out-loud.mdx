Students can learn more when they explain **why an answer works**. In one experiment, preschoolers who explained a pattern were better able to solve new ones than children who said the correct answer aloud. All three groups spoke. Some repeated an answer, some explained it to themselves, and some explained it to their mothers, who could prompt them to keep going. Other studies have found benefits when students explain in writing or recall material without an audience <Cite k="rittlejohnson2008listening,lachner2021writingexplanations,koh2018retrievalteaching" />.

## What does explaining something to yourself add?

Imagine reading that a heart valve opens in one direction. You could repeat that sentence, or ask yourself what would happen to blood flow if the valve stayed open. The second response makes you connect the fact to a consequence. That is the kind of work researchers call *self-explanation*.

In a study of the circulatory system, 14 eighth graders received prompts to explain the material to themselves as they read. Another 10 read the same passage twice. The prompted group gained more from the pretest to the posttest. A larger review by Kiran Bisra and colleagues examined 64 research reports on self-explanation prompts. It found a positive average effect across activities that included reading texts, studying examples and solving problems. The review measured the effect of prompting students to make connections. It does not establish that they must speak aloud to benefit <Cite k="chi1994eliciting,bisra2018selfexplanation" />.

## Does it matter who listens?

Bethany Rittle-Johnson and colleagues gave 54 four- and five-year-olds patterns made from colored toy bugs. After each practice problem, the experimenter supplied the correct answer. One group repeated it, one explained the pattern to themselves, and one explained it to their mothers. The mothers moved into view. They could repeat the prompt if their child stayed silent for five seconds and offer general encouragement, without supplying the solution. On two familiar test problems, explaining helped, with no significant difference between the two explanation groups <Cite k="rittlejohnson2008listening" />.

The children who explained to their mothers did better on two problems with a new pattern structure. Using the researchers’ stricter scoring rule, which required both the bug and its color to be correct, the mother group averaged 64% correct and the self-explanation group averaged 39%. By our calculation, that is a 25 percentage-point difference on those two problems. Each percentage describes the group’s answers across those two items. Whether an audience helps in a college lecture or a different kind of task is a separate question <Cite k="rittlejohnson2008listening" />.

## What if nobody is there to teach?

Keiichi Kobayashi reviewed 28 studies of learning by preparing to teach. Students who studied expecting to teach generally learned more than those who studied without that expectation. The review also found a benefit when students prepared and then taught, compared with studying without expecting to teach. Most studies involved adults. Those two comparisons alone cannot tell a teacher exactly how much an extra minute of explanation will add <Cite k="kobayashi2019prepareteach" />.

One experiment gives a reason to put the notes down for a moment. A week after studying, learners who had taught from memory and learners who had retrieved the material without teaching both scored better on a comprehension test than learners who had taught with a script or done arithmetic instead. Remembering enough to reconstruct the idea may account for part of the benefit. This experiment leaves open what a listener’s questions might contribute in a live exchange <Cite k="koh2018retrievalteaching" />.

Writing can also work. In a laboratory experiment with 147 participants, Andreas Lachner and colleagues found no learning advantage for writing explanations over their comparison activities. In a second experiment with 50 participants, students who wrote explanations to themselves did better on problems requiring them to apply what they knew than when they wrote for an imagined student. An imagined reader, a mother sitting beside a child, and a classmate who can ask a question give a learner different tasks <Cite k="lachner2021writingexplanations,rittlejohnson2008listening" />.

## How could a class try this?

Suppose a class is learning why a larger sample does not necessarily fix a biased survey. Give students one example: 100 people leaving a concert are asked whether the town needs more live music. Have each student explain to a partner why interviewing 1,000 people at the same exit would still miss part of the town. Then give each student a new case to answer alone: a school surveys only the students who arrive early. The second answer gives the teacher something to check beyond how convincing the first explanation sounded. This is a proposed classroom exercise. It has yet to be tested in this form.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should students read their notes aloud?

If the goal is understanding, ask students why a step follows, or what would change if one condition were different. In the pattern experiment, repeating the answer aloud was less useful than explaining how to find it <Cite k="rittlejohnson2008listening" />.

### Does a student need a partner?

A student can explain a connection privately and then try a fresh problem. The eighth graders who explained the circulatory system to themselves gained more on the test than the group who reread the passage <Cite k="chi1994eliciting" />.

### How can a teacher check what the student learned?

Change a feature of the problem and ask the student to use the same idea independently. The pattern study illustrates why this matters: the two explanation groups performed similarly on familiar problems, but differently when the pattern changed <Cite k="rittlejohnson2008listening" />.
