Often, yes. In small groups, the person who talks the most is more likely than anyone else to be picked as the leader, a pattern one meta-analysis described as strong. In a recent study, speaking time still predicted leadership votes after the researchers accounted for intelligence, personality and gender. But in one study of problem-solving groups, airtime predicted who seemed influential, while expertise did more to determine whose input actually counted. For a quieter person, that means good contributions can go underrated. For a chair, it means the most talkative member may not be the one who knows the answer. Most of this evidence comes from groups studied in research settings rather than ongoing workplace meetings.

## How strong is the link between talking and leadership?

A meta-analysis combined studies of leadership emergence in small groups, meaning who comes to be seen as the leader when nobody is appointed. The member who participated most verbally tended to be chosen as the leader, an effect the authors called significant and strong. Its size varied with how rare high participators were in the group, with whether members or outside observers judged leadership, and with whether the group was a real one. The authors said the link reflects how noticeable the top talker is and the top talker's motivation, and partly an artifact of how studies were run. The review, published in 1989, draws on older studies <Cite k="mullen1989participation" />.

A second meta-analysis examined speaking time and dominance. Across studies, the link between the two was significant. It was stronger when observers inferred dominance from how much people spoke than when researchers checked whether people who were more dominant, by personality or by an assigned role, actually spoke more. The link also grew stronger as groups got larger <Cite k="schmidmast2002speakingtime" />.

A more recent study video-recorded 33 groups of students, with 4 to 10 members each, during a 10-minute planning session and 60 minutes of work on a computer simulation of either a military map task or a business problem. Members also voted on who had emerged as a leader. The correlation between a member's total speaking time and the votes they received was 0.67 <Cite k="maclaren2020babble" />.

## Is it the talking or what is said?

The idea that only the amount of talk matters, and not its quality, is known as the babble hypothesis. A complication is that the direction can run both ways. Group members seem to regulate one another's speaking time, so how much someone talks may partly reflect how the group already sees them. The 2020 study used a statistical method meant to address that problem. The authors concluded that speaking time still predicted who emerged as a leader after accounting for intelligence, personality and gender <Cite k="maclaren2020babble" />. The groups were made up of students working with a simulation, so the result may not hold for colleagues who already know each other.

Other experiments suggest the quality of what people say can matter more. In four studies, participants judged the leadership ability of four group members. When they read summaries of each member's contributions, or saw statements presented one after another like a discussion, quality influenced their judgments more than quantity did. When they read plain lists of the statements each person made, quantity mattered more <Cite k="jones2007quantityquality" />. These participants were reading about a group rather than taking part in one.

## Are the people who talk most the most competent?

Not necessarily. A study of 33 problem-solving groups included 64 middle-level managers and 93 graduate management students, who worked on an exercise called the moon problem while being audio-recorded. How influential a member seemed was predicted more strongly by airtime than by expertise. Actual influence, by contrast, depended more on expertise than on participation. The author concluded that group problem solving may be more rational than studies of perceived influence suggest <Cite k="bottger1984airtime" />.

Two studies of task groups looked at people high in the personality trait of dominance, who tend to gain influence in groups. Fellow members, outside observers and research staff rated them as more competent, even after the researchers accounted for their actual abilities. Counts of specific behaviors showed that more dominant people did more of the things that signal competence, and those behaviors went with higher competence ratings <Cite k="anderson2009dominance" />. Most group theories hold that assertiveness alone cannot win influence. These authors proposed instead that dominant people gain influence by acting in ways that make them seem competent, even when they are not. The study's summary does not list the behaviors, so it shows that behavior signaling competence matters, not that speaking time alone does.

## What does this mean for quieter people?

Airtime was spread unevenly in the simulation study. In all, 61.7% of participants spoke for no more than the average amount of time, and 26 did not speak at all. At least one member stayed silent in 19 of the 33 groups. By our calculation, every member had some recorded speaking time in the other 14 groups <Cite k="maclaren2020babble" />.

In groups like these, saying little may mean being overlooked for leadership. The moon problem study offers some reassurance, since expertise still carried weight in actual influence even when airtime shaped impressions <Cite k="bottger1984airtime" />. None of these studies tested whether a quiet person who starts talking more is then seen differently by colleagues who already know them. Several involved short sessions in newly formed groups.

## What can a meeting chair do?

In two studies with 699 people working in groups of two to five, researchers measured how well each group performed across a range of tasks. In a subset of groups whose members wore sensors that counted speaking turns, groups where a few people dominated the conversation scored lower on the researchers' measure of collective intelligence, a general factor in how well a group performs across tasks (r = -0.41). When turn-taking was analyzed together with members' social sensitivity and the share of women in the group, all three had similar predictive power, but only social sensitivity reached statistical significance <Cite k="woolley2010collective" />. The data show an association. They do not show that evening out turns makes a group perform better.

Making airtime visible did not help in one experiment. In a small experiment, researchers randomly assigned university students to conditions for a 20-minute online brainstorming task, and the final sample was 81 students in 23 teams of three or four. Some teams saw a live display of each member's speaking time, and some were told at the start to participate equally. Teams that saw the display listed an average of 6.96 ideas, compared with 12.13 for teams that did not. The instruction to participate equally made no significant difference. The authors suggest that watching their own airtime may have distracted people from generating ideas <Cite k="hong2026participationfeedback" />.

Neither study tested what happens when a chair invites a particular person to speak. Our guide to [what to ask before a meeting vote](/learn/what-to-ask-before-a-meeting-vote) covers how to draw out facts that only one person holds.

## Try this at your next meeting

If you tend to stay quiet, write down one point you know well before the meeting, such as a figure you track or a problem you have seen firsthand, and note which decision it bears on. Make that point, and say where it comes from. Afterward, notice whether anyone referred back to it.

If you chair, keep a private tally of who has spoken. Because the live display in the brainstorming study went with fewer ideas, we suggest not showing the tally to the group. Before the group decides, ask anyone who has not spoken a direct question about what they know. When you choose who will lead the follow-up work, list the contributions that changed the discussion and who made them, rather than relying on who spoke most. The studies above did not test either routine. Both draw on findings that airtime shapes how influential people seem, and on one experiment in which a visible speaking-time display backfired.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the babble hypothesis?

It is the idea that how much someone talks, regardless of what they say, determines whether a group sees them as a leader. Studies generally find a strong link between speaking time and leadership ratings, though its size varies with the group and with who is judging. Experiments on whether quality also matters have produced mixed results.

### Should I talk more in meetings to be seen as a leader?

In studies of small, newly formed groups, members who spoke more received more leadership votes. Those studies mostly involved students in short sessions. In a study that included managers, expertise still mattered more than airtime for actual influence.

### Does the person who talks the most usually know the most?

Not necessarily. In one study, airtime predicted how influential members seemed more strongly than expertise did. In another, more dominant people were rated as more competent even after their actual abilities were taken into account.
