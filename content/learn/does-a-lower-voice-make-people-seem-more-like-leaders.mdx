Sometimes. When people listened to the same appeal twice and picked a leader, lowering the pitch of the recording could win the speaker more votes. Other experiments found that policy proposals mattered and that listeners did not always favor a lower voice. And a preference for a voice tells us little about what the speaker will accomplish in office <Cite k="klofstad2012winner,cinar2023policy,bowern2023japan,klofstad2018ability" />.

## What did listeners hear?

In an early US experiment, researchers took a short request for votes and made higher- and lower-pitched recordings of each speaker. Listeners chose between versions of the same person saying the same words. Both male and female listeners tended to pick the lower voice for male and female speakers. Ordinarily, when two candidates sound different, they also say different things. Playing two versions of one recording allowed researchers to isolate pitch <Cite k="klofstad2012winner" />.

Asli Ceren Cinar and Özgür Kıbrıs added a choice about public spending. When candidates offered the same policy, the lower voice received 69.12% of choices between men and 59.63% of choices between women. By our calculation, the gap was 9.49 percentage points, matching the researchers’ reported figure. Nobody in that comparison chose between a man and a woman. When the researchers changed the spending proposals, the vote shares changed too <Cite k="cinar2023policy" />.

## Did another experiment find the same preference?

In Japan, 414 respondents took part in a different test. Each heard one version of a female political candidate's speech. The researchers varied both the pitch and how often the candidate was said to have won election. Across respondents, they found no consistent advantage for lowering her pitch. The study also lacked the statistical power to establish whether men and women responded differently to pitch <Cite k="bowern2023japan" />.

The studies asked different things of listeners. Hearing one candidate and deciding whether you would vote for her gives you no second voice for comparison. The experiments also differed in language, participants and material. The results cannot identify which difference accounts for the change in preferences <Cite k="klofstad2012winner,bowern2023japan" />.

## What happens in an actual election?

Casey Klofstad compared recordings of candidates who ran for the US House in 2012 with how those candidates fared. The association varied with whom they ran against. Unlike an experiment, an election gives voters a party label, a record and an opponent along with a voice. The comparisons cannot tell us whether changing a particular candidate’s pitch would have changed the outcome <Cite k="klofstad2016elections" />.

Klofstad and Rindy Anderson later studied elected officials and also tested whether listeners were persuaded by policy statements. They found no evidence in those tests that speakers with lower voices were better leaders. A person can sound authoritative on a recording without giving a listener evidence about how they would run a meeting or carry out a plan <Cite k="klofstad2018ability" />.

## How could I practice evaluating the message?

Pick a short proposal from a meeting you have permission to record. Read a transcript first. Write down what the speaker wants to do and which detail would help you decide. Then listen. If your assessment changes, name the word, the sound or the piece of reasoning that changed it. This is an untested listening exercise.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a deeper voice always get more votes?

No. The US paired-recording study and the Japanese single-speech study produced different results. Each tested a particular set of speakers, listeners and voting questions <Cite k="klofstad2012winner,bowern2023japan" />.

### Did researchers ask men and women to compete against one another?

In the public-spending experiment, listeners compared two men or two women. The 9.49-point figure compares those two groups of listeners. It does not describe a mixed-gender race <Cite k="cinar2023policy" />.

### Should I lower my voice for a leadership interview?

These studies tested judgments about political speakers. They give no estimate of how changing your pitch would affect an interviewer’s decision or how you would perform in the job <Cite k="klofstad2012winner,klofstad2018ability" />.
