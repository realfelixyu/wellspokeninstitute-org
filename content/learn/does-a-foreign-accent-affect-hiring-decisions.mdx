In experiments, often yes, though the size of the effect depends on the accent, the job and the setting. A meta-analysis of hiring experiments found that candidates with standard accents were rated more hireable than candidates with nonstandard accents, by about half a standard deviation. A field experiment with real employers in Germany found that callers with a Turkish accent were somewhat less likely to be told a job was still open. Not every accent was penalized in every study, and research on whether accented speech simply sounds less believable has produced mixed results, including a failed direct replication and two studies that found no effect. Most of the studies described here asked listeners to rate recordings, so it shows how judgments shift rather than how often accented candidates lose real job offers.

## How large is the accent gap in hiring experiments?

A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 139 effect sizes, covering 4,576 participants in total, from studies comparing how candidates with standard and nonstandard accents were judged for jobs. Candidates with a standard accent were rated more hireable than candidates with a nonstandard accent, by a standardized difference of 0.47. The gap was larger for jobs with high communication demands. It was also larger for foreign accents than for regional ones and for female candidates than for male candidates. The size of the gap tracked how listeners perceived the candidates' social status <Cite k="spence2022accenthiring" />.

One finding is hard to square with the idea that the penalty is only about being understood. Across studies, how comprehensible a candidate was compared with the other candidate did not significantly change the size of the gap. The authors write that this result and the status findings are difficult to explain if the bias were only a practical response to communication concerns <Cite k="spence2022accenthiring" />. A meta-analysis cannot show what any single interviewer was thinking, and the pooled studies used different accents, jobs and listeners.

## Does every accent get the same treatment?

No. In one experiment, 286 college students at two locations judged applicants with Standard American English, French or Japanese accents for four jobs that differed in status and communication demands. Japanese-accented applicants fared worse than French-accented ones on employment decisions, especially for jobs with high communication demands, even after accounting for how understandable the applicants were. French-accented applicants were viewed as favorably as Standard American English speakers or more so. Each accent came with a different applicant name, so the study cannot separate the effect of the accent from the effect of the name <Cite k="hosoda2010accent" />.

A second study had US participants listen to an interview for a human resource manager job, recorded with a Midwestern US, French or Colombian accent. The Midwestern applicant was rated more positively than the French-accented one. Ratings of the Colombian-accented applicant did not differ significantly from either. In that study, the gap for the French accent ran through how similar listeners felt the applicant was to themselves <Cite k="deprezsims2010accent" />. Our guide to [accent and intelligibility](/learn/accent-vs-intelligibility) explains why how strong an accent sounds and how easy it is to understand are separate questions.

## Does accent bias show up with real employers?

A field experiment in Germany between October 2014 and October 2015 tested the first step of many job searches: a phone call asking whether an advertised position is still open. Trained testers, almost all actors or acting students, could speak German both with a Turkish accent and with standard pronunciation. The same tester called each employer up to three times, varying the name given (Turkish or German) and the accent. Across 1,804 calls to 702 employers, callers with a German name and standard German were told the job was still available 79.83% of the time, and callers with a Turkish name and standard German 79.51%. Callers with a Turkish name and a Turkish accent heard that 72.85% of the time, a statistically significant gap of about 7 percentage points <Cite k="schmaus2021accentfield" />.

Accented callers were more often told the position had already been filled. The gap was not larger for jobs whose advertisements stressed communication skills, and the authors concluded that the difference did not appear to stem from how well employers understood accented callers. In their models, the gap was smaller at larger firms, at external recruitment agencies and at firms that mentioned diversity strategies. The authors suggest that more standardized hiring leaves less room for bias. The study measured replies to a first phone call, not job offers, and covered one accent in one country <Cite k="schmaus2021accentfield" />.

## Do accents make statements sound less true?

A widely cited 2010 study asked 28 native English speakers to rate how true trivia statements such as "Ants don't sleep" were on a 14 cm line. Participants were told that the speakers were only reading statements the experimenter wrote. Statements from speakers with a mild or heavy foreign accent were rated less true (6.95 and 6.84) than the same kinds of statements from native speakers (7.59). In a second experiment, participants who were told the study concerned how hard speakers were to understand rated mildly accented statements as true as native ones, but still rated heavily accented statements lower <Cite k="levarikeysar2010" />.

Later attempts to find this effect have been mixed. A study in Switzerland asked 194 French-speaking and 183 Swiss-German-speaking participants to judge 48 trivia statements recorded with French, Swiss-German, Italian and English accents. Foreign accent did not appear to affect credibility ratings, even though participants did hold different stereotypes about the language groups <Cite k="stocker2017credibility" />. A 2024 study in Italy randomly assigned 60 participants to hear statements from 20 native Italian speakers or 20 speakers with strong foreign accents from 16 countries. Statements heard a second time were rated truer in both groups by about the same amount, and new statements were rated about the same (3.58 versus 3.50 on a 6-point scale). A separate group of listeners rated the foreign-accented speakers lower on traits such as competence and success, but adding those ratings did not improve the statistical model of truth judgments <Cite k="lorenzoni2024illusorytruth" />. The 2024 authors also describe an earlier direct replication attempt that did not reproduce the original result. A 2021 study described below did find accented trivia statements rated less true <Cite k="boduchgrabka2021exposure" />. These studies used trivia statements, not interviews.

## Can listeners' bias be reduced?

One study suggests that brief exposure to an accent can shrink, but not remove, a credibility gap. Researchers randomly assigned 220 participants to hear eight short stories told in English by Polish-accented speakers or by speakers of British English, about 10 minutes in all. Everyone then rated trivia statements recorded by both groups on a 100-point scale. Among people who first heard British speakers, British-accented statements were rated 22.26 points truer than Polish-accented ones. Exposure to Polish speakers reduced that gap by 7.71 points. By our calculation, the exposed group was left with a model-estimated gap of about 14.55 points. Better transcription of Polish-accented sentences accounted for most of the effect of exposure. The authors call the reduction numerically small and note that mostly the same speakers appeared in both parts of the study <Cite k="boduchgrabka2021exposure" />.

## What can employers and candidates do?

For employers, the authors of the three-accent study with 286 students suggest structured interviews, training interviewers about possible bias against accented applicants and gathering more individual information about each applicant to reduce reliance on stereotypes <Cite k="hosoda2010accent" />. Our guide to [structured job interviews](/learn/what-is-a-structured-job-interview) describes what that format involves. The German field study points the same way: the accent gap was smaller where hiring tends to follow set procedures <Cite k="schmaus2021accentfield" />. Neither study tested these steps as an intervention, so they are informed suggestions rather than proven fixes. This guide describes research and is not legal advice about discrimination.

For candidates, the research does not offer a tested remedy, and the meta-analysis suggests the gap is not only about being understood <Cite k="spence2022accenthiring" />. What candidates can control is how easy their answers are to follow, which matters to any listener. Our guides on [whether pronunciation teaching helps adults be understood](/learn/does-pronunciation-teaching-help-adults-be-understood) and [whether feeling nervous hurts an interview](/learn/does-feeling-nervous-hurt-a-job-interview) cover the evidence on those skills.

## Practice making key details easy to catch

The studies above did not test this routine. It is a way to reduce the chance that a listener misses details, which you can control, rather than a way to change a listener's attitudes, which you cannot. List the five details an interviewer most needs to catch, such as your job title, a number from a result and a tool or method you used. Record a two-minute answer to "Tell me about yourself" and play it to a friend who has not seen your list. Ask them to write down every detail they caught. For any detail they missed, try a pause before it, a slower pace on the key word or a short restatement ("that's about 40 orders a day"), then record again and compare.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do employers rate candidates with foreign accents lower?

On average, in experiments, yes. A meta-analysis found a gap of about half a standard deviation favoring standard accents, larger for jobs with heavy communication demands. Results varied by accent, and some accents were rated as favorably as standard speech.

### Is accent bias just about being hard to understand?

Probably not entirely. In the meta-analysis, how comprehensible candidates were did not significantly change the size of the gap, and in the German field study, the gap did not grow for jobs that stressed communication.

### Do people believe things less when said in a foreign accent?

The evidence is mixed. One early study found accented trivia statements were rated less true, and a later study replicated that pattern, but studies in Switzerland and Italy found no difference.
