Public speaking is the most common social fear in large U.S. surveys, but it is not most people's biggest fear, and it is not more feared than death. In the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, a nationally representative study of adults, 21.2% reported a lifetime fear of public speaking or performing, more than for any of the other 13 social situations the survey asked about <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears" />. That is roughly 1 in 5 people. The often-repeated claims that 75% of people fear public speaking, or that they fear it more than death, do not hold up when you trace them back.

## Where the "more than death" idea came from

The claim has a specific origin. In 1973, R. H. Bruskin Associates ran a survey of American fears that was reported in the London Sunday Times and then repeated in communication textbooks as proof that people fear public speaking more than death <Cite k="dwyerdavidson2012" />.

In 2012, communication researchers Karen Kangas Dwyer and Marlina Davidson went back to that study and repeated its design with 815 college students. Students picked their fears from a list and then ranked their top fears. Public speaking was selected as a fear more often than anything else on the list, including death. But when students were asked to choose their single top fear, death came first <Cite k="dwyerdavidson2012" />.

The difference matters. A checklist shows how many people have some fear of a situation. It does not show which fear is strongest. Many people feel nervous before a speech, and far fewer would trade that nervousness for dying.

Current national data point the same way. In the 2026 Chapman University Survey of American Fears, 28.9% of Americans said they were afraid or very afraid of public speaking, which ranked 51st among the fears tested. In the same survey, 30.3% said they were afraid of dying <Cite k="chapmanfears2026" />. Fears about corruption, war, and the health of loved ones ranked far higher than either.

## What national surveys actually find

The strongest evidence on how many people fear public speaking comes from epidemiological surveys that interview nationally representative samples of households using structured diagnostic questions. The National Comorbidity Survey Replication, fielded from 2001 to 2003, is the main U.S. example.

It found that 21.2% of adults had a lifetime fear of public speaking or performing, and 19.5% had a fear of speaking up in a meeting or class. Nearly one-fourth (24.1%) had at least one lifetime social fear of any kind <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears" />. Public speaking topped the list, so the narrow version of the popular claim is right: it is the most common fear of a social situation.

The estimated gap between those two fears was 1.7 percentage points. Respondents could name both. The ranking alone cannot establish that the population rates differ. <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears" />.

Smaller studies that ask about milder nervousness find higher numbers. In a random telephone survey of 499 adults in Winnipeg, one third said they felt excessive anxiety when speaking to a large audience <Cite k="stein1996publicspeaking" />. That is a reasonable upper estimate for "fear of public speaking" in the general population. We could not find a survey or government report that measured 75%.

## Fear, anxiety, and disorder are different things

Part of the confusion comes from mixing three things that researchers measure separately.

The first is ordinary nervousness before speaking. Many people feel some, and it is not what the surveys below count as a fear.

The second is a fear of public speaking. This is what the 21% figure measures: a self-reported fear strong enough that people recognize it as a problem at some point in their lives.

The third is social anxiety disorder, a clinical diagnosis. The National Institute of Mental Health estimates that 7.1% of U.S. adults had social anxiety disorder in the past year and 12.1% will have it at some point in their lives <Cite k="nimhsad" />. The earlier National Comorbidity Survey, from the early 1990s, put lifetime prevalence at 13.3% <Cite k="magee1996phobias" />.

Fear of public speaking overlaps with social anxiety disorder but is not the same thing. A review of 18 studies found that public speaking anxiety works as a distinct subtype of social phobia, different from other subtypes in both kind and degree <Cite k="blote2009psa" />. Many people fear speeches without fearing parties, phone calls, or meeting strangers.

## Why the fear is so common

Speaking in front of a group combines several things people find uncomfortable: being watched, being evaluated, and having to think in real time. The Winnipeg survey asked people with public-speaking fears what they worried would happen. The most common answers were trembling or showing other signs of anxiety (80%), their mind going blank (74%), and doing or saying something embarrassing (64%) <Cite k="stein1996publicspeaking" />.

The fear also starts young. In the same survey, half of the people with public-speaking fears said it began by age 13, and 90% by age 20 <Cite k="stein1996publicspeaking" />. That timing means the fear usually takes shape before people start their careers, long before the presentations that make it costly.

The impact is real for a minority. One in 10 respondents in the Winnipeg study said public-speaking anxiety had markedly interfered with their work, social life, or education, or caused them marked distress <Cite k="stein1996publicspeaking" />.

## What helps

The encouraging part of this research is how well the fear responds to treatment. A 2019 meta-analysis pooled 30 randomized controlled trials with 1,355 participants and found an average effect of g = 0.74 for psychological interventions targeting fear of public speaking, a moderate to large improvement <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />. Most of the treatments tested were cognitive or behavioral approaches, which typically pair questioning worst-case predictions with repeated, gradual practice.

Treatment delivered online or with other technology worked about as well as face-to-face treatment in that meta-analysis <Cite k="ebrahimi2019fops" />. An earlier review also found support for virtual reality exposure and Internet-based self-help programs <Cite k="pull2012psa" />.

For everyday nervousness, the same principle applies on a smaller scale: speak out loud, often, in front of slowly larger audiences. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the number one fear in America?

In the 2026 Chapman University Survey of American Fears, the most common fear was corrupt government officials, reported by about 61% of Americans <Cite k="chapmanfears2026" />. Public speaking ranked 51st.

### Is fear of public speaking a phobia?

It can be. When the fear is intense, lasting, and interferes with daily life, it may be part of social anxiety disorder, which affects about 7.1% of U.S. adults in a given year <Cite k="nimhsad" />. Most people who dislike public speaking do not meet that threshold.

### Where does the 75% statistic come from?

We could not find any survey or study that reports it. National data show about 21% of U.S. adults with a lifetime fear of public speaking, and smaller surveys that count milder anxiety reach about one third <Cite k="ruscio2008socialfears,stein1996publicspeaking" />.
