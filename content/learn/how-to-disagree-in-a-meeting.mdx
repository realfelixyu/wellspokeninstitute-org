Name the part of your colleague's argument that matters to them. Give them a chance to correct you before you say where you disagree. Suppose they want to post next week's shifts on Thursday. “You'd like people to have more time to trade shifts. Have I got that right?” gives them something they can confirm or correct. “I hear you” leaves them guessing what you heard.

## Why can disagreement feel like poor listening?

In one experiment, 116 undergraduates discussed a campus issue on a video call. Actors nodded and gave brief responses, and only after the call did students see a form saying whether the listener agreed with them. Researchers had prepared that feedback in advance. Students rated one statement about whether the person was an attentive listener. On that five-point item, the average was 4.32 for listeners described as agreeing and 4.00 for those described as disagreeing. That **0.32-point** gap was in the speakers' impressions of listeners trained to behave similarly <Cite k="ren2024disagreement" />.

One way to make your own listening easier to judge is to be specific about where your views part company. “I’d keep Friday as the posting day because availability forms come in Thursday night” gives your colleague a reason they can answer.

## What if my colleague says I missed their point?

Let them finish the correction. At the scheduling meeting, they might say, “People can find someone to trade with. The trouble is there's no manager on Saturday to approve the swap.” That detail changes the discussion. You could reply, “So it's getting approval before the weekend. I'd keep Friday for the final schedule. Could we have someone available to approve swaps on Saturday?”

Researchers studying conversational receptiveness taught people writing about contentious issues to do things such as acknowledge an opposing argument and qualify a claim. Other people who read their replies rated the writers as more desirable collaborators and their messages as more persuasive. Those were ratings of written messages. A separate study asked readers to imagine conflicts and judge which opening lines would put the recipient on the defensive. They expected less defensiveness when the speaker used first-person language and included both people's perspectives <Cite k="yeomans2020receptiveness,rogers2018conflictperspective" />.

## Rehearse the disagreement

Ask a coworker to argue for posting the schedule on Thursday. Let them pick a reason and keep it to themselves until the conversation starts. After they explain it, tell them which problem you think the earlier schedule would solve. Pause long enough for a correction. Then give your preferred posting day and your reason.

Afterward, ask, “Which words told you I understood your concern?” Then ask where they think you still disagree. If they can describe your position but you missed theirs, go back to the moment just before you gave your reason and try that part again.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if I agree with the problem they have identified?

Say exactly what you share. “I agree that weekend swaps need approving. I still think Friday is the right day to post the schedule.” The chair can now ask who could approve a swap on Saturday while the posting day is still under discussion.

### Will listening change someone's mind?

It depends on what you measure. In a live experiment about universal basic income, speakers who experienced higher-quality listening took less extreme positions after the discussion. The change in overall support for the policy did not differ reliably between listening conditions <Cite k="itzchakov2024listeningdisagreement" />.

In another experiment, 1,485 people completed a survey just after a video call about in-state college tuition for unauthorized immigrants. People who heard a canvasser’s story and received high-quality listening felt better understood than those who heard the story alone. The researchers found no added persuasive effect from listening, either then or at the five-week follow-up <Cite k="santoro2025listenforchange" />.
