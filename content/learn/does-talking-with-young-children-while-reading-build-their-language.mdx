Probably somewhat, but less than early studies suggested. Dialogic reading, in which an adult asks questions and invites the child to talk about the book, produced moderate vocabulary gains in studies with younger children. The 2008 review found smaller effects for 4- to 5-year-olds and at-risk children; a 2019 review found no difference by socioeconomic status, near-zero effects when the comparison group did another activity and little evidence that gains last. Separately, children who have more back-and-forth conversation with adults tend to have stronger language. That link comes mostly from observational studies, and an MRI substudy from one randomized trial that increased conversation did not raise language scores more than in a control group. Reading and talking together is reasonable, but the size of the payoff is uncertain.

## What is dialogic reading?

In ordinary shared reading, an adult reads a book aloud while the child listens. Dialogic reading turns the session into a conversation: the adult asks questions, prompts the child to describe the pictures and responds to what the child says, so the child talks more and the adult talks less. A 2008 meta-analysis described it as an interactive format that emphasizes active rather than passive participation by the child <Cite k="mol2008dialogic" />. A broader 2019 review describes it as a specific set of techniques used to scaffold a conversation between the adult and the child <Cite k="noble2019sharedreading" />.

## What did the first reviews find?

The 2008 meta-analysis compared dialogic reading with typical shared reading in 16 studies. For expressive vocabulary, meaning the words children could produce, the combined effect was d = 0.59, which the authors call moderate. That estimate came from 9 studies with 322 children. The effect shrank substantially when children were 4 to 5 years old or at risk for language and literacy problems <Cite k="mol2008dialogic" />.

A 2009 meta-analysis by the same group looked at programs in early education that trained educators to involve children before, during and after reading. Across 31 experiments and quasi-experiments with 2,049 children, it found a moderate effect on oral language. It also found that results obtained when researchers did the reading were not replicated when children's own teachers did it in an ordinary classroom <Cite k="mol2009interactive" />. That gap matters for anyone hoping to scale up a program.

## Did later, stricter reviews agree?

Not fully. A 2019 meta-analysis covered 54 studies published between 1989 and 2017, with 316 effect sizes and 5,569 children. It included only studies of typically developing children aged 7 or younger that had a control group. After removing outliers, the average effect of shared reading on language was small, g = 0.194, and corrections for possible publication bias suggested the true effect might be smaller still <Cite k="noble2019sharedreading" />.

Comparison-group type and baseline differences together explained most of the variation. When children who read were compared with children who simply continued as usual, the effect was g = 0.263. When the comparison group took part in a different activity, the effect was near zero, g = 0.028, though only 12 studies used that design. Whether a program used dialogic techniques made no significant difference, and in the 9 studies that tested children again later, the effect was not statistically significant. The authors conclude that the benefits found so far may come from the general experience of being in a program rather than from book reading itself, and that they may be temporary. They also say it would be premature to dismiss shared reading, partly because the most common programs lasted only 6 to 8 weeks, which may be too short to produce lasting effects <Cite k="noble2019sharedreading" />.

## Does training parents help?

A 2020 meta-analysis included only randomized controlled trials of programs that train caregivers to share picture books with children aged 1 to 6. Across 19 trials with 2,594 caregiver and child pairs, the programs had a small effect on children's expressive language (d = 0.41) and receptive language, meaning the words they understand (d = 0.26). The effect on how well caregivers shared books was large (d = 1.01) <Cite k="dowdall2020booksharing" />.

Program length made a difference. The authors split programs into those with less than 60 minutes of total training and those with more. The brief programs had no significant effect on children's language. Longer programs tended to do better on expressive language (d = 0.54), though the difference between the two groups fell just short of statistical significance. Few trials tested children again after the program ended. The one that followed children for more than a year found no long-term effect, but it was the briefest program in the review, so the authors caution against reading much into it <Cite k="dowdall2020booksharing" />.

## Is back-and-forth conversation what matters?

Researchers measure conversational turns with small recorders that children wear for a whole day. Software counts a turn when an adult speaks and the child responds, or the reverse, with no more than a 5-second gap between them <Cite k="romeo2018conversational" />.

In one study, 146 children aged 2 to 36 months were recorded once a month for 6 months and tested again at 9 to 14 years of age. Turn counts between 18 and 24 months accounted for 14% to 27% of the differences in IQ, verbal comprehension and vocabulary scores about a decade later, after accounting for family socioeconomic status. The authors note that the results are correlational and do not establish cause. Only 10 children came from the lowest socioeconomic group, and all spoke only English. The study was funded by the LENA Research Foundation, which developed the recording software, and three authors worked there <Cite k="gilkerson2018turns" />.

A study of 122 children followed from 9 to 24 months found that conversational turns and vocabulary growth were related in both directions, even after accounting for how many adult words children heard. Children's vocabulary was measured by parent report <Cite k="donnelly2021turntaking" />. The direction matters: children who already talk more may draw more conversation from adults, not only the other way around.

A brain-imaging study recorded the home language of 36 children aged 4 to 6. Children who had more conversational turns with adults showed more activity in Broca's area, a left-hemisphere region involved in language, while listening to stories. That held after accounting for socioeconomic status, IQ and the amount of adult speech alone. The study excluded children with language disorders or low language scores, and the recorders counted talk without capturing what was said <Cite k="romeo2018conversational" />.

## Can a program increase conversation, and does that help?

In an MRI substudy of 52 children from a randomized trial of 107, researchers tested that question. The children were aged 4 to 6 and mostly from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, and their families had been randomly assigned to a 9-week family program or to a control group with no contact. The program met weekly in small groups for two hours and covered stress, discipline, responsiveness and language use, along with a component for children <Cite k="romeo2021turntaking" />.

Among families with usable recordings, those who attended gained 4.24 conversational turns per hour on average, while control families lost 7.01. By our calculation, the groups' changes differed by 11.25 turns per hour, partly because control families declined. Yet children's language scores rose about equally in both groups, so the program did not produce a measurable language advantage. Across both groups combined, children whose turn counts rose more tended to show larger gains in language scores. The authors note that their strongest results were indirect, which limits causal conclusions, and that the program changed several things besides conversation <Cite k="romeo2021turntaking" />.

## Try it at home

This routine borrows from the dialogic reading and conversation research above. None of the studies tested this exact routine.

Choose a picture book your child likes and plan to read it more than once. On each page, pause and ask a question your child can answer in their own words, such as what is happening or what a character might do next. When your child answers, repeat what they said and add a word or two. Let your child turn pages and point things out, and follow their interest even if you skip text. Keep it short and stop while it is still fun. Outside reading time, treat your child's comments as openings and reply with a question or comment of your own, so that the talk goes back and forth several times. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is dialogic reading better than just reading aloud?

In studies with younger children, it produced moderate gains in the words children could say compared with ordinary reading. A later review of 54 studies found that whether a program used dialogic techniques made no significant difference, and effects were small overall.

### At what age does shared reading help most?

The evidence is mixed. In one meta-analysis, effects of dialogic reading were substantially smaller for 4- and 5-year-olds than for younger children. A review of parent training trials found no clear difference by age between 1 and 6 years.

### How many conversational turns does a child need?

No study has set a target. Turn counts are linked to later language skills, but mostly in observational studies, and in an MRI substudy from one randomized trial, raising turns did not raise language scores more than in a control group.
