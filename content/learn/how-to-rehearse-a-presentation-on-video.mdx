Pick the part of your talk that gives you trouble and record yourself giving just that part. Before you press play, write down what a classmate should be able to explain afterward. Watch for the moment you give them enough information to do it. If you never quite say the thing you want them to remember, you have a sentence to work on.

Suppose you're showing a graph with two lines. On the recording you hear yourself say, “This one goes up much faster.” Look at the screen at that moment. Can a person in the back row tell which line you mean? Try the passage again with the label spoken aloud. “After the fare change, weekend ridership rose faster than weekday ridership.” Play both versions to a classmate and ask which comparison they heard.

## What does watching a rehearsal change?

In one classroom study, students gave two recorded slide presentations. One section had to review the first talk using a scoring sheet. In the other section, students could watch the first recording if they chose. Everyone received comments from classmates and the instructor. On the second talk, the section assigned the scoring sheet earned higher marks for explaining the material, timing and speech skills. The comparison was between two course sections, both with access to recordings <Cite k="ritchie2016rehearsal" />.

Borrow one item from your own assignment sheet. If it asks you to explain evidence, start the replay when your graph appears and stop it when you've said what the numbers mean. Write down the words you used. Seeing the slide for a full minute and spending a full minute explaining it can feel like the same thing until you listen back.

## What should I ask a classmate?

Give them a question they can answer from what they heard. For the graph, try “Which line rose faster, and when?” If they picked the other line, ask which words pointed them there. Put the timestamp next to their answer so you can find that spot on your next run.

After 95 undergraduate presentations, a researcher scored the feedback students received. On a five-point scale for specificity, teachers averaged 3.85, peers guided by a tutor averaged 3.44, and peers working on their own averaged 2.82. The **0.41-point** gap between guided peers and teachers was statistically inconclusive. The scores describe how closely the comments addressed a particular part of the talk <Cite k="vanginkel2017specificity" />.

Save questions about grading for the instructor. In a related study of 144 first-year undergraduates, students who received teacher feedback made the strongest gains in how they performed presentations. The researchers assessed performance separately from students’ knowledge and attitudes about presenting <Cite k="vanginkel2015feedback" />.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I grade my own talk from the recording?

Use the recording to check something you can hear or see, such as whether you named the two groups in your graph. Give an instructor a say in how well you did. In a pharmacy course, first-year students rated the same simulated counseling encounter before and after they watched it. Nearly two-thirds raised their own rating after the replay. They were watching the encounter they had already completed <Cite k="mort2010videoreview" />.

### What if we're sharing a presentation?

Record the handoff together with everyone's agreement. Listen to the last sentence of your section and the first one from your partner. If you both introduce the graph from scratch, decide who will name it and who will explain it. In an Australian first-year business course, each group could review its recorded presentation in a restricted course space. The study asked students about their experience reviewing their group's work <Cite k="barry2012groupvideo" />.

### Do I have to record the whole talk again?

For a wording change, repeat just the passage and the sentence that follows it. If your assignment has a time limit, then run the whole talk with your slides, including the handoff and the time you spend pointing things out.
